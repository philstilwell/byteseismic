Prompt 1: Economics has been called the “dismal science”. Explain the context in which this was said, and comment on the notion.

Economics has been called the “dismal science”. Explain the context in which this was said, and comment on the notion.

ChatGPT response · editorial edition

Thomas Carlyle used “dismal science” in his 1849 essay Occasional Discourse on the Negro Question. His racist argument attacked emancipation in the West Indies and defended coercing formerly enslaved people into plantation labor. He objected to leaving labor relations to free choice and supply and demand. John Stuart Mill answered him in 1850; Mill’s reply should not be described as the target of an earlier essay. The dispute involved human freedom and racial hierarchy, not merely economists’ use of statistics or a shared calculation that slavery was inefficient.

The notion of economics as a “dismal science” has persisted in popular discourse, but its interpretation has evolved over time. Today, it is often invoked to describe the perception that economics focuses on pessimistic predictions or the negative aspects of economic policy and outcomes, such as poverty, inequality, and environmental degradation. However, this view oversimplifies the breadth and depth of the field, which encompasses a wide range of studies on how societies allocate scarce resources to meet the diverse needs and wants of their populations.

Economics studies production, exchange, distribution, and choices under constraints. Its descriptive claims can be tested, while recommendations also depend on values and whose welfare counts. Methods and research quality vary; the discipline’s usefulness does not require portraying every economic study as rigorous or benevolent.

Thus, while the term “dismal science” captures a historical critique rooted in a specific ideological stance, it does not accurately reflect the comprehensive and dynamic nature of economics as a field of study. Instead, economics provides critical tools and frameworks for understanding and addressing the multifaceted challenges of the modern world.

Gemini response · editorial edition

The term “dismal science” to describe economics was coined by Scottish writer Thomas Carlyle in 1849. Here’s a breakdown of the context and the idea itself:

Context:

  • Carlyle contrasted political economy with the “gay science” of troubadour poetry. The relevant context was his opposition to emancipation and voluntary labor, not a neutral complaint that economic findings were sad.
  • Malthus’s population argument is often associated with later pessimistic uses of the label, but it is not the context in which Carlyle coined it in this essay. Nor did Malthus claim actual population must always exceed food: preventive and positive checks were central to his argument.

The Notion:

  • The term is somewhat derogatory, implying economics deals with depressing realities.
  • There’s some truth to this. Economics often grapples with issues like scarcity, poverty, and inequality.
  • However, economics also studies ways to improve resource allocation, growth, and overall well-being.
  • So, while it acknowledges challenges, it also seeks solutions.

Modern uses of “dismal science” can refer to scarcity or disappointing forecasts, but those meanings should be distinguished from the phrase’s pro-coercion historical origin.

Prompt 2: Some modern pundits have called economics dismal due to its unwieldy nature in respect to scientific inquiry and measurement. Please provide insight on this notion.

Some modern pundits have called economics dismal due to its unwieldy nature in respect to scientific inquiry and measurement. Please provide insight on this notion.

ChatGPT response · editorial edition

Economic inquiry faces difficult measurement and causal questions because policies, institutions, and expectations interact. This is a difference of degree and setting, not a clean division between experimental natural science and nonexperimental economics: natural sciences also study complex systems, and economists use laboratory, field, and natural experiments.

  1. Complexity and Causality: One major challenge in economics is the difficulty of establishing clear cause-and-effect relationships. The multifaceted nature of economic phenomena means that outcomes are often the result of interrelated factors, making it hard to isolate the impact of individual variables. This complexity is compounded by the fact that economic actors (individuals, firms, governments) behave in ways that are influenced by expectations, information asymmetries, and strategic interactions, which can lead to outcomes that are unpredictable or counterintuitive.
  2. Measurement and Data Limitations: Economic research relies heavily on the availability and quality of data. However, accurate data collection can be hampered by limitations in resources, methodologies, and accessibility. Furthermore, the need to make simplifying assumptions to develop economic models can lead to discrepancies between theoretical predictions and real-world observations. The challenge of accurately measuring concepts such as utility, welfare, or the informal economy adds another layer of complexity to economic inquiry.
  3. Methodological Diversity and Disputes: The field of economics encompasses a wide range of methodological approaches, from highly mathematical models to qualitative analyses. This diversity, while a strength, can also lead to debates over the appropriateness and validity of different methods, further complicating the pursuit of definitive conclusions. The existence of competing economic theories and models reflects the difficulty of capturing the full complexity of economic phenomena within a single, unified framework.

Statistical and computational methods broaden what can be studied, but they do not repair biased data or identify causes by themselves. Researchers must explain the comparison group, assignment process, assumptions, uncertainty, and whether results transfer to other settings.

The criticism that economics is “dismal” because of its methodological challenges overlooks the discipline’s contributions to understanding and addressing key societal issues, such as poverty, inequality, and market failures. While acknowledging its limitations, it is important to recognize that economics provides essential tools for policy analysis and decision-making, aiming to improve societal welfare within the constraints of scarce resources.

Gemini response · editorial edition

You’re right, some modern critics echo the “dismal science” idea by pointing to the limitations of economics as a scientific discipline. Here’s a breakdown of their arguments and some counterpoints:

Criticisms of Economics as a Dismal Science (Modern Version):

  • Isolating causes: Economic outcomes have many influences. Experiments can isolate some effects; observational methods require credible assumptions about what would have happened without the intervention. Physics and chemistry also confront complex systems.
  • Human Behavior: Economic models often rely on assumptions about human behavior, which can be unpredictable and influenced by emotions and social factors.
  • Historical Specificity: Economic theories may not hold true across different historical periods or cultures.

Counterpoints to the Criticisms:

  • Progress in Methodology: Economists are constantly developing more sophisticated methods for data analysis and causal inference.
  • Behavioral Economics: This subfield integrates psychology into economic models to account for human biases and emotions.
  • Mechanisms: A proposed mechanism explains how an effect might arise. Its plausibility is useful, but evidence must still distinguish it from rival explanations and test when it applies.

Overall, while economics may not be a perfect science, it does strive to be rigorous and evidence-based. The field acknowledges its limitations and constantly refines its methods to provide better insights into economic behavior and policy.

Here are some additional points to consider:

  • Predictive Power: While economics may not perfectly predict future events, it can identify trends and potential risks, informing better decision-making.
  • Policy Formulation: Economic models, though imperfect, are valuable tools for policymakers to evaluate potential outcomes of different interventions.

So, the debate about economics as a “dismal science” continues. However, recognizing its limitations shouldn’t diminish the valuable role economics plays in understanding our complex world.

The perceived credibility and predictive accuracy of economic theories and models appear to have increased substantially in recent decades, accompanied by a period of relative macroeconomic stability in many developed economies. To what extent can this phenomenon be attributed to the proliferation of empirical economic research and controlled experiments, advances in theoretical modeling and econometric techniques, the use of technological innovations in data collection and computational analysis, or other potential factors? ChatGPT response · editorial edition

The prompt’s premise needs qualification. Some empirical methods have improved substantially, but that does not establish a uniform increase in macroeconomic forecast accuracy or credibility. The Great Moderation describes reduced volatility in many advanced economies before the financial crisis; later crises show why a stable interval is not proof that forecasting has been solved.

  1. Empirical research and experiments: Randomized trials can estimate effects of particular interventions; natural experiments exploit circumstances that plausibly approximate random assignment. The 2021 economics prize recognized empirical and causal-method contributions. Effects may depend on the population, implementation, and scale. A local causal estimate is not evidence of accurate aggregate forecasting.
  2. Theory and econometrics: Models make assumptions explicit, while statistical methods can estimate relationships under stated conditions. More flexible methods may improve fit yet overfit the sample. Test new predictions on data not used to choose the model and compare with simple alternatives.
  3. Data and computation: Larger datasets, simulations, and machine learning expand analytical capacity. Their value depends on representativeness, measurement, privacy, and performance outside the training sample; correlations can change when policy or behavior changes.
  4. Behavioral economics: Psychological findings can improve particular models of decisions. Adding behavioral mechanisms does not automatically improve every forecast; the revised model needs its own empirical comparison.
  5. Macroeconomic stability: In his 2004 Great Moderation speech, Ben Bernanke considered structural change, improved policy, and smaller shocks. Their relative contributions were disputed. Stability alone cannot identify how much credit belongs to economic research, and policy success is not equivalent to advance prediction of a crisis.

Theory, data, and credible research designs can inform each other and improve decisions. Assess the result question by question, using published assumptions, uncertainty, forecast comparisons, and later evaluation. Neither a growing dataset nor a calm period establishes general predictive mastery.

Gemini response · editorial edition

These are plausible contributors to better research, but the premise of a broad increase in forecast accuracy requires evidence. Separate improved causal inference about particular policies from successful predictions of economy-wide fluctuations.

Factors Contributing to Perceived Credibility and Predictive Accuracy:

  • Empirical Research and Controlled Experiments:
    • The rise of empirical studies using real-world data has helped ground economic theories in evidence.
    • Randomized field and laboratory experiments are used in economics. When randomization is infeasible, quasi-experimental methods can help if their identifying assumptions are credible; ethical and practical constraints apply to both.
  • Advancements in Theoretical Modeling and Econometric Techniques:
    • More sophisticated economic models that better capture real-world complexities have been developed.
    • Improved econometric techniques can support causal inference when the data and research design satisfy their assumptions; statistical sophistication alone does not identify a cause.
  • Technological Innovations in Data Collection and Analysis:
    • The explosion of digital data and advancements in data collection methods (e.g., web scraping, sensor networks) have provided economists with a richer and more detailed picture of the economy.
    • Increased computational power allows for processing of massive datasets and the application of complex statistical models.

Interplay and Impact:

  • These factors work together to strengthen the scientific rigor of economics.
  • Empirical research informs model development, which is then tested using advanced econometrics.
  • New data allow further tests and revisions, but repeated model selection on the same data can overfit. Reserve independent tests and report unsuccessful predictions.
  • This improved scientific foundation enhances the ability of economic models to:
    • Inform Policymaking:
      • By analyzing potential outcomes of policy interventions, economists can provide more robust recommendations for policymakers.
    • Anticipate Economic Fluctuations:
      • Models can inform conditional scenarios and risk monitoring. Claims of more accurate forecasts require a specified variable, horizon, benchmark, and evaluation period.

However, it’s important to consider limitations:

  • Economic models are still simplifications of reality, and human behavior remains a challenge to fully account for.
  • The field is constantly evolving, and past successes don’t guarantee future accuracy.

These developments can improve research and policy analysis. Whether they improve macroeconomic forecasting, and how much they contributed to stability, remain separate empirical questions.

Other Potential Factors:

  • Increased Focus on Specific Phenomena: Research may have become more targeted, leading to deeper understanding of specific economic issues.
  • Greater Transparency and Open Access to Data: This allows for independent verification of research findings and fosters collaboration.

The field seeks better explanations and policy evidence, but progress is uneven. A credible assessment includes failures, changing institutions, and independent replication as well as successful studies.

Prompt 4: How has the evolution of economics affected the evolution of economics programs in universities? How has the recommended skill set changed over the decades?

How has the evolution of economics affected the evolution of economics programs in universities? How has the recommended skill set changed over the decades?

ChatGPT response · editorial edition

Economics curricula have changed with research methods, computing, and employment needs. The outline below describes broad tendencies, not a uniform sequence followed by every country, university, or degree level.

Early to Mid-20th Century

  • Theory, history, and institutions: Many earlier programs emphasized these subjects, but mathematical economics and empirical work also have long histories. No single “original curriculum” represents all universities.

Late 20th Century

  • Quantitative and Analytical Skills: As the field began to emphasize more quantitative methods, economics programs started incorporating more mathematics and statistics into their curricula. This shift was in response to the growing use of mathematical models and statistical analysis in economic research. Skills in calculus, linear algebra, and statistical theory became crucial for understanding and conducting economic analysis.
  • Computational Skills: With the advent of computers and software, computational skills started to become more important. Students were encouraged to learn programming languages and software tools for data analysis, such as Stata, SAS, or R, to perform econometric analyses and manipulate large datasets.

Early 21st Century to Present

  • Data and programming: Many programs teach statistical computing and data analysis; advanced courses may add machine learning. Python and R are useful examples, not universal requirements, and “more complex” is not a meaningful reason to prefer a language.
  • Interdisciplinary Approach: Economics programs have also become more interdisciplinary, reflecting the field’s integration of insights from psychology (behavioral economics), political science (political economy), and environmental science (environmental economics), among others. This broadened perspective necessitates a diverse skill set, including the ability to engage with literature and methodologies from related fields.
  • Soft Skills and Critical Thinking: Beyond technical skills, there’s an increased emphasis on developing soft skills such as critical thinking, problem-solving, effective communication, and the ability to work in diverse teams. These skills are vital for applying economic reasoning and quantitative analysis to real-world problems and policy questions.

Looking Forward

  • Ethics and global issues: Inequality, development, and environmental questions can connect technical methods with ethical judgment. Their prominence varies; the response does not establish that every program now requires separate ethics courses.

In summary, the evolution of economics has led to a corresponding evolution in economics programs at universities, with a significant shift towards quantitative and computational skills, interdisciplinary approaches, and a broader understanding of global and ethical issues. This change reflects the discipline’s ongoing efforts to remain relevant and effective in addressing complex economic problems in a rapidly changing world.

Gemini response · editorial edition

The evolution of economics as a field has definitely impacted how economics programs are structured in universities. Here’s a look at the cause-and-effect relationship:

Changes in Economics, Changes in Curriculum:

  • Greater empirical emphasis: Many programs expanded econometrics and data analysis alongside theory. This did not result simply from the phrase “dismal science,” nor did economics previously consist only of pure theory.
  • Rise of Subfields: The emergence of new areas like behavioral economics, development economics, and environmental economics led to the introduction of specialized courses and even entire tracks within economics programs.
  • Focus on Technology and Problem-Solving: The increasing role of technology in data collection and analysis is reflected in programs offering courses on big data, coding, and computational methods. Additionally, there’s a growing emphasis on applying economic theory to solve real-world problems.

Impact on Skill Sets:

  • Quantitative Skills: The ability to handle complex data, use statistical software, and conduct econometric analysis is now a core skill set for economists.
  • Computer Programming: Coding skills are becoming increasingly valuable for economists to manipulate and analyze data.
  • Communication and Storytelling: Economists need to effectively communicate complex concepts and findings to a variety of audiences, both technical and non-technical.

Beyond the Core:

  • Interdisciplinary Approaches: Economics programs are recognizing the value of interdisciplinary approaches, encouraging students to take courses in fields like psychology, sociology, and computer science to complement their economics foundation.
  • Soft Skills: Critical thinking, problem-solving, and the ability to work effectively in teams are essential skills for economists in any field.

Not all change is uniform:

  • The specific curriculum changes may vary depending on the university’s focus (e.g., research-oriented vs. undergraduate-focused) and program type (e.g., Bachelors, Masters, PhD).
  • The pace of change may also differ – some programs adapt rapidly, while others may take longer to incorporate new trends.

Overall, the evolution of economics has led to economics programs becoming more quantitative, data-driven, and interdisciplinary, while equipping students with a broader skill set that goes beyond traditional economic theory.
