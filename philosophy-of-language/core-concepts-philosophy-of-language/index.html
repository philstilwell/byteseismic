Prompt 1: List and define 30 key terms in the philosophy of language.

Thirty terms in each glossary

ChatGPT response · editorial edition

  1. Denotation: The direct, explicit meaning or reference of a word or term, as opposed to its implied or associated meanings (connotations).
  2. Connotation: The implied or associated meaning of a word or phrase, in addition to its explicit or direct meaning (denotation). Connotations can involve emotional, cultural, or social overtones.
  3. Convention: A shared practice or norm governing language use. Speakers need not explicitly agree to it; learning a language usually means acquiring established patterns of meaning and use.
  4. Semantic Drift: The process by which the meaning of a word changes over time. This can occur due to changes in culture, technology, or social attitudes, leading to a shift in how a word is understood or used.
  5. Disambiguation: The process of resolving ambiguity and clarifying the meaning of words, phrases, or sentences in a specific context. This is crucial for understanding and interpreting language accurately.
  6. Syntax: The set of rules and principles that govern the structure of sentences in a language, including the arrangement of words and phrases.
  7. Semantics: The branch of linguistics concerned with the meaning of words, phrases, and sentences, and how meaning is constructed and interpreted.
  8. Pragmatics: The study of how context influences the interpretation of meaning in language, including the use of language in social contexts and the implications of utterances.
  9. Phonetics: The study of the sounds of human speech, including their physical production, acoustic properties, and auditory perception.
  10. Phonology: The branch of linguistics that deals with the systematic organization of sounds in languages, including the patterns and distribution of phonemes.
  11. Morphology: The study of the structure and form of words in a language, including the formation of words through the combination of morphemes.
  12. Morpheme: The smallest grammatical unit in a language that carries meaning. Morphemes can be words, prefixes, or suffixes.
  13. Reference: The relation between an expression used in context and what it picks out. “That planet,” accompanied by pointing, can refer to Venus. This replaces the original list’s second occurrence of Syntax.
  14. Grammar: The set of structural rules governing the composition of clauses, phrases, and words in any given natural language.
  15. Implicature: Something a speaker conveys beyond what is explicitly said. “Some guests arrived” may suggest that not all arrived, though the speaker can cancel that suggestion. This replaces the repeated Pragmatics entry.
  16. Discourse: Extended verbal communication or debate; the way in which language is used in texts and contexts to convey broader meanings.
  17. Semiotics: The study of signs and symbols, their use and interpretation. In linguistics, it deals with how meaning is created and communicated.
  18. Phoneme: The smallest unit of sound in a language that can distinguish one word from another.
  19. Grapheme: A contrastive unit in a writing system. Graphemes do not always correspond one-to-one with phonemes; writing systems differ in what their units represent.
  20. Presupposition: Background information an utterance takes for granted. “Mira stopped smoking” normally presupposes that Mira previously smoked. This replaces the third Syntax entry.
  21. Linguistic Relativity: The hypothesis that the structure of a language affects its speakers’ world view or cognition.
  22. Code-Switching: The practice of alternating between two or more languages or varieties of language in conversation.
  23. Diglossia: A stable division of functions between language varieties in a community, often with one used in formal settings and another in everyday conversation. Merely speaking two languages is not sufficient.
  24. Polysemy: Several related senses of one word, as with the mouth of a person and the mouth of a river. Related senses differ from unrelated words that happen to share a form.
  25. Homophony: The phenomenon of two or more words having the same pronunciation but different meanings, origins, or spelling.
  26. Metaphor: A figure of speech in which a word or phrase is applied to an object or action to which it is not literally applicable.
  27. Metonymy: A figure of speech in which a thing or concept is referred to by the name of something closely associated with that thing or concept.
  28. Synchronic Analysis: The study of a language at a particular point in time.
  29. Diachronic Analysis: The study of changes in a language over time.
  30. Corpus Linguistics: The study of language as expressed in corpora (bodies) of “real world” text, utilizing computational and statistical techniques to analyze and interpret language patterns.

The source begins a thirty-term glossary but stops during entry 23. The following retains its sequence, completes that definition, and supplies entries 24–30 as identified editorial additions. These linguistic terms support the philosophical concepts developed in the next response.

  1. Denotation: The literal meaning of a word or phrase, independent of any additional emotional associations or implications. For example, the denotation of the word “red” is the color red, regardless of any cultural associations with the color (e.g., love, danger, etc.).
  2. Connotation: The additional emotional associations or implications of a word or phrase beyond its literal meaning. For example, the word “red” may also connote concepts like love, passion, anger, or danger depending on the context.
  3. Convention: A shared linguistic practice that permits coordination without requiring explicit agreement. English speakers call a familiar animal a “cat”; the sound is not inherently tied to the animal, though not every feature of language is arbitrary.
  4. Semantic drift: The gradual change in the meaning of a word or phrase over time. For example, the word “meat” originally referred to any kind of food, but it has come to primarily refer to the flesh of animals.
  5. Disambiguation: Resolving multiple available readings using context. “We met by the bank” could indicate a financial institution or a riverbank; a reference to fishing would favor the latter.
  6. Reference: The relation between a contextual use of an expression and its target. “That cat,” with a pointing gesture, can pick out one animal; this differs from describing the general category of cats.
  7. Speech act: The action performed by saying something, such as making a request, giving a command, or offering a promise. For example, saying “I apologize” is a speech act of apology.
  8. Pragmatics: The study of how context affects the meaning of language. This includes factors such as the speaker’s intention, the relationship between the speaker and hearer, and the shared knowledge they possess.
  9. Prescriptivism: Recommending or enforcing standards for how people should use language, often for a particular setting. A style rule can be useful for a publication without establishing that other dialects lack grammar.
  10. Descriptivism: The view that language is a constantly evolving system and that the goal of linguistics is to describe how language is actually used rather than prescribe rules for how it should be used.
  11. Competence: The knowledge of the rules and structure of a language that allows a speaker to produce and understand grammatical sentences.
  12. Performance: Actual language use, including pauses, slips and memory limits. In the competence/performance distinction, a failed sentence need not show that a speaker lacks the relevant linguistic knowledge.
  13. Grammar: The system of rules governing the structure of words, phrases, and sentences in a language.
  14. Morphology: The study of word structure. “Unhappy” has two morphemes: “un-” and “happy.” “Unhappily” adds the suffix “-ly,” with a spelling change in the root.
  15. Syntax: The study of how words are combined to form phrases and sentences, and the rules governing the order and arrangement of words in a sentence.
  16. Semantics: The study of meaning in language, including how words and sentences relate to the entities and ideas they represent.
  17. Phonology: The study of how a language organizes contrastive sounds and their patterns. It differs from phonetics, which examines speech sounds as physical events.
  18. Phoneme: The smallest unit of sound in a language that can distinguish one word from another. For example, the sounds /p/ and /b/ are distinct phonemes in English, as they can differentiate words like “pin” and “bin.”
  19. Allophone: A phonetic realization of a phoneme. In many English varieties, /t/ is aspirated in “top” but unaspirated after /s/ in “stop”; that difference does not distinguish words by itself.
  20. Articulation: The physical production of speech sounds by the vocal organs, including the lungs, larynx, tongue, and lips.
  21. Phonetics: The study of the production, transmission, and perception of speech sounds.
  22. Dialect: A variety of a language that is distinguished from other varieties by its pronunciation, grammar, or vocabulary.
  23. Register: A variety of language associated with a purpose or situation, such as a technical report or a conversation among friends. Speakers may change vocabulary and formality across registers. Editorial completion of the source’s interrupted definition.

Editorial additions completing the requested thirty entries:

  1. Proposition: What a declarative utterance expresses as true or false; different sentences can express the same proposition.
  2. Entailment: A relation in which the truth of one claim guarantees another. “Every guest arrived” entails that no guest failed to arrive.
  3. Implicature: An additional message inferred from a speaker’s words and context, often cancelable without contradiction.
  4. Presupposition: Information treated as background, as prior smoking is in “Mira stopped smoking.”
  5. Indexical: An expression such as “I,” “here” or “now” whose reference depends on the context of use.
  6. Vagueness: The presence of borderline cases, as with “tall”; this differs from having several distinct meanings.
  7. Compositionality: The principle that the meaning of a complex expression depends on its parts and their mode of combination.

Prompt 2: List and provide explanations of key concepts in the philosophy of language.

Meaning, reference and use

ChatGPT response · editorial edition

  1. Truth Conditions: What would have to be the case for a statement to be true, distinct from the evidence by which we discover whether it is true. “The window is open” is true if the relevant window is open, even when nobody has checked.
  2. Reference and referential theories: Reference concerns what an expression picks out. Referential theories seek to explain meaning through this relation, but an account must also handle expressions without ordinary objects as referents and different ways of presenting the same object.
  3. Sense and Reference: Frege distinguishes an expression’s referent from its mode of presentation. “The morning star” and “the evening star” both pick out Venus but present it differently. Learning that they identify the same object can therefore be informative.
  4. Speech Acts: The actions performed via utterances, according to J.L. Austin. Speech acts can be classified into locutionary acts (the act of saying something), illocutionary acts (the social function of what is said, such as requesting, promising), and perlocutionary acts (the effect achieved by saying something, like convincing, scaring).
  5. Pragmatics: How speakers communicate through context and use. “It is cold in here” can function as a request to close a window. The request is not entailed by the sentence: “but please leave the window open” can cancel it. This distinguishes contextual inference from logical consequence.
  6. Intension and Extension: An extension is what an expression applies to in a circumstance; in possible-world semantics, an intension assigns extensions across possible circumstances. Two predicates may apply to the same things here yet differ in what they would apply to elsewhere. An intension is not simply a collection of every possible referent.
  7. Propositional Attitudes: The attitude someone holds toward a proposition, such as believing, desiring, hoping. Propositional attitudes are crucial in understanding the cognitive states of individuals and how they relate to the truth conditions of statements.
  8. Language Games: A concept introduced by Ludwig Wittgenstein, suggesting that the meaning of words is determined by their use in various forms of social interaction, which he termed “language games.” This concept emphasizes the fluidity of language’s meaning based on context and usage.
  9. Semiotics: The study of signs and symbols as elements of communicative behavior; the analysis of systems of communication, including language. Semiotics involves understanding how meaning is constructed and interpreted.
  10. The Principle of Compositionality: The principle that the meaning of a complex expression is determined by the meanings of its constituent expressions and the rules used to combine them. This principle is fundamental in semantics.
  11. Private Language Argument: Wittgenstein questions whether signs tied to sensations accessible only to their user could acquire a standard distinguishing correct use from merely seeming correct. This is not an objection to diaries or secret codes based on a learnable language. Interpretations differ; the issue is a criterion of correctness, not simply how many people speak. See Philosophical Investigations §§243–258.
  12. Conventionality: The idea that linguistic meaning arises from social conventions or agreements among speakers of a language. This concept emphasizes the role of societal norms in the establishment and understanding of language.
  13. Indeterminacy of Translation: Quine argues that rival overall translation schemes can fit all the relevant behavioral evidence while assigning different translations. The thesis concerns underdetermination even with the evidence available in principle, not a claim that ordinary translation has no objective constraints or that every translation is equally good.
  14. Metaphor: Considered both a linguistic and cognitive phenomenon, metaphors involve understanding and experiencing one thing in terms of another. Philosophers of language study metaphors to understand how they convey meaning and shape thought.
  15. Deixis: Context-dependent pointing through expressions such as “here,” “now” and “you.” Understanding “Meet me here tomorrow” requires identifying the speaker, place and time of utterance, not just knowing dictionary meanings.
  16. Performative Utterances: Another concept from J.L. Austin, where certain expressions do not just convey information but perform a certain action. For example, saying “I apologize” performs the act of apologizing.
  17. Normativity of Meaning: The idea that the meanings of words are governed by norms or rules within a language community. This concept explores how the correct use of language is determined by these shared norms.

These concepts represent foundational ideas in the philosophy of language, exploring how language functions, how meanings are conveyed, and how linguistic communication shapes and is shaped by human cognition and social interaction.

Gemini response · editorial edition

Here are 10 key concepts in the philosophy of language with explanations:

  1. Meaning: This is a central and contested concept in the philosophy of language. While everyone agrees that words and sentences convey meaning, there are different theories about what meaning is and how it works. Some prominent theories include:
    • Referential Theory: Meaning is determined by what words and sentences refer to in the real world. For example, the word “cat” refers to the category of feline animals.
    • Mentalist approaches: Accounts connecting linguistic meaning with ideas or mental representations. They must explain how speakers share meanings despite differences in imagery and associations; whatever a word happens to evoke is not automatically its meaning.
  2. Reference: This refers to the act of using language to connect words or phrases to entities or ideas in the world. For example, the word “cat” refers to the real-world category of feline animals. There are different theories about how reference works, such as:
    • Direct Reference Theory: For expressions treated as directly referential, their contribution to propositional content is their referent rather than a descriptive condition. This is a claim about semantic content, not a denial that speakers use mental representations to identify people or things.
    • Descriptive Theory of Reference: Words and phrases refer to their referents by providing a description that uniquely identifies them.
  3. Truth: This concept is closely related to meaning and reference. A statement is considered true if it accurately reflects the state of the world that it describes. However, there are different theories about what makes a statement true, such as:
    • Correspondence Theory of Truth: A statement is true if it corresponds with the way the world actually is.
    • Coherence Theory of Truth: Accounts explaining truth through coherence within a suitably specified system of propositions. Mere agreement with whatever beliefs someone already accepts is insufficient; the adequacy of the system and the kind of coherence require explanation.
  4. Speech Acts: These are the actions performed by saying something, such as making a request, giving a command, or offering a promise. For example, saying “I apologize” is a speech act of apology. Philosophers of language are interested in the conditions under which speech acts are successful and the relationship between what is said and what is done in saying it.
  5. Intentionality: This refers to the ability of mental states (such as beliefs, desires, and intentions) to be directed towards objects or states of affairs in the world. In the context of language, philosophers are interested in the role of speaker intention in determining meaning and reference.
  6. Vagueness: Many words and phrases are vague, meaning that they have unclear boundaries or edges in their application. For example, it can be difficult to define exactly what counts as a “tall” person. Philosophers of language debate the nature and significance of vagueness in language and how we deal with it in communication.
  7. Ambiguity: This refers to situations where a word or phrase has multiple possible meanings. For example, the sentence “The bank is closed” could refer to a financial institution or the edge of a river. Philosophers of language are interested in how we disambiguate language and resolve ambiguity in communication.
  8. Frege’s Puzzle: Why can “The morning star is the evening star” convey an astronomical discovery while “The morning star is the morning star” is uninformative, if the expressions refer to the same planet? The puzzle motivates separating reference from mode of presentation rather than equating meaning with the object named.
  9. Wittgenstein’s Later Philosophy: Ludwig Wittgenstein, a prominent philosopher, argued against traditional theories of language that tried to provide a single, unified account of how language works. Instead, he emphasized the diversity of language use and the importance of understanding meaning in the context of specific language games.
  10. Performativity: This concept suggests that some utterances do not simply describe the world but also perform actions in the world by saying them. For example, saying “I promise” does not just describe a mental state but also creates a new obligation for the speaker. Philosophers of language are interested in the nature and significance of performative utterances.

These are just a few examples, and there are many other fascinating and complex areas of inquiry within the philosophy of language.

Prompt 3: Provide a timeline of the philosophy of language. Include deeper explanations for any paradigm shifts.

Changes in philosophical method

ChatGPT response · editorial edition

This selective timeline follows the original response’s predominantly European and analytic sequence. Its “shifts” are changes of emphasis and method, not moments when every philosopher abandoned one settled theory for another.

Ancient Philosophy

  • 5th to 4th century BCE: Early explorations by Plato and Aristotle in ancient Greece laid the groundwork. Plato questioned the relationship between words, things, and ideas in his dialogues, while Aristotle’s “On Interpretation” discussed the relation between words and their referents, and the dichotomy between truth and falsehood.

Medieval Philosophy

  • Late antiquity through the Middle Ages: Augustine’s work on signs belongs to late antiquity and influenced medieval thought. Boethius transmitted and commented on ancient logic; later scholastics, including Aquinas, investigated universals, predication and theological language. Their questions linked the structure of statements to what could be said about things.

Early Modern Philosophy

  • 17th and 18th centuries: Philosophers like John Locke, Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz, and George Berkeley considered the origins of language, the connections between words and ideas, and the role of language in human understanding. Locke’s notion of words as symbols for ideas and his distinction between primary and secondary qualities were influential.

Late 19th Century: The Linguistic Turn

  • Late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries: Frege’s logic and sense/reference distinction made linguistic analysis a powerful method for investigating thought and knowledge. The shift was not the discovery that language mattered; ancient and medieval thinkers already studied it. New logical tools exposed distinctions that ordinary grammar can conceal.

Early to Mid 20th Century

  • Early 1900s: Ludwig Wittgenstein’s “Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus” (1921) introduced the idea that the structure of language mirrors the structure of reality, suggesting that philosophical problems arise from misunderstandings of the logic of language.
  • 1930s–1940s; posthumous publication in 1953: Wittgenstein developed the later investigations of language-games and use before Philosophical Investigations appeared. Asking how an expression functions in an activity—giving an order, telling a story, making a calculation—challenged the demand for one model fitting every meaningful utterance.
  • Mid-20th Century: The development of ordinary language philosophy, particularly by J.L. Austin and later by John Searle, focused on the analysis of everyday language and introduced the concept of speech acts, further emphasizing the pragmatic and social aspects of language.

Late 20th to 21st Century

  • 1960s onward: Davidson developed truth-theoretic approaches to meaning; Kripke and Putnam challenged influential descriptive accounts of names and natural-kind terms. Kripke’s Naming and Necessity lectures were delivered in 1970, published in 1972 and issued as a book in 1980. A person can use a name successfully while holding mistaken descriptions of its bearer, motivating attention to how reference is transmitted through a community.
  • Contemporary Discussions: Today, the philosophy of language intersects with cognitive science, computer science (particularly in areas like natural language processing), and linguistics, exploring the cognitive underpinnings of language, the nature of linguistic communication, and the evolution of digital and artificial languages.

Paradigm Shifts Summary

  • The Linguistic Turn: The shift towards seeing language as the central subject of philosophy itself, influencing how philosophical problems are framed and approached.
  • From structure toward use: Attention to promising, requesting and other activities broadened inquiry beyond descriptive sentences. Formal semantics continued to develop; studying use did not make logical structure irrelevant.
  • Expansion to Interdisciplinary Connections: The growing intersection with cognitive science, computer science, and linguistics, reflecting a broader understanding of language as a cognitive and social tool.

These shifts reflect the evolving understanding of language’s role in human thought, communication, and social interaction, illustrating the dynamic nature of the philosophy of language.

Gemini response · editorial edition

A Narrative Timeline of the Philosophy of Language: A Journey Through Meaning

Pre-20th Century: Seeds of Inquiry

  • Ancient Greece (5th-4th centuries BC): Plato and Aristotle, considered precursors, grappled with questions of language and thought. Plato explored the nature of meaning and reference in his dialogue “Cratylus,” while Aristotle focused on logic and the structure of language in “De Interpretatione.”
  • Medieval Period (5th-15th centuries AD): Scholars like Thomas Aquinas debated the relationship between language and reality. They studied “suppositio,” referring to how words stand for things, laying the groundwork for future theories of reference.

Early 20th Century: The “Linguistic Turn”

  • Late 19th century: Gottlob Frege, a mathematician and philosopher, revolutionized the field with his work on logic and language. His distinction between “sense” (how a word is understood) and “reference” (what it refers to) had a profound impact.
  • Early 20th century: Bertrand Russell built upon Frege’s work, developing the “Theory of Descriptions” to explain how we refer to things using phrases like “the tallest mountain.” This period, known as the “Linguistic Turn,” saw philosophers shift focus to understanding the world through language analysis.

Mid-20th Century: Shifting Tides

  • 1921: Wittgenstein’s Tractatus presented a picture account of propositions: a proposition represents a possible arrangement of things. It was highly influential, but it was not the sole philosophy of language of its period.
  • 1920s–1930s: The Vienna Circle connected the cognitive meaning of empirical claims with verification or confirmation, while distinguishing logical and mathematical statements. Versions of the criterion differed and were revised. Requiring conclusive verification of every meaningful statement proved too restrictive for general scientific laws; verification and falsification should not simply be equated.
  • 1930s–1940s: Wittgenstein developed the later work published posthumously as Philosophical Investigations in 1953. Its attention to language-games challenges the assumption that all meaningful speech works by describing facts. The development preceded publication, not the reverse.

Late 20th Century and Beyond: Diversifying Perspectives

  • 1951: In Two Dogmas of Empiricism, Quine challenged both a sharp analytic/synthetic divide and reduction of each meaningful statement to immediate experience. His alternative emphasizes how connected bodies of theory face evidence. This is not simply a thesis that words depend on neighboring words, nor an instruction to reject logic.
  • Mid-20th century onwards: Philosophy of language diversified, exploring various topics:
    • Speech Act Theory: Examining the actions performed through language (e.g., promising, apologizing).
    • Pragmatics: Studying how context affects meaning and communication.
    • Ordinary Language Philosophy: Emphasizing the importance of analyzing language in its everyday use.
    • Hermeneutics: Focusing on interpretation and understanding in the context of language, history, and culture.

The Present and Future:

The philosophy of language continues to evolve, tackling new challenges like:

  • The relationship between language and thought: Are they distinct or intertwined?
  • The role of embodiment in language: How do our bodies and the environment influence meaning?
  • Machine learning and language: What can AI teach us about human language processing and meaning-making?

This narrative timeline offers a glimpse into the dynamic journey of the philosophy of language. Each paradigm shift arose from attempts to grapple with fundamental questions: how do we use language to express ourselves and understand the world? This ongoing exploration continues to shape our understanding of ourselves and the world around us.

Research directions and their limits

ChatGPT response · editorial edition

The February 2024 source groups research directions and interdisciplinary possibilities under “new areas.” The list below preserves those topics without implying that they all began recently or constitute equally established specialties.

  1. Computational Linguistics and AI: The interaction between natural language processing (NLP) and artificial intelligence (AI) has become a fertile ground for philosophical inquiry. This includes questions about the nature of meaning and understanding in machines, the possibility of AI achieving true linguistic competence, and the ethical implications of AI in language use.
  2. Digital and Internet Language: The advent of digital communication has led to new forms of language and communication practices. Philosophers of language are interested in the implications of these changes for traditional concepts of meaning, identity, and community, including how digital platforms influence language evolution.
  3. Interspecies Communication: Advances in cognitive science and animal studies have prompted philosophers to explore the boundaries of language and communication beyond the human species. This includes investigating the nature of communication among animals and the potential for meaningful interspecies dialogue.
  4. Neurophilosophy of Language: This area bridges neuroscience and philosophy to explore how language functions are realized in the brain. Topics of interest include the neural basis of linguistic understanding, the relationship between language and thought, and the neurological differences underlying various language capabilities.
  5. Language and Embodiment: Emphasizing the role of the body in shaping linguistic understanding and communication, this area examines how sensory experiences and bodily interactions with the environment contribute to language acquisition, comprehension, and usage.
  6. Ecological Linguistics: Reflecting growing concerns about environmental issues, ecological linguistics explores the relationship between language, ecological thought, and environmental action. It investigates how language shapes and reflects our relationship with the natural world and how linguistic practices can promote sustainability.
  7. Ethics of Communication: The ethical dimensions of language use, including issues of speech acts, misinformation, censorship, and free speech, have gained renewed interest. This includes examining the responsibilities of speakers and listeners in various contexts and the impact of language on social justice.
  8. Language and Identity: With increasing awareness of social diversity and the politics of identity, philosophers of language are examining how language reflects, constructs, and influences identities. This includes studies on gendered language, linguistic discrimination, and the role of language in cultural and ethnic identity.
  9. Language and Mindfulness: A possible interdisciplinary question is how attention to wording and interpretation affects communication. The source’s label is retained as a proposed direction, not a claim that a distinct established specialty exists or that linguistic mindfulness has demonstrated therapeutic benefits.
  10. Transhumanism and Posthuman Language: As technology advances, there is speculative interest in how language might evolve in transhuman or posthuman contexts. This includes considering how enhancements to human cognitive and communicative abilities might transform language practices and the conceptual frameworks we use to understand language.

These areas reflect the dynamic and expanding nature of the philosophy of language, incorporating insights from various disciplines and addressing complex questions about the role of language in an increasingly interconnected and technologically advanced world.

Gemini response · editorial edition

These six topics mix established research programs with newer applications. “New” here describes directions highlighted by the 2024 conversation, not a ranking of current research or a claim that each originated recently.

1. Two-dimensional semantics: These approaches use two parameters to distinguish aspects of meaning. In an epistemic version, one asks what an expression would pick out if a scenario were actual; another asks how it applies in counterfactual circumstances with the actual reference fixed. This is not the division between a word’s internal structure and its sentence neighbors. See David Chalmers’s account.

2. Modality and epistemic modals: “Might” and “must” can express what is possible or necessary relative to available information, rules or other standards. “The key might be upstairs” differs from “You must return it.” Knowledge and belief reports interact with these issues but are not themselves simply modal words.

3. Semantic relationism: Kit Fine’s proposal concerns representational relations between expressions or thoughts that are not reducible to their individual representational properties. It is not just the general claim that words acquire meaning from related words. His 2007 book develops coordination within language, thought and communication.

4. Language and embodiment: This emerging area explores the connection between our physical bodies and language use. It investigates how our bodily experiences and actions influence the way we think, communicate, and understand language.

5. Social and political aspects of language: This area investigates how language reflects and shapes social power dynamics, cultural identities, and political structures. It examines how language can be used to marginalize or empower different groups and explores the ethical implications of language use.

6. Philosophy of artificial language: In this response the label concerns artificial intelligence systems that process and generate natural language. Questions about successful use, reference and understanding must be distinguished from questions about consciousness. Fluent output alone does not settle all of them; formal or constructed languages are a separate use of “artificial language.”

These topics extend the older problems of meaning and reference into different explanatory settings. Their value depends on stating a precise question and what would count as an answer, rather than treating technological novelty as a solution.
