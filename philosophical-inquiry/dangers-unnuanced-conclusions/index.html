Prompt 1: Rational belief is a degree of belief that maps to the degree of the relevant evidence. What makes humans inclined to quickly reach for dogmatic conclusions that do not reflect the degree of the evidence?

Why confidence can outrun evidence

ChatGPT response · editorial edition

Confidence can serve two purposes that pull apart: representing what the evidence supports and providing emotional or social security. The following factors help explain that gap; they are possibilities to examine, not a diagnosis of every person who disagrees with us.

  1. Cognitive biases: Heuristics are shortcuts; biases are systematic tendencies toward error. A shortcut can work well, but confirmation bias can make us search for supporting observations while overlooking evidence that would change our minds.
  2. Emotional influences: Fear can make a familiar belief feel safer to retain, and belonging can make dissent costly. Emotions can also alert us to real risks. The problem arises when comfort or loyalty determines confidence independently of the evidence.
  3. Cultural and Social Conditioning: People are often raised in environments where certain beliefs are deeply ingrained. This cultural and social conditioning can make it difficult for individuals to question or reconsider these beliefs, even when presented with contradictory evidence.
  4. Cognitive Dissonance: When individuals encounter evidence that challenges their beliefs, it can create cognitive dissonance—a state of mental discomfort. To alleviate this discomfort, people may reject the new evidence or double down on their existing beliefs, even if they are irrational.
  5. Complexity and Uncertainty: Some issues are inherently complex, and the available evidence may be uncertain or ambiguous. In such cases, people may be more likely to rely on intuition, heuristics, or preconceived notions rather than engaging in thorough, rational analysis.
  6. Desire for Certainty: Humans have a natural desire for certainty and security. Dogmatic beliefs can provide a sense of stability and certainty in an uncertain world, even if they are not supported by evidence.
  7. Lack of Critical Thinking Skills: Finally, many people lack the critical thinking skills necessary to evaluate evidence objectively and critically. Without these skills, individuals may be more susceptible to dogmatic thinking.

Overall, dogmatic thinking is a complex phenomenon influenced by a variety of psychological, social, and cognitive factors. Overcoming it requires self-awareness, critical thinking skills, openness to new evidence, and a willingness to challenge one’s own beliefs.

You’re right, ideally, beliefs should align with evidence. But there are several reasons why humans often jump to dogmatic conclusions:

  • Cognitive biases: Shortcuts often help us cope with limited time. They mislead when, for example, we count confirming instances but never look for failures, or mistake an easily recalled event for a frequent one.
  • Emotional attachment: When a belief is tied to belonging or self-respect, reconsidering it can feel like a personal loss. That cost can discourage scrutiny; it does not itself show whether the belief is true. The original link about relationship attachment styles did not substantiate this claim and has been removed.
  • Social pressure: Humans are social creatures who crave group acceptance. We may adopt the beliefs of our family, community, or social circles to fit in, even if the evidence for those beliefs is weak.
  • Need for certainty: The world is complex and uncertain. Dogmatic beliefs can provide a sense of comfort and control, even if they’re not entirely accurate.

These are just some reasons why we stray from rationality. The good news is that we can become more aware of these biases and strive for a more nuanced understanding of the world by:

  • Seeking out diverse perspectives: Actively look for information that challenges your existing beliefs.
  • Evaluating evidence critically: Don’t accept information at face value. Look for the source, methodology, and potential biases behind any claim.
  • Being open to changing your mind: New evidence can emerge, and it’s okay to adapt your beliefs accordingly.

It’s a constant process, but by being mindful of these tendencies, we can move closer to forming beliefs that truly reflect the available evidence.

Claude response · editorial edition

Rational belief is a degree of belief that maps to the degree of the relevant evidence. What make humans inclined to quickly reach for dogmatic conclusions that do not reflect the degree of the evidence?

Several tendencies can make a confident answer easier to reach than a well-supported one. These mechanisms need not share a single evolutionary explanation, and useful mental shortcuts should not all be classified as errors:

  1. Confirmation bias: People tend to seek out, interpret, and remember information in a way that confirms their existing beliefs or hypotheses, while ignoring or discounting contradictory evidence.
  2. Motivated reasoning: People often have emotional or psychological motivations that lead them to arrive at particular conclusions, regardless of the evidence. These motivations can include desires for certainty, identity protection, or maintaining a positive self-image.
  3. Availability heuristic: People tend to judge the likelihood of events based on how easily examples come to mind, which can lead to overestimating the probability of vivid or recent events, and underestimating the probability of less salient occurrences.
  4. Anchoring bias: People can become overly attached to initial impressions or pieces of information, even when subsequent evidence contradicts or outweighs those initial anchors.
  5. Overconfidence: We may overestimate the accuracy of our judgments or give uncertainty ranges that are too narrow. Neither tendency occurs uniformly in every person or task.
  6. Tribalism and in-group bias: People have a strong tendency to favor and identify with their own social groups, which can lead to dogmatism and dismissal of evidence that challenges the group’s beliefs or interests.
  7. Effort and attention: Evaluating competing explanations takes time and mental effort. Fatigue, distraction or limited access to information can encourage premature closure; describing all such cases as laziness would itself erase important differences.

While these biases and heuristics can be adaptive in certain situations, they can also lead to dogmatic thinking that is disconnected from the available evidence. Overcoming these tendencies requires active effort to cultivate critical thinking, intellectual humility, and a willingness to update beliefs in light of new evidence.

Prompt 2: What are best practices to ensure our degree of confidence in a proposition does not exceed the degree of evidence for that proposition?

Practices for proportionate confidence

ChatGPT response · editorial edition

Begin by stating the proposition precisely: which population, outcome and time period does it concern? Evidence may strongly support a narrow claim while only weakly supporting a broader one. Then apply these checks:

  1. Be aware of cognitive biases: Awareness alone is not protection. Before deciding, write down what observation would count against your preferred view, and apply the same evidential standard to competing views.
  2. Seek diverse sources: Look for serious counterarguments and independent evidence. Ten articles repeating one study are not ten independent confirmations. Diversity helps reveal omissions; it does not require giving weak and strong evidence equal weight.
  3. Evaluate the Quality of Evidence: Assess the quality and reliability of the evidence supporting a proposition. Consider factors such as the source’s credibility, the methodology used to gather the evidence, and whether the evidence has been peer-reviewed or subject to rigorous scrutiny.
  4. Consider Alternative Explanations: When evaluating evidence, consider alternative explanations or hypotheses that could account for the observed data. Assess the strength of each explanation based on its explanatory power, coherence, and consistency with other evidence.
  5. Update Your Beliefs in Response to New Evidence: Be open to revising your beliefs in light of new evidence. Avoid clinging to dogmatic positions and be willing to adjust your degree of confidence in a proposition as new information becomes available.
  6. Quantify uncertainty: Use probabilities or ranges when you can explain their basis; avoid decorative precision. For repeatable forecasts, record predictions before outcomes are known and check whether events assigned similar probabilities occur at roughly those rates. For a one-off judgment, state assumptions and what would change your confidence.
  7. Engage in Critical Thinking: Develop and apply critical thinking skills to evaluate arguments, assess evidence, and identify logical fallacies. Learn to distinguish between sound reasoning and flawed arguments, and be willing to question your own assumptions and beliefs.
  8. Seek Feedback and Discussion: Engage in discussions with others who hold different perspectives or expertise. Solicit feedback on your reasoning and conclusions, and be open to constructive criticism that challenges your beliefs.

By following these best practices, you can strive to ensure that your degree of confidence in a proposition reflects the degree of evidence available, leading to more rational and informed beliefs and decisions.

Gemini response · editorial edition

Here are some best practices to ensure your confidence in a proposition aligns with the evidence:

Seek Diverse Evidence:

  • Go beyond confirmation bias: Don’t just look for information that confirms what you already believe. Actively seek out opposing viewpoints and research that might contradict your initial assumptions.
  • Consider the source: Relevant expertise and a record of correction matter, but reputation does not replace checking methods, conflicts of interest and whether several reports depend on the same evidence.
  • Beware of emotional appeals: Don’t let strong emotions or persuasive language cloud your judgment. Focus on facts and verifiable data.

Evaluate Evidence Critically:

  • Understand limitations of studies: Research often has limitations like sample size, methodology, or potential biases. Be aware of these limitations when interpreting results.
  • Look for independent support: Replication can strengthen a study’s result, though repeating a shared error does not cure it. Historical and singular events may instead be checked through independent records or converging methods.
  • Consider alternative explanations: Are there other factors that could explain the observed phenomenon? Don’t jump to conclusions based on limited evidence.

Maintain a Skeptical Mindset:

  • Don’t be afraid to say “I don’t know”: It’s perfectly okay to admit when the evidence isn’t clear enough to form a strong opinion.
  • Beware of empty hedging: Words such as “possibly” and “likely” help only if they convey the evidence’s strength. Strong evidence can justify a firm conclusion; adding “maybe” to everything is not calibration.
  • Embrace the process of learning: View new information as an opportunity to refine your understanding, not a threat to your existing beliefs.

Practice Humility and Open-Mindedness:

  • Be willing to change your mind: New evidence can emerge, and it’s a sign of intellectual growth to adapt your beliefs accordingly.
  • Engage in respectful discussions: Listen to opposing viewpoints with an open mind. Look for common ground and a deeper understanding of the issue.
  • Recognize the limits of human knowledge: There’s always more to learn. Stay curious and keep an open mind to new possibilities.

These practices reduce avoidable error without guaranteeing perfect confidence. Keep the belief question separate from the action question: a serious possible harm may justify a precaution before it becomes probable, while an expensive intervention may require stronger support. Acting cautiously does not require pretending the underlying claim is certain.

Claude response · editorial edition

Here are some best practices to help ensure our degree of confidence in a proposition does not exceed the degree of evidence for that proposition:

  1. Actively seek disconfirming evidence: Instead of just looking for evidence that confirms our existing beliefs, we should actively seek out contradictory information and counterarguments. This can help counter confirmation bias.
  2. Consider alternative explanations: Rather than fixating on one explanation, we should intentionally consider multiple possible explanations for the evidence and weigh their relative merits. This can mitigate anchoring bias.
  3. Be aware of motivated reasoning: We should reflect on our own potential biases, emotional attachments, or vested interests that could be influencing our interpretation of the evidence. Maintaining intellectual humility is key.
  4. Quantify uncertainties: A proposition can be true or false while our confidence in it is intermediate. Use a probability or range when defensible, and explain the assumptions; a precise-looking number does not supply missing evidence.
  5. Integrate new evidence objectively: As new evidence emerges, we should update our confidence levels accordingly, even if it means changing our position. Holding beliefs too rigidly can lead to dogmatism.
  6. Seek informed opinions: Consulting subject matter experts and authoritative sources can provide additional evidence and perspective beyond our own limited knowledge.
  7. Be wary of vivid anecdotes: While compelling stories and examples can shape our intuitions, we should balance them against robust statistical evidence to avoid falling prey to the availability heuristic.
  8. Remain revisable: Be willing to change your mind without treating every possibility as equally credible. Tentativeness concerns openness to correction, not a duty to keep well-supported conclusions weak.

Implementing these practices can help us maintain calibrated confidence levels that accurately reflect the strength of the evidence, rather than succumbing to overconfidence or belief perseverance in the face of contradictory information.

Prompt 3: Create a dialogue that saliently highlights the contrast between an individual who keeps their conclusions appropriately nuanced and and individual who does not.

Three dialogues about evidence and certainty

ChatGPT response · editorial edition

Alice (Nuanced Thinker): I’ve been reading about climate change. The evidence that human activity causes global warming is very strong. The uncertainty is not evenly spread: a specific local impact or policy forecast can be much less certain.

Bob (Unnuanced Thinker): Climate change? Oh, it’s just a hoax perpetuated by those environmentalists trying to push their agenda. There’s no real evidence to support it.

Alice: The IPCC assessment attributes global warming to human activity, drawing on several lines of evidence. Temperature measurements and physical explanations matter here, not just a headcount of scientists.

Bob: Come on, Alice, you’re buying into all that alarmist rhetoric. The planet has been through climate changes before. It’s just natural variability. We shouldn’t be wasting resources on these so-called solutions.

Alice: Past natural changes do not establish the cause of present warming. We have to compare explanations against the observations. I can be confident about the human contribution without claiming that every weather event has the same attribution or that every future regional effect is precisely known.

Bob: I don’t know, Alice. It just seems like fearmongering to me. Besides, even if climate change is real, there’s nothing we can do about it anyway.

Alice: Reducing emissions can limit further warming. That does not settle every choice of technology or policy: those choices also involve costs, timing and distribution of benefits. Uncertainty about the best policy does not erase evidence about the cause.

Bob: Well, agree to disagree, I guess. But I’m not convinced.

Alice: We can disagree, but the competing claims still need evidence. What would make you reconsider the hoax claim? I would revise a particular forecast if observations or a better-supported model contradicted it.

Bob: Yeah, yeah, I hear you. But I still think it’s all blown out of proportion.

Alice: Let’s agree to revisit this conversation after you’ve had a chance to explore the evidence more thoroughly. It’s essential to approach these issues with nuance and critical thinking to make informed decisions for the future.

Prompt 4: Provide cases from history in which unnuanced conclusions lead to disastrous results.

Historical failures and limits of the examples

ChatGPT response · editorial edition

These cases illustrate failures in handling evidence and uncertainty. Their disasters had multiple causes; the links support narrower claims than “a lack of nuance caused everything.” The relevant test is what decision-makers had reason to believe at the time, not merely whether events ended badly.

  1. Challenger (1986): The Rogers Commission found a flawed launch decision, including failures to convey engineers’ concerns about cold-weather joint performance to key decision-makers. Challenger broke apart 73 seconds after launch, killing all seven crew members. The lesson concerns communication and standards for accepting risk, not simply one person’s excessive confidence.
  2. Financial crisis (2007–2008): The Financial Crisis Inquiry Commission’s majority conclusions describe failures of regulation, governance and risk management. Confidence in financial products and safeguards was part of a wider institutional failure. Major institutions failed and the system suffered a severe crisis; saying the entire global financial system collapsed would overstate the outcome.
  3. Pearl Harbor (1941): Warnings of conflict did not produce adequate readiness for the attack on Hawaii. The Army’s historical account describes intelligence and organizational weaknesses. Treating one expected scenario as sufficiently exhaustive is a useful warning, but this summary does not establish that all commanders shared a single confident prediction or knew the attack’s target in advance.
  4. Soviet invasion of Afghanistan (1979): The intervention developed into a prolonged war. Released documents and participants’ retrospective discussions describe secrecy, faulty intelligence and miscalculation. The case cautions against assuming that installing a friendly government will secure durable control; it does not support a simple story of unanimous confidence in a quick victory.
  5. Bay of Pigs (1961): The US-backed invasion by Cuban exiles failed to overthrow Fidel Castro. The failed operation is a useful case for examining assumptions about military support, local conditions and contingencies. Failure alone, however, does not identify which belief was unjustifiably confident; a full assessment requires comparing the plan’s assumptions with the information available before the landing.

The recurring lesson is to expose pivotal assumptions, transmit dissent and examine downside scenarios before acting. These examples do not imply that caution guarantees success or that every unsuccessful decision was unreasonable.
