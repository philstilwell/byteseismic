Prompt 1: Once the rational demand for evidential substantiation has been removed or diminished, configuring and defending an internally logical ideology from whole cloth becomes much easier. Explain how this works using Carl Sagan’s The Dragon in my Garage.

How additions can insulate a claim

ChatGPT response · editorial edition

Carl Sagan’s parable of “The Dragon in My Garage” from his book “The Demon-Haunted World: Science as a Candle in the Dark” illustrates the critical role of evidential substantiation in distinguishing between what is real and what is merely an unfounded belief. The scenario described helps explain how an internally logical ideology can be easily constructed and defended once the demand for rational evidence is removed or diminished.

The Process of Constructing an Internally Logical Ideology:

  1. Assertion of Existence:
  • The initial claim introduces the existence of something extraordinary—in this case, a fire-breathing dragon living in a garage. This claim sets the stage for building an ideology around the dragon.
  1. Removal of Empirical Checks:
  • Seeing nothing prompts invisibility; flour cannot catch footprints if the dragon floats; a heat detector fails if its fire gives off no heat. Each proposed test loses its force through a newly supplied property. The problem is not invisibility alone: it is the systematic removal of observable consequences.
  1. Application of Special Explanations:
  • Paint is defeated by declaring the dragon incorporeal. An auxiliary explanation can be legitimate when independently supported, but here each addition merely rescues the claim from the test just proposed. No new reason for believing the addition is supplied.
  1. Creating a Closed Ideological System:
  • A defender can try to keep these additions mutually consistent while insulating the central claim. That makes coherence easier to arrange; it does not guarantee coherence or truth. The inability to disprove the resulting story supplies no positive evidence for its inhabitants.
  1. Invoking Wonder and Belief Without Evidence:
  • Wonder may explain why the story matters to someone, but it does not establish a creature in a garage. Philosophical argument and emotional meaning have legitimate roles; neither should quietly replace the evidence originally promised for a claim about the world.
  1. Expanding the Ideology:
  • The claim might grow to suggest a broader phenomenon (invisible dragons in many garages) based on collective assertions from multiple individuals. This communal reinforcement can lend undue credence to the baseless claims.

Implications of Removing Rational Demand for Evidence:

  • Erosion of empirical foundations: A claim that excludes every contrary observation cannot earn empirical support simply by surviving challenges. It remains open to logical and conceptual criticism; escaping a physical test does not escape all rational assessment.
  • Entrenchment of Beliefs: Beliefs can become deeply entrenched when they are not subject to empirical checks. People may begin to identify emotionally and culturally with these beliefs, making them part of personal or group identity.
  • Spread of Misinformation: In the absence of a requirement for proof, misinformation can spread unchecked. This can foster a culture where myths and superstitions overshadow scientific understanding.
  • Challenge in Debunking: Debunking such ideologies becomes increasingly difficult as they are designed to be unfalsifiable. The more flexible and tautological the explanations, the harder it is to effectively challenge them.

The curator’s point is therefore about the freedom to construct: remove the obligation to justify additions, and a coherent story becomes much easier to assemble and defend. Whether that story describes reality is a separate question. Insulation blocks a route to justification; it does not by itself demonstrate deliberate deception or establish that every component is false.

Gemini response · editorial edition

Here’s how ideologies can expand with less need for evidence, using Carl Sagan’s “Dragon in My Garage” analogy:

The Freedom to Embellish: The Garage Grows

Carl Sagan’s “Dragon in My Garage” highlights how ideologies thrive when the pressure for evidence weakens. Here’s how this allows for the unconstrained addition of fantastical elements to a fabricated ideology:

  • The Expanding Explanations: Imagine the “invisible dragon” scenario. Every attempt to find the dragon is met with a new excuse – it floats, its fire is heatless, etc. This creates a pattern: the ideology can endlessly adapt to explain away any potential challenge.
  • Ontological Buffet: This lack of evidentiary constraints opens the door to an “ontological buffet.” Just like adding features to the invisible dragon (floating, heatless fire), new entities and qualities can be freely incorporated into the ideology.
  • Introducing a “dragon whisperer”: As an extension of the analogy in this response, imagine a person who claims privileged access to the dragon. The person’s existence may be ordinary; the unsupported addition is the special ability. What would distinguish accurate reports from invention? This character is not part of Sagan’s original garage exchange.
  • The Expanding Lore: The ideology can develop a whole backstory for the dragons. Where do they come from? Why are they invisible? Entire narratives can be woven without needing evidence, enriching the ideology and making it more elaborate.
  • Immunity to empirical counterarguments: If every contrary result receives a new exemption, observation loses its ability to discriminate between the story and its rivals. An appearance of coherence can remain, but contradictions may still be exposed.

The Peril of Embellishment:

While these embellishments might seem attractive, they create significant problems:

  • Loss of grounding: Elaborateness is not the problem by itself. The problem is increasing detail without increasing evidence: a richer description is being mistaken for a better-supported account.
  • Echo Chamber Mentality: Believers become convinced of their ideology’s truth due to its internal consistency and elaborate explanations. This can lead to dismissal of any evidence that contradicts the embellished ideology.
  • Erosion of trust: Repeated evasions give outsiders reason to distrust the explanation, even while committed insiders may become more attached to it.

Sagan’s Message:

Sagan’s practical lesson is to remain open to evidence without treating an unrefuted story as established. His garage exchange contrasts failed tests with an imagined case in which detectable traces really appear. See The Demon-Haunted World, chapter 10, pp. 160–162. Prompt 2: What actual ideologies or mythologies employ this same ontological buffet modus operandi?

Examples and the limits of the comparison

ChatGPT response · editorial edition

The following are real families of beliefs, but belonging to one is not evidence that a particular believer fabricated anything. The relevant test concerns a specific defense: does it add unsupported entities or exemptions to absorb contrary evidence? Some original examples show that pattern more clearly than others.

1: Religious and Spiritual Claims:

    • Creationism and intelligent design: These are distinct positions, and particular claims can be tested. A narrower example is mature creation: Jerry Bergman’s defense invokes created maturity to reconcile apparent age with Genesis. My criticism is conditional: if any apparent history may be attributed to initial creation without independent constraints, observations cease to discriminate between histories. That criticism does not establish that every creationist or design argument uses this maneuver.
    • Spiritual and paranormal phenomena: A claim that a spirit moves an object has observable consequences even if the proposed cause is nonphysical. It becomes insulated when failures count as the spirit’s refusal to cooperate while successes count as confirmation, with no independent way to test the refusal explanation.

    2: Conspiracy Theories:

      • Moon-landing-hoax claims: The insulating version dismisses every photograph, sample or independent observation as another product of the alleged conspiracy. Evidence of fabrication would be relevant; merely adding fabricators whenever contrary evidence appears is the buffet mechanism. This diagnoses a form of argument, not every person’s reasons for doubting a landing.
      • Flat-Earth claims: Claims about Earth’s shape have testable consequences. An evasive defense adds coordinated deception whenever measurements conflict with its account, instead of offering a model that consistently explains the measurements. The test is whether the proposed deception has support beyond its usefulness in rescuing the claim.

      3: Historical Mythologies:

        • Ancient myths and legends: A phoenix story offered as symbolism need not pass a zoological test. The relevant evasion occurs when a narrator asserts literal history, cites evidence for it, then retreats to allegory only when that evidence fails while retaining the original factual conclusion. Mythmaking alone does not demonstrate this sequence.
        • Loch Ness Monster and Bigfoot: An undiscovered animal is a testable possibility; rarity can genuinely make detection difficult. The buffet comparison applies only when new evasive powers or cover-ups are invoked without support to neutralize every unsuccessful search. Absence of detection matters in proportion to how likely the search was to detect the proposed animal.

        4: Alternative Medicine:

          • Homeopathy: Clinical benefit can be tested even when a proposed mechanism is disputed. NCCIH’s evidence overview describes substantial evidential and theoretical problems. The buffet maneuver would be to invoke an undetectable energy as a reason that unfavorable outcome tests cannot count. An unfamiliar mechanism alone is not that maneuver, and the cited overview does not establish that every practitioner uses it.

          5: Ideological Constructs in Politics and Culture:

            • “Cultural Marxism” conspiracy narratives: Claims about particular scholars or institutions can be investigated. A sweeping narrative becomes insulated if all cultural change confirms a hidden coordinated plot and all contrary findings become evidence of concealment. This criticism targets that self-protecting argument, not cultural criticism or the study of Marxist ideas as such.

            These comparisons differ in strength. Mature creation provides a named published defense to examine; several other entries describe conditional patterns rather than documented episodes of a speaker changing a claim. None establishes fabrication merely from a label. The shared danger is an addition whose only credential is that it protects the conclusion.

            Gemini response · editorial edition

            The original examples can illustrate different ways to add entities and powers, provided we distinguish a rich imagined world from an account offered as factual knowledge:

            • Creation myths: Divine powers can supply an account of origins without independently establishing it. As symbolic narratives, such stories need not be attempts at empirical explanation. As literal accounts, their added powers need justification; flexibility alone does not supply it.
            • Conspiracy theories: Secret cooperation is possible and can be evidenced. The dangerous version adds an otherwise unsupported agent or technology each time a difficulty arises, then treats the theory’s ability to accommodate everything as confirmation.
            • Cryptozoology: Searching for an unrecorded animal is not intrinsically evasive. Repeatedly inventing new reasons why no adequate search could find it is different. A useful proposal specifies habitat, traces and search conditions that could change confidence.
            • Religious eschatology: Detailed accounts of souls, judgment and an afterlife illustrate how much can be specified beyond available observation. That evidential gap does not itself prove invention. The buffet objection is strongest when new details are introduced to defeat objections without an independent reason to accept them.
            • Astrology and alchemy: These broad labels should not erase differences between predictions, symbolic practices and historical experiments. A concrete prediction can fail a test. Invoking a previously unspecified influence solely to excuse failure is the relevant maneuver; merely investigating an unknown influence is not.

            The distinction is between proposing possibilities and claiming warranted belief. An imaginative explanation may begin an inquiry, but its details must eventually constrain expectations or gain other relevant support. These examples are analytical comparisons, not evidence that whole traditions share one origin or motive.

            Prompt 3: Explain the asymmetry between the rhetorical limits on those who employ the ontological buffet and those constrained by proper standards of evidence.

            Cheap invention and the cost of checking

            ChatGPT response · editorial edition

            The asymmetry between those who employ the “ontological buffet” approach—effectively crafting an unfalsifiable belief system by continuously adjusting its tenets to avoid empirical refutation—and those adhering to proper scientific standards of evidence, is substantial and significant in both philosophical and practical terms. This disparity profoundly affects discourse, debate, and the acceptance of ideas within society.

            Rhetorical Limits of the Ontological Buffet Approach

            1: Freedom from Empirical Constraints:

              • Flexibility in Argumentation: Proponents of ideologies that utilize the ontological buffet are not limited by the need to provide empirical evidence. They can adapt their arguments to any counter-evidence or inquiry, often resorting to claims that lie outside the realm of observational science (e.g., supernatural or metaphysical claims).
              • Repeated adjustability: New exemptions can defeat successive tests, as with a floating dragon and heatless fire. This protects the claim rhetorically while reducing what it predicts; it does not turn each rescue into evidence.

              2: Strategic Ambiguity:

                • Vague Assertions: Claims are often made in sufficiently vague terms that they cannot be directly tested or refuted. This vagueness serves as a rhetorical shield against critical scrutiny.
                • Shifting Goalposts: Proponents can change the criteria or conditions required for proving the claim whenever their positions are challenged, a tactic known as shifting the goalposts.

                Rhetorical Limits of Those Constrained by Evidence

                1: Empirical Rigor:

                  • Requirement for evidence: Responsible claims need support proportionate to their content and confidence. Repeatable experiments are one source; independently checkable records and converging traces also matter for events that cannot be rerun.
                  • Exposure to correction: Empirical claims must allow observations to count against them. A failed prediction may challenge the claim, its supporting assumptions or the measurement. The task is to investigate which failed, not to protect the central claim by automatic exemption.

                  2: Consistency and Coherence:

                    • Logical consistency: An account must avoid contradiction in its own commitments. It can challenge existing science, but it then needs to explain the evidence that made the displaced account credible. Agreement with accepted theory is not a veto on discovery.
                    • Predictive Power: Scientific theories are expected to provide predictions that can be tested and potentially falsified. Lack of predictive power diminishes the credibility of a scientific theory.

                    Practical and Discursive Consequences

                    • Debate Dynamics: In debates, those employing the ontological buffet approach have a rhetorical advantage because they can always manufacture a way out of objections, while scientific skeptics are limited to arguing within the confines of empirical evidence. This can make scientific skepticism appear weaker or less imaginative, especially in public forums where intricate understanding of evidence and methodology is lacking.
                    • Public Perception and Policy Influence: The apparent certainty and simplicity offered by unfalsifiable ideologies can be more appealing to the public than the conditional, probabilistic statements typical of scientific findings. This can influence public opinion and policy in ways that favor unscientific views.
                    • Educational and Cultural Impact: The spread of ontologically unfalsifiable ideologies can undermine public understanding of science and the acceptance of scientific consensus on issues like climate change, vaccine efficacy, and evolution. This erosion of empirical standards can have broad societal consequences, affecting education, healthcare, and governance.

                    The asymmetry is a difference in the cost of answering objections: an unsupported rescue can be invented quickly, while checking it takes work. A useful response is to hold one claim fixed, ask what would lower confidence in it, and require support for each added mechanism before pursuing an endless series of new claims.

                    Gemini response · editorial edition

                    There’s a significant asymmetry between those who employ the “ontological buffet” approach and those who rely on proper standards of evidence. Here’s why:

                    Freedom for the Buffet Builder:

                    • Cheap explanations: A new power or hidden agent can be proposed in seconds. Our added “dragon whisperer” illustrates this extension of Sagan’s scenario; it is not his character. Describing how such an agent could rescue the story does not establish that the agent has that power.
                    • Attempted burden shifting: The claimant may demand that a skeptic disprove every new explanation. That is rhetorical pressure, not a legitimate transfer of responsibility: the person asserting the entities or powers still owes reasons to accept them. A critic who makes a separate positive claim must support that claim too.
                    • Weak pressure to maintain consistency: Evidence avoidance does not logically require contradiction. A buffet account may be internally consistent, as the curator’s first prompt allows. But an audience satisfied by a story may overlook contradictions, so consistency and evidential support require separate checks.

                    Constraints on the Evidence-Based Approach:

                    • Limits of disproof: Some negative claims are readily established under specified conditions. What is difficult is ruling out an entity whose properties change after every test. Withholding belief does not require disproving every imaginable version.
                    • The Need for Rigorous Testing: Evidence-based explanations must undergo rigorous testing and scrutiny. This can be a slow and meticulous process, often failing to produce quick and definitive answers. This can seem less compelling compared to the seemingly all-encompassing explanations offered by the ontological buffet.
                    • The Challenge of Public Understanding: Scientific concepts can be complex and nuanced. Communicating them effectively to the public can be challenging. This can leave space for the “buffet builder’s” simplistic explanations, even if they lack factual grounding, to gain traction.

                    The Resulting Imbalance:

                    This creates a practical imbalance: invention is cheap and checking is costly. A defender may sound certain without having met the evidential burden. Critics can refuse an endless chase by asking for independent support for the new explanation before spending more time testing it.

                    It’s important to note:

                    • Critical thinking skills and scientific literacy can help us recognize the limitations of the ontological buffet approach.
                    • Demanding evidence and evaluating the internal consistency of explanations are crucial tools for evaluating ideologies.

                    By understanding this asymmetry, we can be better equipped to challenge unfounded claims and advocate for the importance of evidence-based reasoning.

                    Prompt 4: What clues can warn us that an ideology has been fabricated though the ontological buffet process?

                    Warning signs require investigation

                    ChatGPT response · editorial edition

                    Recognizing when an ideology has been constructed through the “ontological buffet” process involves identifying certain key characteristics and patterns that signal the use of unfalsifiable assertions, vague premises, and shifting goalposts. These clues help differentiate between genuinely empirical, evidence-based thinking and ideologies crafted to resist refutation regardless of the evidence against them. Here are some signs to look out for:

                    1: Unfalsifiability:

                      • Untestable mechanisms: Invisibility is not enough: an unseen cause may leave measurable effects. The warning sign is a mechanism declared exempt from every relevant test while still invoked to explain observable events.
                      • Absence of Disproof Considered as Proof: The claim that there is no way to disprove the ideology is used as evidence of its validity, rather than recognizing the scientific principle that a claim should be falsifiable to be testable.

                      2: Shifting Goalposts:

                        • Ad Hoc Adjustments: Whenever the ideology is challenged by new evidence, its proponents modify the initial conditions or explanations to accommodate or dismiss the evidence. These adjustments are made post-hoc and are not based on any new empirical findings.
                        • Evolving Definitions: Key terms and definitions within the ideology change depending on the context or in response to criticism, making it difficult to pin down what exactly is being claimed.

                        3: Vagueness and Ambiguity:

                          • Lack of Specificity: The claims made are overly broad or vague, preventing clear understanding and testing. This vagueness acts as a rhetorical shield against criticism.
                          • Elastic explanations: Both a result and its opposite are presented as confirming the same claim. Metaphor is legitimate as metaphor; the evasion is switching between metaphorical and literal meanings to retain a factual conclusion without its evidential burden.

                          4: Appeal to Mystery and Complexity:

                            • Mystification: The ideology often appeals to the mysterious, invoking complexity or unknown phenomena as reasons why the ideology cannot be understood or examined by conventional science.
                            • Supernatural exemptions: Calling a cause supernatural does not erase the observable consequences of a claim that it changes the world. Ask which consequences are expected and whether their absence matters. Metaphysical language alone does not establish evasion.

                            5: Dismissal of Contradictory Evidence:

                              • Rejection of Mainstream Science: Proponents of the ideology systematically dismiss scientific evidence and consensus that contradicts their views, often accusing the scientific community of conspiracy or closed-mindedness.
                              • Selective Use of Data: Evidence that supports the ideology is exaggerated or taken out of context, while evidence against it is minimized, ignored, or discredited.

                              6: Circumstantial Evidence Touted as Conclusive:

                                • Anecdotal Emphasis: Reliance on anecdotal evidence and testimonies as the primary support for the ideology, while disregarding statistical or experimental data that provide a more comprehensive view.
                                • Correlation Equated with Causation: Drawing causal conclusions from mere correlations, often ignoring alternative explanations that are more plausible and better supported by evidence.

                                7: Resistance to Peer Review:

                                  • Insularity: The ideology is promoted within a closed community or echo chamber where critical review and open debate are absent. Proponents may avoid exposing their ideas to peer-reviewed contexts where they might be rigorously evaluated.

                                  8: Persecution Complex:

                                    • Martyrdom narrative: Suppression is possible and should be investigated when supported. But evidence that someone has been treated unfairly is not evidence that their theory is true. Using persecution to dismiss every criticism prevents that distinction from being examined.

                                    Treat these as reasons to investigate, not a checklist that proves fabrication or intent. Record the original claim, the troublesome observation, the added explanation and its independent support. A revision that yields a new successful test differs from one that only makes failure impossible to acknowledge.

                                    Gemini response · editorial edition

                                    Here are some clues that can warn us an ideology might be a product of the “ontological buffet” process, where fantastical elements are freely added without the need for evidence:

                                    Red Flags in Explanations:

                                    • Overreliance on Unfalsifiable Claims: The ideology centers around explanations that cannot be definitively disproven through observation or experimentation. This makes it difficult to challenge the core tenets, even if they seem improbable. (e.g., Beliefs about a hidden government controlling everything).
                                    • Constant Introduction of New Entities: The ideology readily introduces new fantastical entities or qualities to explain away any challenges. These additions (e.g., psychic abilities, secret technologies) often lack grounding in established knowledge or evidence.
                                    • Internal inconsistencies: A contradiction is a separate defect, even if a compelling narrative conceals it. Conversely, removing contradictions would not establish the existence of the proposed entities. Narrative unity, logical consistency and factual support are different achievements.

                                    Focus on Narrative over Evidence:

                                    • Emotional Appeals over Reason: The ideology relies heavily on emotional appeals, invoking fear, anger, or a sense of belonging, to win adherents. Critical thinking and the demand for evidence are discouraged.
                                    • Lack of Peer Review or Scrutiny: The core ideas are not subjected to rigorous peer review or scientific scrutiny. The ideology might exist in a closed loop, where information is primarily disseminated by believers, reinforcing existing biases.
                                    • Demonization of Skepticism: Critical thinking and questioning are seen as threats to the ideology. Skeptics might be ostracized or labeled as enemies, discouraging open discussion and examination of the core beliefs.

                                    Beware the Allure of Certainty:

                                    • Easy certainty about complex problems: Simplicity can be a virtue when a model explains evidence well. The warning sign is confidence that outruns support, combined with refusal to discuss omitted causes, limitations or failed predictions.
                                    • Us vs. Them Mentality: The ideology fosters a sense of in-group vs. out-group mentality. Believers see themselves as enlightened or chosen, while those who question the ideology are seen as ignorant or even malicious.
                                    • Resistance to Updating Beliefs: New evidence or contradictory information is dismissed or reinterpreted to fit the existing narrative. The ideology remains rigid and inflexible, even in the face of new discoveries.

                                    These signs warrant checking the claim’s history and evidence. They do not establish the claimant’s intentions. Ask for one clear prediction and a possible result that would change the account; then examine whether the response supplies a reason or merely another exemption.
