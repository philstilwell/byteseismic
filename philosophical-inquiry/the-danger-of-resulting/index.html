Annie Duke’s notion of resulting concerns judging a decision’s quality from its outcome. This conversation moves from examples to history, dating, investing and self-assessment. The original seven prompts, two response tracks and curator’s caution after the historical examples are restored. Outcomes remain evidence for learning; avoiding resulting does not excuse bad reasoning or require loyalty to a failed plan.

Highlights · edited summary

  • A favorable outcome cannot by itself show that a decision was sound.
  • Judge the earlier choice using the information reasonably available then; make the next choice using what is known now.

Prompt 1: Annie Duke introduces a concept called “resulting”. Provide a definition for and examples of “resulting”.

Judging decisions without letting one outcome decide

ChatGPT response · editorial edition

“Resulting,” as popularized by Annie Duke, means equating a decision’s quality with the quality of its outcome. A lucky win can disguise a poor choice, and an unlucky loss can follow a reasonable one. Duke makes the distinction in her own account; the examples below are hypothetical illustrations, not quotations from her.

Definition of Resulting

Resulting is the cognitive bias that occurs when people equate the quality of a decision with the quality of its outcome. This bias leads to a misunderstanding of the relationship between outcomes and decision-making, often causing people to overvalue their success or failure as a direct reflection of their decision-making abilities. Essentially, it suggests that good outcomes always stem from good decisions and bad outcomes from bad decisions, which isn’t necessarily true.

Examples of Resulting

  1. Poker Game Decisions:
  • In a hypothetical poker hand, suppose a player estimates a 75% chance of winning. Losing does not by itself show that going all in was mistaken. But the percentage alone does not prove the choice was sound either: the estimate, payoff, alternatives, amount at risk and consequences of losing all matter. Resulting skips that assessment and lets this one result settle the verdict.
  1. Sports Coaching Decisions:
  • A football coach chooses a two-point attempt that would win the game instead of a one-point kick that could lead to overtime. Suppose the estimates favor the attempt after accounting for both conversion and overtime chances. Condemning it solely because it fails is resulting; criticism of an unreasonable estimate or ignored game condition would be a different matter.
  1. Business Decisions:
  • A business invests in a new technology after comparing alternatives and estimating downside risks. An unforeseen downturn then causes a loss. Saying “it lost money, so the decision was bad” bypasses the earlier evidence. Yet calling every loss bad luck is equally evasive: the review must check whether the business ignored warning signs or risked more than it could afford.
  1. Medical Decisions:
  • Suppose a clinician and patient choose an option after considering the available evidence, alternatives, risks and the patient’s preferences. An adverse outcome alone does not establish that the choice was wrong. The outcome may still reveal an error or new information that warrants review and a change in care. This illustrates the distinction; it does not assess any particular treatment.

Resulting can encourage excessive caution after an unlucky loss or excessive confidence after a lucky win. The remedy is to compare the decision with feasible alternatives using the evidence available then, while using subsequent outcomes to improve future judgments.

Gemini response · editorial edition

In Annie Duke’s usage, “resulting” is treating outcome quality as if it settled decision quality. Outcomes matter, but one outcome is not a complete evaluation of the reasoning, alternatives and risks at the time.

Here are some examples of “resulting”:

  • Investment decision: You invest in a company that does well and see it as a good decision, but you might have made the same decision based on bad information and just gotten lucky.
  • Sports game: A coach calls a poorly justified play, it succeeds through an opponent’s unexpected error, and observers call the strategy brilliant solely because the team won. The success does not repair the weak reasoning that preceded it.
  • Daily life: You carry a light umbrella because rain is plausible and being soaked would be costly. Sunshine does not retroactively make the precaution foolish. Its reasonableness depends on the forecast and the burden of carrying it; the result alone does not decide.

Prompt 2: Provide 3 examples from history of “resulting”.

Three historical illustrations and the curator’s caution

ChatGPT response · editorial edition

The three original cases are retained, but a distinction is needed: a bad historical outcome is not itself evidence that later observers committed resulting. Each case below states how outcome-only judgment would go wrong and what a fuller assessment needs. These are applications to historical events, not documented diagnoses of particular historians’ reasoning.

1. The Bay of Pigs Invasion (1961)

Event: In 1961, the U.S. government, under President John F. Kennedy, sponsored an invasion of Cuba by Cuban exiles intending to overthrow Fidel Castro. The plan failed disastrously, with most of the invaders captured or killed within days.

Resulting test: “The invasion failed, therefore every prior decision was irrational” would be an outcome-only inference. But reliance on an intelligence agency does not establish sound reasoning either. The JFK Library’s account identifies dependence on a popular uprising and difficulties with the landing site and air support. Those are substantive planning questions. Avoiding resulting requires examining what officials could reasonably know about them, not excusing the plan because it had a Cold War rationale.

2. The Charge of the Light Brigade (1854)

Event: During the Battle of Balaclava in the Crimean War, British cavalry, known as the Light Brigade, was mistakenly sent into a frontal assault against a well-entrenched Russian artillery. The charge resulted in high British casualties.

Resulting test: Heavy losses alone do not identify who made which mistake. The surviving order and museum account document a misunderstood direction of attack. Review what commanders could see, how the message was conveyed and which ambiguities could have been resolved. Confusion may explain an error without making the process sound. A lucky escape would not have made an unclear order clear.

3. The Treaty of Versailles (1919)

Event: The 1919 Treaty of Versailles set peace terms with Germany, including territorial changes, military restrictions and reparations. It was one part of the postwar settlement, not a treaty with every defeated power. The U.S. Office of the Historian describes competing Allied priorities and the treaty’s contested reception.

Resulting test: “Another world war followed, so every term was obviously wrong in 1919” would substitute hindsight for analysis. Conversely, a stated desire for peace does not establish that the terms were prudent. Assess contemporary warnings, feasible alternatives and the settlement’s likely effects. The later Nazi rise cannot be attributed to the treaty alone, nor can it be dismissed as irrelevant to evaluating the settlement’s consequences.

These cases illustrate a discipline of judgment, not a blanket defense of unsuccessful decisions. Outcome evidence, causal analysis and scrutiny of the original process belong together. A historical label such as “disaster” does not perform that analysis for us.

  1. The Fall of the Western Roman Empire: Its eventual collapse cannot show that every earlier imperial decision was foolish. To assess a specific decision, identify the ruler, date, options and evidence then available. The broad label “fall of Rome” is too large to supply those details. This original example therefore illustrates a possible hindsight error rather than establishing that any particular emperor made the best available choice.
  2. The American Civil War: Confederate defeat does not, by itself, establish the quality of every strategic forecast made before secession. But sincere belief in success is not good reasoning, and explaining motives is not a moral defense. Slavery must not be hidden behind generic “economic interests”: Mississippi’s 1861 declaration explicitly identifies its preservation as central. Evaluate strategic expectations from contemporary evidence and the project’s ethics separately; neither question is settled solely by the eventual defeat.
  3. The Voyages of Christopher Columbus: An outcome-only judgment would call the voyage well reasoned simply because land was reached, without examining the geographic assumptions, alternatives and risks that informed it. A fuller account must also consider harms to Indigenous peoples. Those harms matter to ethical and causal assessment, but merely adding bad consequences to good ones is not yet the analysis of decision quality that Duke’s distinction requires.

Prompt 3: How can I apply Annie Duke’s notion of “resulting” to my dating life?

Dating: fair review and new information

ChatGPT response · editorial edition

Apply the distinction to two different questions: was the earlier choice reasonable, and what should you do now? A relationship ending does not automatically answer the first. New evidence about compatibility, conduct or safety may answer the second even when the original decision was reasonable.

1. Evaluate the Earlier Decision Using the Earlier Evidence

Explanation: Shared interests and values can be reasons to accept a date, but they do not guarantee compatibility or establish that every warning sign was considered. A relationship’s length is also an imperfect measure of its quality.

Application: After a breakup, ask what you knew, what you could reasonably have checked, and whether you respected your own and the other person’s boundaries. A sensible initial choice can lead to a relationship you should now leave; a lasting relationship can begin with a poorly considered choice.

2. Recognize the Role of Luck and Circumstance

Explanation: Many uncontrollable factors influence dating outcomes, including timing, external stressors (like job loss or family issues), and the other person’s emotional state or readiness for a relationship.

Application: Consider timing and circumstances without turning them into automatic excuses. You cannot control whether someone reciprocates interest. You can examine whether you communicated honestly, respected consent and responded appropriately to information you actually had.

3. Avoid Overcorrection Based on Outcomes

Explanation: One disappointing date does not establish that your whole approach is defective. However, one instance of coercion, deception or a serious boundary violation can be decisive new evidence. Avoiding resulting is not a rule to wait for repeated harm.

Application: Identify what the outcome revealed. “We did not want the same kind of relationship” may support a targeted change in how you discuss intentions. A serious breach of trust may justify ending contact. Neither requires condemning yourself for facts you could not reasonably have known.

4. Develop a Process for Decision Making

Explanation: Clarifying your values, boundaries and relationship goals makes later review more specific. Treat these as guides for mutual exploration, not a scorecard that turns another person into a predictable payoff.

Application: Decide which expectations need early discussion and which qualities can only become clear over time. Revise your criteria when experience exposes a poor assumption. Following a checklist is not sufficient if the checklist itself is weak.

5. Learn from Each Experience

Explanation: While you shouldn’t judge a decision solely by its outcome, every outcome still offers valuable data. Analyzing what went right and what went wrong can refine your decision-making process.

Application: Review preferences, boundaries and approaches to conflict. Separate “I ignored evidence” from “I learned something unavailable earlier.” Include positive experiences in this review too: success can conceal poor communication just as disappointment can accompany respectful choices.

Conclusion

Fair review allows learning without converting every disappointment into self-blame. It also allows changing course now without declaring that every earlier choice was a mistake.

Gemini response · editorial edition

Here’s how you can apply Annie Duke’s concept of “resulting” to your dating life:

1. Evaluating Past Dates:

  • Focus on the process as well as the outcome: Did you choose to meet on reasonable information, communicate respectfully and honor boundaries? Enjoyable conversation is an experience to value, but it does not prove that all the earlier reasoning was sound. A second date is neither owed nor a complete score for the first.
  • Learn from both successful and unsuccessful dates: Even if a date didn’t lead to a relationship, did you learn something new about yourself or what you’re looking for? Consider the process as a learning experience.

2. Making Dating Decisions:

  • Don’t chase sunk costs: Just because you’ve invested time in someone, doesn’t mean you have to continue if they’re not a good fit. Evaluate each interaction based on current information, not just the initial attraction.
  • Examine your criteria: Attraction, compatibility and shared values can all matter. Ask whether your actual choices fit your goals and whether you have ignored contrary evidence. Excitement is not inherently irrational, and a familiar checklist is not inherently wise.

3. Managing Expectations:

  • Understand the inherent uncertainty: Dating involves chance encounters and unpredictable outcomes. Don’t get discouraged by setbacks. Focus on making good decisions based on the information available.
  • Recognize good choices: You can value being honest and respecting a refusal even when no relationship follows. That assessment concerns how you acted, not whether the date delivered a desired outcome.

Use the distinction to review choices fairly and make the next choice on current information. You can want a lasting relationship while recognizing that its arrival is not fully under your control.

Prompt 4: How can I apply Annie Duke’s notion of “resulting” to my investment portfolio?

Portfolio review: process, evidence and risk

ChatGPT response · editorial edition

For a portfolio, separate review of the earlier decision from review of the current position. Record the original reasons and risks, then ask what new information changes today’s choice. This is a general decision-review framework, not a recommendation about particular holdings or a promise of better returns.

1. Evaluate Investment Decisions on Their Own Merits

Explanation: Just as with poker decisions, investment decisions should be evaluated based on the information and rationale at the time the decision was made, not solely on the outcome.

Application: Compare the initial choice with realistic alternatives using the information available then. Consider costs, concentration, time horizon, ability to bear losses and the quality of the evidence. “I researched it” is not enough if the research ignored adverse information or unsuitable risk.

2. Understand the Role of Luck and External Factors

Explanation: The markets are influenced by numerous external factors such as economic changes, political events, and global incidents, which can affect investment outcomes.

Application: A market shock may explain a loss without showing that it was reasonably unforeseeable or that the exposure was suitable. Ask which risks were recognized, how large a loss was tolerable and what new facts emerged. “Bad luck” should be a conclusion to examine, not a default excuse.

3. Avoid Outcome Bias in Future Decisions

Explanation: Outcome bias occurs when a decision is judged based on its result rather than how it was made. This can lead to overcorrection or inappropriate adjustments in strategy.

Application: Poor performance alone does not dictate a sale, but neither does an unchanged personal conviction justify holding. Reassess evidence, valuation, costs, alternatives and your own needs. A decision that made sense earlier can require a different action now.

4. Develop a Robust Decision-Making Framework

Explanation: A structured decision-making process can provide consistency and reduce the emotional impact of market fluctuations on your decisions.

Application: Write down the purpose of the investment, relevant uncertainties, acceptable exposure and conditions for review. The SEC’s investor education material explains why time horizon, risk tolerance and diversification matter. Rules need revision when the facts or your circumstances change; consistency alone is not soundness.

5. Learn from Outcomes to Refine Your Process

Explanation: While it’s important not to confuse the quality of a decision with its outcome, outcomes still provide valuable information that can be used to refine your decision-making process.

Application: Review successes as well as losses against an appropriate comparison, including costs and the risks taken. Several holdings can rise together because of one shared exposure, so their gains need not be independent confirmations of skill. Use a sufficiently informative record while remaining alert to a single decisive fact such as fraud.

6. Maintain Emotional Equanimity

Explanation: Emotional reactions to winning and losing can cloud judgment and lead to poor investment decisions.

Application: Choose a review schedule and explicit triggers that fit the investment and your needs. A pause can prevent impulsive action, but a calendar should not delay attention to a serious new fact, a changed cash need or an unsuitable exposure.

Conclusion

The aim is accountable reasoning: explain the choice, test its assumptions and revise it when warranted. Neither a profit nor a loss by itself settles whether the original process was sound, and a careful process still cannot eliminate investment risk.

Gemini response · editorial edition

Use Duke’s distinction to improve how you review a portfolio decision. It does not supply an asset selection rule or guarantee returns.

1. Evaluating Past Performance:

  • Don’t judge solely on gains or losses: Past performance is not necessarily indicative of future results. Focus on the process used to make investment decisions. Did you follow your investment plan and conduct thorough research? Did you diversify your portfolio appropriately for your risk tolerance?
  • Learn from successes and failures: Compare what you expected with what happened and ask which assumptions were supported or challenged. Skill, market exposure and chance can operate together; one gain or loss rarely lets you assign exact shares to each.

2. Making Investment Decisions:

  • Don’t chase past winners: Just because an investment performed well historically doesn’t guarantee future success. Evaluate each investment opportunity based on current market conditions and your investment goals.
  • Review process and risk management: Check whether the allocation was suitable for the time horizon, foreseeable needs and ability to bear losses. Then check whether those conditions still hold. Keeping a plan is valuable only while the plan remains defensible.

3. Managing Expectations and Discipline:

  • Understand uncertainty: Short-term prices are uncertain. That makes a single fluctuation an unreliable verdict on skill, but it does not make every risk unknowable or every loss irrelevant.
  • Recognize sound reasoning: Give credit for comparing alternatives, accounting for costs and limiting an unsuitable exposure, whether the next price move is favorable or unfavorable. A gain after extensive research does not alone validate the research.
  • Avoid automatic reactions: Neither “sell because it fell” nor “hold because I had a plan” is an adequate rule. Reassess the facts and your circumstances; if rebalancing is warranted, consider transaction costs and tax effects rather than assuming that every adjustment is free.

Judge the earlier process using earlier evidence, and decide what to do next using current evidence. Outcomes contribute to that learning. The SEC’s discussion of fund performance also explains why a past winner is not a guarantee of future success.

Prompt 5: How might we identify when we have become susceptible to “resulting”?

Recognizing outcome-driven judgments

ChatGPT response · editorial edition

The clearest sign is a verdict that changes solely because you learn the outcome, with no new information about the original reasoning. The following seven indicators can prompt a review; they are not a diagnostic test. Feeling disappointment or celebrating success is not itself resulting.

1. Overemphasis on Recent Outcomes

  • Indicator: You call a choice wise after a win and foolish after a loss without examining what was known or reasonably knowable when it was made.
  • Management Strategy: Record reasons, alternatives and expectations before the result. Later, assess the process first, ideally with the outcome concealed from an independent reviewer, then examine what the result teaches. Waiting longer without such a record does not prevent hindsight.

2. Post-Outcome Rationalization

  • Indicator: Rationalizing decisions strictly based on how they turned out, rather than the validity of the decision-making process itself. This often manifests as “I was right because I won” or “I was wrong because I lost.”
  • Management Strategy: State plausible outcomes and their estimated chances before deciding. Afterwards, compare the result with that range. One unlikely event can occur without invalidating the estimate; repeated or highly diagnostic surprises may require revision.

3. Emotional Decision-Making

  • Indicator: The feeling produced by a win or loss becomes your entire argument about whether the decision was justified. The emotion itself is not the error.
  • Management Strategy: Pause long enough to state the evidence for a change. Use written review criteria that include new information and changed needs, rather than rules that protect the old decision from criticism.

4. Pattern Recognition Errors

  • Indicator: Misinterpreting random events as patterns (e.g., seeing patterns in losses or wins and basing decisions on these misconceptions).
  • Management Strategy: Compare observed patterns with what chance and shared conditions could produce. Check the size and independence of the sample and whether you noticed the pattern only after searching many possibilities.

5. Short-Term Focus

  • Indicator: Concentrating on immediate results rather than long-term outcomes. This short-term focus can skew your perception of what constitutes a ‘successful’ decision.
  • Management Strategy: Align review with the actual objective and time horizon, then evaluate reasoning and outcomes separately. A long-term success can be lucky too; changing the time horizon alone does not remove resulting.

6. Lack of Robust Review Processes

  • Indicator: Not having a consistent method for reviewing and evaluating decisions.
  • Management Strategy: Use a repeatable review with two stages: reconstruct the earlier choice and alternatives, then assess new evidence from outcomes. Preserve records of both favorable and unfavorable results.

7. Echo Chambers and Confirmation Bias

  • Indicator: Surrounding yourself with opinions or data that reinforce the outcomes rather than challenge the process.
  • Management Strategy: Invite someone to identify an assumption that could be wrong. Ask whether their verdict on the earlier process would survive a different outcome generated by the same information. Criticism of a new fact is legitimate; criticism based only on the outcome label is the concern.

Conclusion

A useful self-check is: “Would I praise or condemn this same reasoning if the luck had gone the other way?” Follow it with: “What did the actual result teach me?” Together these questions resist both outcome worship and the refusal to learn.

Gemini response · editorial edition

These signs can indicate susceptibility when they lead you to infer decision quality solely from outcomes. Ordinary regret, grief or satisfaction does not establish a reasoning bias.

Emotional Reactions:

  • Excessive focus on outcomes: Do you find yourself dwelling on the results of decisions, even if you made well-informed choices at the time?
  • Discouragement treated as proof: Do you infer that your earlier choice was foolish merely because you feel disappointed, without inspecting the evidence available then?
  • Celebrating only success: Do you only feel good about your decisions when they lead to positive outcomes?

Decision-Making Biases:

  • Hindsight bias: Do you now treat the outcome as having been inevitable? This differs from resulting: hindsight changes your estimate of what was foreseeable, while resulting treats outcome quality as decision quality. They can reinforce one another.
  • Confirmation bias: Do you tend to seek out information that confirms your existing beliefs about a decision, even if there’s contradictory evidence?
  • Regret aversion: Do you reject a well-supported option solely to avoid feeling responsible for a possible loss? This is related to emotion and anticipated regret, but is not identical to resulting.

Focus on Control:

  • Defending an earlier choice: Do you cite a favorable result as your entire justification? Explaining a choice from its original evidence can be appropriate, even after failure.
  • Replaying events: Do you compare the actual outcome with an imagined alternative while assuming the alternative would certainly have succeeded? Repeated reflection is not itself resulting; the unsupported counterfactual certainty is the concern.
  • Calling every setback a mistake: Have you shown a flaw in the choice, or simply renamed an unwanted outcome? A genuine mistake still deserves correction.

If you recognize some of these signs in yourself, here are some tips to overcome your susceptibility to “resulting”:

  • Focus on the process: Actively remind yourself to analyze the quality of your decisions based on the information available at the time, not just the outcome.
  • Practice self-compassion: Accept that mistakes are inevitable and view them as learning opportunities.
  • Celebrate good choices: Acknowledge the times you made well-informed decisions, even if the outcome wasn’t ideal.
  • Embrace uncertainty: Accept that many situations have unpredictable elements, and focus on what you can control – your decision-making process.
  • Seek external perspective: Discuss your decision-making with a trusted friend or mentor to gain a different perspective.
  • Pause before reviewing: A brief pause may help you notice an emotional verdict. Then write down what was known and what changed. The pause is a practical aid, not an established cure for outcome bias.

Use these prompts to inspect a particular judgment, not to label your personality. A fair assessment can acknowledge disappointment, responsibility and uncertainty at the same time.
