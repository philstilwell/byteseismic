Prompt 1: What gradients of philosophical thought can we assesses against particular philosophers?

Nine areas for comparing philosophical commitments

ChatGPT response · editorial edition

Gradients of Philosophical Thought

A gradient orders positions by a stated feature, such as how much independent justificatory weight a theory gives to experience. A subject area or list of schools is not yet such an ordering. The nine areas below preserve the original map while specifying questions from which useful comparisons can be built. Some disagreements require separate categories rather than intermediate points.

1. Metaphysical Gradients

Metaphysics asks what exists and how reality is structured. Compare commitments within a specified domain: whether minds depend on physical processes, for instance, is different from how many kinds of fundamental entity a theory admits.

  • Ontology: What entities exist? How are they categorized?
  • Cosmology: What is the origin and structure of the universe?
  • Theology: What is the nature and existence of God or gods?
2. Epistemological Gradients

Epistemology asks what counts as knowledge and what supports belief. Separate the origin of a concept from the justification of a claim: learning a concept through experience does not settle whether a particular inference requires new observation.

  • Rationalism vs. Empiricism: Is knowledge primarily derived from reason or sensory experience?
  • Skepticism: What are the limits of human knowledge?
  • Justification: How are beliefs justified?
3. Ethical Gradients

Ethical theories differ over duties, consequences, and character. These can overlap. A possible gradient concerns the priority given to outcomes when they conflict with a specified constraint; virtue ethics is not simply a midpoint between the other two.

  • Deontology: What are the duties and rules governing moral actions?
  • Consequentialism: What are the consequences of actions, and how do they determine morality?
  • Virtue Ethics: What are the character traits that constitute a good life?
4. Political Gradients

Political comparisons need a named institution or decision. A thinker can favor wide personal liberty while endorsing extensive collective provision; neither position determines the other.

  • Liberty: What is the nature and scope of individual freedom?
  • Justice: What constitutes a fair and just society?
  • Authority: What is the source and justification of political power?
5. Aesthetic Gradients

In aesthetics, ask how much a particular judgment depends on form, expression, representation, or context. These are different considerations, not mutually exclusive schools on one scale.

  • Art Theory: What is the nature and purpose of art?
  • Beauty: What constitutes beauty and how is it perceived?
  • Criticism: How should art be evaluated and interpreted?
6. Logical Gradients

Logical systems and methods answer different questions. Propositional and predicate logic are not rival degrees of logical rigor. A usable comparison might track the extent of formalization within a particular argument.

  • Formal Logic: What are the rules governing valid inference?
  • Informal Logic: How are everyday arguments constructed and evaluated?
  • Philosophy of Logic: What is the nature and scope of logical systems?
7. Phenomenological Gradients

Phenomenology examines how things are experienced, including their directedness, horizons, and bodily or social setting. Compare an author’s descriptive emphasis without ranking some experiences as more real than others.

  • Intentionality: How is consciousness directed toward objects?
  • Experience: What is the nature of subjective experience?
  • Essence: What are the essential structures of phenomena?
8. Existential Gradients

Existential questions concern situated freedom, responsibility, authenticity, and meaning. Compare what an author says a person can choose in a specified situation; do not confuse practical power with the claim that one must take a stance toward a situation.

  • Freedom: What is the role of individual freedom in shaping existence?
  • Authenticity: How should individuals live authentically?
  • Absurdity: How do humans confront the absurdity of existence?
9. Analytical Gradients

Analytic philosophy includes diverse uses of language analysis, logic, and scientific inquiry. Clarity and argument are not its exclusive possessions. A useful local comparison asks what role empirical research plays in resolving a particular philosophical question.

  • Language: What is the relationship between language and reality?
  • Logic: How are logical methods used to solve philosophical problems?
  • Science: What is the relationship between philosophy and the natural sciences?

These nine areas are a research map. For any proposed gradient, write the question and the ordering rule before adding names. If two positions differ in kind or answer different questions, retain a branching classification. A midpoint should express a coherent position, not merely soften two incompatible slogans.

Prompt 2: For each of those gradients, provide at least 5 defined points.

Definitions first; nine explicit five-point scales below

ChatGPT response · editorial edition

Metaphysical Gradients
  1. Materialism:
    • Physicalism: Everything is physical or depends wholly on the physical. Identity, realization, and other dependence accounts differ; physicalism does not by itself identify every mental-state type with one brain-state type.
    • Naturalism: In metaphysics, reality contains no supernatural entities or interventions. Methodological naturalism instead concerns how inquiry proceeds; it does not alone settle this metaphysical claim.
    • Mechanism: Explaining phenomena through organized parts and their activities. Historical mechanical worldviews often added determinism, but a mechanistic explanation need not claim that every event is predetermined.
    • Reductionism: The belief that complex phenomena can be explained by simpler, more fundamental parts.
    • Eliminative Materialism: The belief that certain categories of mental states that common sense takes for granted do not exist.
  2. Dualism:
    • Cartesian Dualism: The belief in the separation of mind and body as two fundamentally different substances.
    • Property Dualism: Mental properties are irreducible to physical properties. This does not by itself require two kinds of substance; competing versions explain the dependence and causal role differently.
    • Epiphenomenalism: The belief that mental phenomena are the byproducts of physical processes and cannot affect the physical world.
    • Parallelism: Mental and bodily sequences correspond without direct causal interaction between them. Pre-established harmony is one account of that correspondence, not the definition of every parallelism.
    • Interactionism: The belief that the mind and body can interact and influence each other.
  3. Idealism:
    • Objective Idealism: Reality is understood through an ideal or intelligible order that is not reducible to any individual human’s perceptions. The role of mind differs among its proponents.
    • Subjective Idealism: In the Berkeleyan case, sensible objects consist in ideas perceived by spirits; this does not mean only my mind exists. Berkeley distinguishes passive ideas from active spirits in Principles, §§27 and 86.
    • Transcendental Idealism: Kant distinguishes objects as experienced under our forms of sensibility from things considered in themselves. He does not simply deny external objects. See Prolegomena, §13, Remark II (a modernized rendering).
    • Absolute Idealism: The belief that reality is the manifestation of an absolute, all-encompassing mind or spirit.
    • Personal Idealism: A family of views giving persons or spiritual selves a fundamental place in reality. It is not simply the thesis that people collectively invent the world.
  4. Monism:
    • Material Monism: Reality has one fundamental kind, the physical. Distinguish one kind of substance from literally one individual substance.
    • Immaterial Monism: Reality has one fundamental nonphysical kind. Its identification as mental or spiritual needs a further argument.
    • Neutral Monism: Mental and physical descriptions derive from a more basic reality characterized as neither intrinsically mental nor intrinsically physical.
    • Spinozism: The belief that there is only one substance, which can be thought of as God or nature.
    • Hylomorphism: Concrete substances are understood through matter and form. Retained here as a comparison case: it is not straightforwardly a subtype of substance monism or Cartesian dualism.
  5. Pluralism:
    • Metaphysical Pluralism: The belief that there are many kinds of substances or fundamental entities.
    • Value Pluralism: More than one irreducible value may matter, and values can conflict. This ethical claim does not establish many kinds of metaphysical substance.
    • Cultural Pluralism: Support for the coexistence of different cultural practices or communities. This political or social position is not itself a metaphysical ontology, nor does it validate every practice.
    • Epistemic Pluralism: Different methods or perspectives may contribute to understanding. Their adequacy still requires criteria; plurality does not make every purported way of knowing reliable.
    • Ontological Pluralism: A view allowing fundamentally different ways of being. Distinguish this from the simpler claim that many objects or many kinds of object exist.
Epistemological Gradients
  1. Empiricism:
    • Classical Empiricism: Experience plays a fundamental role in acquiring ideas or knowledge. The strength and scope of this claim vary; it need not deny every a priori truth.
    • Radical Empiricism: For William James, experienced relations count as part of experience alongside the things related. It is not the positivist analytic-or-verifiable criterion. See Essays in Radical Empiricism, “A World of Pure Experience”.
    • Logical Empiricism: A movement combining logical analysis with empirically accountable inquiry. Verification, confirmation, and the status of theoretical terms were disputed within it.
    • Naive Empiricism: A loose critical label for treating observations as a sufficient, interpretation-free foundation. Specify the target rather than treating this as a precisely bounded historical school.
    • Moderate Empiricism: Here, an approach giving experience a central role while allowing some nonexperiential contribution. The allowed contribution must be specified; this is a broad contrast, not a standard five-stage classification.
  2. Rationalism:
    • Classical Rationalism: Some substantive knowledge can be justified through reason independently of experience. This does not mean rationalists dispense with observation in every inquiry.
    • Neo-Rationalism: A variable label for renewed rationalist approaches. Specify the author and thesis; the need to interpret observations alone does not distinguish a rationalist from an empiricist.
    • Critical Rationalism: A fallibilist approach emphasizing conjectures and criticism rather than conclusive verification. Its use of “rationalism” does not make it the same position as classical a priori rationalism.
    • Intuitionism: The belief that certain truths or principles can be known directly through intellectual intuition.
    • Constructivist Rationalism: An unstable label, not one agreed school. Here it means an approach emphasizing rational construction; identify the particular account before assigning philosophers to it.
  3. Skepticism:
    • Academic Skepticism: A diverse ancient tradition challenging claims to secure knowledge. Its treatments of assent and practical plausibility vary; one formula does not cover the whole Academy.
    • Pyrrhonian Skepticism: Inquiry that suspends judgment when opposing considerations balance, while continuing to live by appearances and ordinary practices. It need not assert that every belief is false.
    • Moral Skepticism: The belief that there are no objective moral truths or that we cannot know them.
    • Religious Skepticism: Doubt or suspended judgment about specified religious claims. It can be local and need not deny the possibility of every justified religious belief.
    • Scientific Skepticism: A practice of critically testing empirical claims and evidence. Distinguish it from global philosophical doubt about whether scientific knowledge is possible.
  4. Foundationalism:
    • Classical Foundationalism: The belief that all knowledge is based on self-evident or indubitable foundations.
    • Modest Foundationalism: The belief that basic beliefs do not need to be infallible but must be justified in some reliable manner.
    • Coherentist Foundationalism: A hybrid label requiring clarification. Basic beliefs might receive initial support independently while coherence improves their standing. Pure coherentism instead denies that privileged foundations are necessary.
    • Infinitism: Justification requires an unending, nonrepeating chain of reasons. Retained for comparison, but it is a rival to foundationalism rather than a subtype of it.
    • Contextual Foundationalism: A label for accounts in which what functions as a starting point depends on the context. Specify whether this concerns justification or merely what is left unquestioned in practice.
  5. Coherentism:
    • Classical Coherentism: The belief that beliefs are justified by their coherence with a system of interconnected beliefs.
    • Holistic Coherentism: Justification depends on relations within a system of beliefs considered together. The phrase overlaps with classical coherentism rather than naming a distinct rung on a scale.
    • Moderate Coherentism: Here, a mixed account assigning coherence an important but insufficient role. If independent evidential support is required, explain how the account departs from pure coherentism.
    • Pragmatic Coherentism: Here, coherence is considered together with success in inquiry or action. Practical usefulness alone does not establish truth; this label needs an author-specific definition.
    • Conceptual Coherentism: Here, emphasis on the compatibility of concepts within a system. Conceptual consistency is not by itself enough to justify factual beliefs.
Ethical Gradients
  1. Utilitarianism:
    • Act Utilitarianism: An action is right when its consequences maximize aggregate welfare among the available alternatives, under the theory’s account of welfare and consequence assessment.
    • Rule Utilitarianism: The belief that the morality of an action depends on its adherence to rules that lead to the greatest good.
    • Preference Utilitarianism: Aggregate preference satisfaction supplies the relevant good. Whose preferences count, how strongly they count, and whether they must be informed require specification.
    • Negative Utilitarianism: Suffering reduction has special or exclusive priority. Strong and weak versions differ in whether other goods can outweigh reductions in suffering.
    • Total Utilitarianism: Outcomes are assessed by total welfare across those who count. This contrasts with average-welfare views and need not restrict welfare to happiness.
  2. Deontology:
    • Kantian Deontology: Moral requirements constrain action through principles such as universalizability and respect for persons. Acting in accordance with duty and acting from duty are different questions.
    • Rights-Based Deontology: The belief that actions are morally right if they respect the rights of individuals.
    • Rule Deontology: The belief that moral principles and rules determine the rightness of actions.
    • Divine Command Theory: The belief that moral actions are those commanded by a divine being.
    • Contractarian Deontology: Contract-based approaches justify norms through a specified form of agreement. They are not all deontological, and actual agreement is not automatically morally authoritative.
  3. Virtue Ethics:
    • Aristotelian Virtue Ethics: The belief that moral virtue is achieved by living in accordance with reason and developing good character traits.
    • Eudaimonism: The belief that the highest moral goal is to achieve human flourishing and well-being.
    • Agent-Based Virtue Ethics: The belief that morality is based on the character and intentions of the moral agent.
    • Care Ethics: An approach centering relationships, dependency, and attentive response. Retained beside virtue ethics as a related approach, not treated as an uncontested subtype of it.
    • Neo-Aristotelian Virtue Ethics: The belief that modern interpretations of Aristotle’s ethics can provide guidance for contemporary moral issues.
  4. Relativism:
    • Cultural Relativism: The belief that moral principles are relative to cultural norms and practices.
    • Subjectivism: The belief that moral judgments are based on individual preferences and perspectives.
    • Moral Pluralism: Several irreducible moral considerations can matter and conflict. A pluralist may still affirm objective moral truths, so pluralism does not entail relativism.
    • Situational Ethics: Moral judgment gives a central role to circumstances. Context sensitivity can coexist with a universal principle; it does not by itself imply relativism.
    • Descriptive Relativism: The observation that moral beliefs differ among people or cultures. Disagreement does not establish that moral truth is relative.
  5. Egoism:
    • Psychological Egoism: The descriptive thesis that every ultimate human motive is self-interested. Its truth is contested, and it would not by itself establish how people ought to act.
    • Ethical Egoism: The belief that individuals should act in their own self-interest.
    • Rational Egoism: Rational action is governed by the agent’s self-interest. This is a claim about rational requirements, which must be distinguished from moral requirements.
    • Hedonistic Egoism: One’s own pleasure or happiness is the ultimate good for one’s action. Hedonism without the self-regarding restriction need not be egoistic.
    • Altruistic Egoism: A loose label for helping others as a means to one’s own benefit. An act can benefit both parties without proving that its ultimate motive was self-interest.
Political Gradients
  1. Liberalism:
    • Classical Liberalism: The belief in individual liberty, private property, and limited government.
    • Social Liberalism: The belief in individual freedoms combined with social justice and welfare policies.
    • Economic Liberalism: The belief in free markets, free trade, and minimal government intervention in the economy.
    • Political Liberalism: Broadly, a family of approaches to legitimate political order and rights. In Rawls’s technical usage it concerns stability and justification amid reasonable disagreement, not simply democracy.
    • Cultural Liberalism: The belief in the protection of individual cultural and lifestyle choices.
  2. Conservatism:
    • Traditional Conservatism: The belief in preserving cultural and social traditions and institutions.
    • Fiscal Conservatism: The belief in limited government spending, low taxes, and balanced budgets.
    • Social Conservatism: The belief in upholding traditional family values and moral standards.
    • Neoconservatism: A historically situated and internally varied political tendency, often associated in the United States with assertive foreign policy. It should not be reduced to one timeless philosophical principle.
    • Libertarian Conservatism: Attempts to combine limited government or market freedom with conservative attachments. Tensions over social regulation must be stated rather than assumed away.
  3. Socialism:
    • Democratic Socialism: The belief in achieving socialist goals through democratic means and political participation.
    • Marxism: The belief in the class struggle, the overthrow of capitalism, and the establishment of a classless society.
    • Utopian Socialism: The belief in creating ideal, cooperative communities free from social inequalities.
    • Revolutionary Socialism: The belief in achieving socialism through radical and revolutionary means.
    • Eco-Socialism: The belief in combining ecological sustainability with socialist economic policies.
  4. Libertarianism:
    • Minarchism: A minimal state, typically restricted to protecting rights through policing, courts, and defense. Broader public-goods provision is disputed within such views.
    • Anarcho-Capitalism: The belief in the elimination of the state and the provision of services through voluntary, private means.
    • Classical Liberalism: The belief in individual liberty, free markets, and limited government.
    • Left-Libertarianism: A variable family of views combining liberty with egalitarian concerns; some specifically combine self-ownership with equal claims to natural resources. Specify the tradition meant.
    • Right-Libertarianism: The belief in strong property rights, free markets, and minimal government intervention.
  5. Communitarianism:
    • Responsive Communitarianism: The belief in balancing individual rights with social responsibilities and community values.
    • Social Capital Theory: Analysis of social networks, trust, and civic cooperation. This can inform communitarian arguments but is not itself one communitarian doctrine.
    • Civic Republicanism: A family of views emphasizing citizenship, civic institutions, and often freedom from domination. It overlaps with some communitarian concerns without being reducible to them.
    • Participatory Democracy: An institutional ideal giving citizens a direct role in decisions. It can be defended from several philosophical positions, not only communitarianism.
    • Cooperative Economics: Economic organization emphasizing cooperation or shared ownership. It is an institutional orientation rather than a single philosophical school.
Aesthetic Gradients
  1. Formalism:
    • Structuralism: Interpretation through relationships within systems of signs or practices. Related to some formal approaches, but not simply the claim that artistic value lies in visible form.
    • Russian Formalism: The belief in analyzing literary texts by their form and technical devices.
    • New Criticism: The belief in close reading and analysis of the text itself, independent of external context.
    • Abstract Art: Art that departs from straightforward representation. This is an artistic category, not a commitment to formalism as the sole theory of value.
    • Geometric Abstraction: An artistic approach using geometric forms. Its presence does not settle the maker’s or critic’s theory of artistic value.
  2. Expressionism:
    • Emotional Expression: The belief that art’s value lies in its ability to express and evoke emotions.
    • Psychoanalytic Art Theory: The belief that art expresses the unconscious mind and inner psychological states.
    • Abstract Expressionism: A diverse artistic movement associated with abstract forms and, in some works, expressive gesture. A movement is not a single philosophical doctrine.
    • Symbolism: Artistic use of symbols and suggestive meanings; also the name of a historical movement. Neither use is simply a subtype of expressionist philosophy.
    • Romanticism: The belief in emphasizing emotion, imagination, and individual expression in art.
  3. Mimesis:
    • Realism: The belief in depicting subjects as they appear in everyday life, without idealization.
    • Naturalism: In art or literature, an approach emphasizing detailed representation and, in some contexts, environmental or social determination. This differs from metaphysical naturalism.
    • Imitation Theory: The belief that art imitates or represents reality.
    • Verisimilitude: The quality of seeming credible or lifelike. It can be achieved in fiction without photographic accuracy.
    • Photorealism: An artistic approach producing images closely related to photographic appearances. It is not a complete theory of what makes art valuable.
  4. Pragmatism:
    • Functionalism: The belief that art’s value lies in its practical function and utility.
    • Instrumentalism: The belief that art serves as a tool for social or personal purposes.
    • Deweyan Pragmatism: Art is considered through organized, lived experience and its continuity with ordinary life; its worth is not reduced to an external practical use.
    • Applied Aesthetics: A broad label for aesthetic inquiry in practical settings, such as design or everyday environments. It is a field of application rather than a single doctrine.
    • Social Art: The belief that art should address and contribute to social issues and change.
  5. Relativism:
    • Cultural Relativism: The belief that aesthetic values are relative to cultural contexts and traditions.
    • Subjective Relativism: The belief that aesthetic judgments are based on individual preferences and tastes.
    • Contextual Relativism: Here, a view making aesthetic assessment depend on a context. Context sensitivity alone does not imply that every context supplies equally valid standards.
    • Historical Relativism: Here, a thesis about the dependence of aesthetic standards on historical settings. Mere historical change in judgments does not prove that thesis.
    • Pluralistic Relativism: A loose label for allowing several aesthetic perspectives. Pluralism does not by itself entail that there are no better or worse judgments.
Logical Gradients
  1. Deductive Logic:
    • Syllogistic Logic: The study of logical arguments based on syllogisms, consisting of premises and a conclusion.
    • Propositional Logic: The study of logical relationships between propositions or statements.
    • Predicate Logic: The study of logical relationships involving predicates and quantifiers.
    • Modal Logic: The study of necessity and possibility in logical statements.
    • Temporal Logic: The study of logical relationships involving time and temporal expressions.
  2. Inductive Logic:
    • Enumerative Induction: The process of drawing general conclusions based on specific observations.
    • Statistical Induction: The process of making probabilistic inferences based on statistical data.
    • Causal Inference: Reasoning about causes using observations, interventions, and explicit assumptions. Correlation alone does not identify a cause, and causal analysis is not a single inductive logic.
    • Bayesian Inference: The process of updating probabilities based on new evidence and prior beliefs.
    • Analogical Reasoning: The process of drawing conclusions based on similarities between different cases or situations.
  3. Modal Logic:
    • Alethic Modal Logic: The study of logical necessity and possibility.
    • Deontic Modal Logic: The study of logical relationships involving moral obligations and permissions.
    • Epistemic Modal Logic: Formal study of knowledge operators; belief is treated in closely related doxastic logic.
    • Temporal Modal Logic: The study of logical relationships involving time and temporal modalities.
    • Dynamic Modal Logic: The study of logical relationships involving actions and their effects.
  4. Symbolic Logic:
    • Boolean Algebra: An algebraic structure modeling operations such as conjunction, disjunction, and complement. Two-valued truth assignments provide a familiar example.
    • Predicate Calculus: The study of formal systems involving predicates, quantifiers, and logical connectives.
    • Formal Proofs: The study of rigorous methods for deriving conclusions from premises using formal rules.
    • Set Theory: The study of sets and their properties, relations, and operations.
    • Model Theory: The study of the interpretation of formal languages and their structures.
  5. Fuzzy Logic:
    • Fuzzy Sets: The study of sets with degrees of membership, rather than binary inclusion or exclusion.
    • Linguistic Variables: The study of variables that represent imprecise or qualitative values.
    • Fuzzy Inference: The process of drawing conclusions based on fuzzy logic rules and principles.
    • Fuzzy Control: The application of fuzzy logic to control systems and decision-making processes.
    • Approximate Reasoning: Reasoning with graded or imprecise classifications. Fuzzy membership, such as a degree of tallness, is distinct from a probability that a sharply defined claim is true.
Phenomenological Gradients
  1. Intentionality:
    • Directedness: The quality of consciousness being directed toward objects or states of affairs.
    • Noematic Content: In Husserlian analysis, the object as intended, including the sense through which it is presented. Interpretations of the noema differ; it is not simply an extra object inside the head.
    • Noetic Act: The aspect of intentionality that refers to the act of consciousness or mental activity.
    • Bracketing (Epoché): The method of suspending judgment about the natural world to focus on the structures of consciousness.
    • Phenomenal Intentionality: Intentionality grounded in the phenomenal character of experience. It concerns a proposed connection between what experience is like and what it is about.
  2. Epoché:
    • Phenomenological Reduction: A shift toward how objects are given in experience, with ordinary existential commitments bracketed. This does not require denying the world’s existence.
    • Transcendental Reduction: The process of focusing on the conditions of possibility for experience and consciousness.
    • Eidetic Reduction: The process of identifying the essential features of phenomena through imaginative variation.
    • Suspension of Belief: Here, setting aside the ordinary commitment to the world’s existence for analysis. It is not a general command to doubt everything or erase all background understanding.
    • Pure Phenomenology: Inquiry into structures of experience and their intentional correlates. It does not simply remove objects or all context from consideration.
  3. Noema and Noesis:
    • Noema: The object as intended and its sense within an intentional experience; the precise analysis is disputed among interpreters of Husserl.
    • Noesis: The intentional act or process of consciousness.
    • Correlation: The relationship between noema and noesis in the structure of experience.
    • Intentional Correlates: The paired aspects of experience (noema and noesis) that together constitute intentionality.
    • Phenomenological Analysis: The method of examining and describing the noetic and noematic aspects of experience.
  4. Lifeworld (Lebenswelt):
    • Pre-Reflective Experience: The immediate, everyday experience of the world before reflection or analysis.
    • Intersubjectivity: The shared, communal aspects of the lifeworld experienced by multiple individuals.
    • Horizon: The background context or framework within which experience and understanding take place.
    • Embodiment: The lived experience of having a body and being situated in the world.
    • Practical Activity: The everyday actions and practices that constitute the lifeworld.
  5. Reduction:
    • Phenomenological Reduction: Returning to how objects are given while bracketing ordinary existential commitments. This repeats the earlier entry because the original outline overlaps; it is not another degree on a scale.
    • Transcendental Reduction: Inquiry into the conditions under which experience and objectivity are possible. This repeats an earlier method rather than adding a new point.
    • Eidetic Reduction: Investigating essential features through imaginative variation. This concerns invariants, not merely shrinking experience to smaller components.
    • Methodological Reduction: The systematic approach to examining consciousness and its structures.
    • Existential Reduction: A nonstandard label in this list. If retained, define the particular author’s method of examining lived existence; do not assume one canonical Husserlian procedure.
Existential Gradients
  1. Freedom:
    • Radical Freedom: In the Sartrean setting, one cannot avoid taking a stance through choice, even within circumstances one did not choose. It does not mean unlimited power to achieve outcomes; compare Existentialism Is a Humanism.
    • Authenticity: Owning one’s situated commitments and responsibility rather than treating them as mechanically dictated. Different existential authors develop the idea differently.
    • Bad Faith: Self-deception about one’s freedom or facticity—the given features of one’s situation. Mere conformity is not a sufficient definition.
    • Existential Choice: The emphasis on the importance of individual decisions and their impact on one’s life.
    • Freedom and Responsibility: The belief that with freedom comes the responsibility for one’s actions and their consequences.
  2. Authenticity:
    • True Self: A contested expression here. Some existential approaches reject a pre-given inner essence; the relevant task is taking responsibility for commitments, not merely discovering a hidden personality.
    • Existential Authenticity: Taking responsibility for one’s situated way of living. It need not mean freedom from every social influence.
    • Individualism: The emphasis on the unique, individual nature of each person’s existence.
    • Authentic Existence: Living with acknowledgment of one’s situation and commitments. Authenticity does not guarantee happiness or morally admirable choices.
    • Self-Actualization: Realizing capacities or potential. This neighboring psychological ideal should not be treated as identical to existential authenticity or assumed to fit every existential account.
  3. Absurdity:
    • Meaninglessness: The absence of a given or guaranteed purpose. This does not by itself show that all human projects lack value.
    • The Absurd Hero: A figure who continues living lucidly without pretending the tension between the demand for meaning and the world’s silence has been finally resolved.
    • Rebellion: Resistance to resignation or imposed meaning. Its ethical and political significance needs more than the assertion of private preference.
    • Camus’s Absurd: The confrontation between the human demand for intelligibility or meaning and the world’s failure to answer that demand. It is a relation, not merely a property of the world alone.
    • Existential Courage: The strength to confront and accept the absurdity of existence.
  4. Alienation:
    • Existential Alienation: The feeling of disconnection from oneself, others, or the world.
    • Social Alienation: The sense of isolation from society and its norms and values.
    • Self-Alienation: Estrangement from one’s activity, commitments, or sense of agency. An account need not assume a fixed inner essence from which one has departed.
    • Alienation in Work: Estrangement involving labor, its product, or its social organization; this can concern objective relations of control, not only a worker’s feelings.
    • Overcoming Alienation: The pursuit of authentic relationships and connections to overcome feelings of alienation.
  5. Existential Angst:
    • Existential Anxiety: The anxiety that arises from the awareness of one’s freedom and responsibility.
    • Fear of Nothingness: The dread of non-existence or the void.
    • Angst and Choice: The anxiety associated with making significant life choices.
    • Authentic Angst: The recognition and acceptance of existential anxiety as part of the human condition.
    • Resolving Angst: Confronting anxiety and reconsidering commitments. An existential account need not promise to eliminate anxiety or treat it as a defect that authenticity cures.
Analytical Gradients
  1. Language Analysis:
    • Ordinary Language Philosophy: The analysis of everyday language use and its implications for philosophical problems.
    • Speech Act Theory: The study of how utterances function as actions in communication.
    • Meaning and Reference: The examination of how words and phrases relate to objects and concepts in the world.
    • Semantics: The study of meaning and interpretation. Syntax concerns sentence structure; semantic analysis asks how expressions and their combinations receive meaning.
    • Pragmatics: The study of how context influences the interpretation of language.
  2. Logical Positivism:
    • Verification Principle: A proposed criterion linking the cognitive significance of empirical statements to possible verification; analytic statements were typically treated separately. Versions and objections differ.
    • Logical Atomism: A view analyzing propositions and the world through elementary facts. It influenced some positivist work but is not simply one of logical positivism’s defining doctrines.
    • Emphasis on Science: The belief that philosophy should be closely aligned with the methods and findings of science.
    • Rejection of Metaphysics: The belief that metaphysical statements are meaningless because they cannot be empirically verified.
    • Analytic and Synthetic Distinction: Broadly, a distinction between truths grounded in meaning and claims whose truth is not settled by meaning alone. Synthetic does not mean empirically verifiable by definition.
  3. Ordinary Language Philosophy:
    • Language Games: The concept that the meaning of words is determined by their use in specific social contexts.
    • Contextual Meaning: The belief that the meaning of language depends on its use in particular situations.
    • Philosophical Problems as Linguistic Confusions: The belief that many philosophical problems arise from misunderstandings of language.
    • Emphasis on Everyday Language: The focus on analyzing ordinary language rather than technical or specialized language.
    • Therapeutic Approach: The idea that clarifying language use can dissolve philosophical confusion and problems.
  4. Conceptual Analysis:
    • Definitional Clarity: The process of providing precise definitions for philosophical concepts.
    • Necessary and Sufficient Conditions: Necessary conditions must hold for application; sufficient conditions guarantee it. A proposed definition seeks an appropriate combination, though not every concept yields to this form.
    • Thought Experiments: The use of hypothetical scenarios to test and refine conceptual understanding.
    • Intuitive Testing: The reliance on intuitive judgments to evaluate the adequacy of conceptual analyses.
    • Philosophical Methodology: The systematic approach to analyzing and clarifying philosophical concepts.
  5. Philosophy of Science:
    • Scientific Method: The study of the principles and practices that underlie scientific inquiry.
    • Theory and Observation: The examination of the relationship between scientific theories and empirical observations.
    • Scientific Realism: Roughly, mature successful theories aim to describe a mind-independent world, including unobservables, and may be approximately true. Realists disagree about scope and justification.
    • Instrumentalism: The belief that scientific theories are useful tools for predicting phenomena, but not necessarily true descriptions of reality.
    • Paradigms and Revolutions: The study of how scientific theories change over time and the role of paradigm shifts in scientific progress.

The definitions above preserve the original nine areas and five-by-five nested lists, but several entries are overlapping theories, practices, or comparison cases rather than points on a line. To answer the request for defined gradient points directly, the following editorial supplement provides one explicit ordering within each area. These are teaching conventions, not equal intervals, exhaustive alternatives, or historical scores.

Editorial supplement: five defined points within each of the nine areas

Read each scale from 1 to 5 using its stated question. These comparisons measure different features; a higher number is not a better philosophy. Mark “not applicable,” “rejects the question,” or “insufficient evidence” separately rather than treating these as midpoint positions.

Metaphysical: explanatory priority of the physical

For a specified phenomenon, how much explanatory priority does the view give physical factors relative to irreducibly nonphysical factors?

  1. No explanatory role for physical factors.
  2. A secondary role, with irreducibly nonphysical factors doing the principal work.
  3. Both kinds are indispensable, with neither given general priority.
  4. Physical factors do the principal work, with a specified nonphysical contribution still required.
  5. A wholly physical account is sufficient in principle.
Epistemological: justificatory weight of experience

In evaluating a specified substantive claim, what weight is assigned to experience relative to nonexperiential reasons?

  1. Experience supplies no justificatory weight.
  2. Experience supplies secondary support; nonexperiential reasons normally decide conflicts.
  3. Both supply indispensable support, with neither given general priority.
  4. Experience supplies the principal support; nonexperiential reasons have a supporting role.
  5. Experience is the sole source of substantive warrant; reasoning organizes rather than adds independent warrant.
Ethical: priority of a specified constraint

When the constraint conflicts with improved consequences, how resistant is it to override?

  1. It has no independent weight.
  2. It supplies a weak presumption.
  3. It supplies a strong but defeasible presumption.
  4. Only an extreme threshold permits override.
  5. No improvement in consequences permits override.
Political: scope of coercive authority

For a specified institution, how much control is legitimate?

  1. No coercive authority.
  2. Authority over a narrowly defined set of acts.
  3. Authority over several delimited public functions.
  4. Broad authority with protected exceptions.
  5. Comprehensive authority without a protected sphere.
Aesthetic: weight of formal features

In judging a specified work, how much evaluative weight is assigned to its arrangement of perceptible elements?

  1. No independent weight.
  2. Minor weight.
  3. Weight comparable to other considerations.
  4. Usually decisive weight.
  5. Exclusive weight.
Logical: extent of formalization

How explicitly is a particular argument represented in a formal system?

  1. Entirely ordinary-language argument.
  2. Key terms stipulated but inferences remain informal.
  3. Selected inferential steps formalized.
  4. Most steps formalized, with a stated informal remainder.
  5. A complete derivation relative to declared premises and rules.
Phenomenological: contextual reach of a description

How much of the experience’s surrounding situation is explicitly analyzed?

  1. One isolated experiential feature.
  2. Its immediate relation to an intended object.
  3. The unfolding experience and its temporal horizon.
  4. Its bodily and practical situation as well.
  5. Its shared lifeworld and historical horizon as well.
Existential: scope of attributed practical control

In the specified circumstances, how much can the person intentionally change?

  1. No available relevant change.
  2. A narrow set of responses.
  3. Several meaningful but constrained courses.
  4. Most relevant circumstances.
  5. Virtually all relevant circumstances.
Analytical: role of empirical inquiry

In resolving the specified philosophical question, what role is assigned to empirical findings?

  1. No justificatory role.
  2. Illustration only.
  3. A check on proposed analyses.
  4. An indispensable partner to conceptual analysis.
  5. The principal basis for resolution.

For example, two hypothetical theorists might both place great weight on consequences but disagree about whether one particular promise may ever be broken. The ethical scale compares the promise constraint, not their entire moral outlook. Likewise, the existential scale concerns practical control, not whether freedom is compatible with determinism. Advice on Populating Gradients with Philosophers

Begin with the question and its definitions, then place claims from particular works. A philosopher’s name is a shortcut to those claims, not evidence for a position. The examples below retain the original proposed populations while marking important corrections and limits.

Step 1: Research and Identify Key Philosophers

Use reference works to locate candidates and primary passages. Keep a record with the author, work and date, domain, proposed placement, supporting passage, reason, and confidence. Record a counter-passage or competing interpretation when one materially changes the placement.

Step 2: Categorize Philosophers

Separate category membership from position on a scale. “Idealist” is a family label; a score about the scope of physical explanation answers a different question. Allow one thinker several placements in different domains or periods, and do not use a midpoint for uncertainty.

Step 3: Verify with Primary Sources

Read the supporting passage in context. Check whether it is the author’s conclusion, an opponent’s view, or a provisional stage in an argument. Consult informed disagreement rather than using a quotation merely to confirm a placement already chosen.

Step 4: Create Comprehensive Lists

Build annotated candidate lists before plotting. A defensible entry might say: “Berkeley, Principles §§27 and 86: ideas and active spirits are distinguished; this supports a mental ontology but not Cartesian mind–matter dualism.” The annotation should make the classification revisable.

Step 5: Review and Revise

Test the definitions on difficult cases. If the same passage seems to justify two incompatible placements, revise the axis or explain the interpretation. Aim for transparent coverage, not a claim to completeness that the reading does not support.

Example Populations for Selected Gradients

The following are provisional category lists, not verified numerical placements on the nine scales. All twelve original five-name groups are retained. An explicit “comparison case” flags an inherited placement that should not be treated as straightforward membership.

Metaphysical Gradients
  1. Materialism
    • Thomas Hobbes
    • Julien Offray de La Mettrie
    • Karl Marx — materialist accounts of history and social production; do not infer a complete theory of mind from this label alone.
    • Daniel Dennett
    • Paul Churchland
  2. Dualism
    • René Descartes
    • Plato — the soul–body distinctions in dialogues such as the Phaedo require their own explanation; not a Cartesian substance account imported unchanged into antiquity.
    • Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz — comparison case: his many simple substances and pre-established harmony resist classification as Cartesian two-substance dualism. See Monadology, §§1–7 and 78–81.
    • George Berkeley — inherited placement corrected: his distinction between spirits and ideas is not mind–matter dualism. His rejection of material substance belongs with the idealism entry below; retain him here as a warning against equating every twofold distinction with Cartesian dualism.
    • David Chalmers — his argument for naturalistic dualism is a relevant candidate, but specify the particular account and date. See his chapter “Naturalistic Dualism”; do not assume every dualist defends Descartes’s framework.
  3. Idealism
    • George Berkeley
    • Immanuel Kant — transcendental idealism coupled with the empirical reality of objects of experience; neither straightforward Berkeleyan idealism nor a denial of external objects.
    • Johann Gottlieb Fichte
    • Friedrich Wilhelm Joseph Schelling

Next Steps
  1. Expand Lists: Add candidates from different traditions and languages, recording where available translations or the categories themselves limit the comparison.
  2. Detailed Annotations: Include a passage, a reason, a domain, and a date. Separate your interpretation from the philosopher’s own terminology.
  3. Create Visuals: Use a line only where the positions are genuinely ordered. Use a category map or multiple axes otherwise, and show uncertainty without turning it into a midpoint.
  4. Cross-Reference: Link the same philosopher’s placements across questions while keeping distinct the evidence for each. Do not infer political positions from metaphysical labels.
  5. Regular Updates: Revise the annotation when better evidence or a stronger objection changes it; keep a record of the reason for the change.

Start with a small, well-supported comparison before filling the map. Its value lies in showing which arguments differ and why. A useful chart can leave a name unplaced; a crowded chart with unsupported coordinates conceals the very distinctions the curator wants to expose.
