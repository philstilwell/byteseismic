Prompt 1: Provide a general description of the philosophical school of Existentialism.

A family of approaches to human existence

ChatGPT response · editorial edition

Existentialism is a family of approaches that place lived existence, choice, responsibility, and finitude at the center of philosophy. It became especially prominent in the twentieth century, drawing on nineteenth-century predecessors. Its thinkers ask how people commit themselves under uncertainty and social pressure. They do not all hold that the universe is meaningless: religious and atheistic approaches disagree about what gives a life its ultimate significance.

Sartre’s account of existence preceding essence rejects a predetermined human purpose that could settle how we ought to live. Nietzsche, an important predecessor rather than a member of a later school, challenges inherited values and explores self-overcoming. Kierkegaard’s concern with subjective commitment belongs to a Christian project; it should not be absorbed into a doctrine of secular meaning-making. These differences matter as much as the recurring questions about becoming a self.

Existentialism has influenced various fields beyond philosophy, including literature, theology, and psychology, encouraging exploration of themes like freedom, isolation, and the existential crisis in the search for personal identity and truth.

Gemini response · editorial edition

Existentialism is a branch of philosophy that grapples with the big questions about human existence. Here are some central ideas:

  • Individuality: A person must live and answer for a particular life; a general system cannot simply perform that task for them. Some existentialists reject a given cosmic purpose, while religious thinkers locate commitment in a relation to God.
  • Freedom and Choice: Choices help shape a life, but they occur within bodily, historical, and social conditions. Responsibility is not equivalent to having unlimited options or causing every circumstance one faces.
  • The Absurd: Especially in Camus, this names a clash between the human demand for meaning and the world’s failure to satisfy it. It is not a finding that facts are unknowable or a doctrine shared identically by every existential thinker.
  • Authenticity: An authentic life owns its commitments and limits instead of using a role or convention as an automatic excuse. This is not simply discovering a finished “true self,” nor does sincerity make any action defensible.

Kierkegaard is a major religious predecessor, Sartre an explicit advocate of existentialism, and Camus a closely associated thinker who resisted the label. Reading them together is useful when their disagreements remain visible.

Prompt 2: Provide a list of the key contributions Existentialism has made to philosophical thought.

Contributions without a single shared creed

ChatGPT response · editorial edition

Existentialism has made several key contributions to philosophical thought, some of which include:

  1. Emphasis on Individual Existence: Existentialists focus on the individual’s unique position as a self-determining agent responsible for the authenticity of their choices and actions.
  2. Concept of Absurdity: Camus makes the conflict between the demand for meaning and the world’s silence a philosophical problem. This contribution is influential within the broader conversation without defining every form of existentialism.
  3. Freedom and Responsibility: Existentialist analyses ask how people participate in shaping a life even within conditions they did not choose. The challenge is to distinguish genuine constraint from an excuse without blaming people for their oppression.
  4. Authenticity: The concept exposes self-deception in treating oneself as merely a role, such as an employee who says a harmful act was “just the job.” Acknowledging agency does not settle which action is right; that still requires judgment about the situation and other people.
  5. Existential Angst and Dread: The philosophy addresses the feelings of disorientation and confusion that arise from understanding the fundamental freedom of choice and the weight of responsibility it entails.
  6. Existential Crisis: Existentialism delves into moments when individuals question the meaning, purpose, and value of life, often leading to a deeper understanding of one’s existence.
  7. Influence on Ethics: Sartre and Beauvoir investigate commitment without a ready-made moral script. This does not reduce ethics to whatever an individual happens to prefer: responsibility, relations with others, and the conditions of freedom remain subjects of argument.
  8. Critique of Exhaustive Systems: Existential thinkers challenge accounts that treat human life as fully captured by an impersonal scheme. The objection concerns what such accounts leave unexplained about commitment and lived experience; it need not reject reasoning or evidence.
  9. Influence on Literature and the Arts: Existential themes have profoundly influenced literature, drama, and film, focusing on characters grappling with the fundamental issues of choice, freedom, and existential despair.
  10. Phenomenology and Existence: Describing being-in-the-world reveals how things matter within practical activity and relations with others. This is not a choice between private feelings and scientific facts: the approaches ask different questions and may also challenge one another’s assumptions.

These contributions have enriched philosophical discourse and have had a significant impact on modern thinking about human freedom, ethics, and the nature of existence.

Gemini response · editorial edition

Existentialism’s contributions to philosophy are far-reaching, influencing not just academic thought but also literature, psychology, and even popular culture. Here are some key areas of its impact:

  • Primacy of Lived Existence: The tradition asks what it means for a particular person to inhabit a commitment. Kierkegaard’s religious project shows why this cannot be equated with a universal rejection of religion or of every shared standard.
  • Freedom and Responsibility: Sartre’s radical account and Beauvoir’s attention to concrete situations make the relation between agency and constraint difficult to ignore. A person can be responsible for a response without having selected the circumstances or possessing every possible option.
  • Authenticity and Self-Creation: Actions sustain or revise commitments. If someone calls themselves a loyal friend while consistently abandoning friends for convenience, the conflict is between a declared identity and a lived practice. Authenticity requires confronting it, not merely asserting sincerity.
  • Anxiety and Absurdity: Existential inquiry treats anxiety, contingency, and mortality as revealing features of human life. Camus’s account of absurdity is one influential approach; Kierkegaard’s anxiety and religious commitment cannot be reduced to accepting a meaningless universe.
  • Relations with Other Fields: Existential thought developed through literature as well as academic argument and later informed forms of psychotherapy. Dostoevsky is an important literary precursor, not simply an author influenced by the twentieth-century movement. Philosophical anxiety should also be distinguished from a clinical diagnosis.

Existentialism doesn’t provide easy answers, but it challenges us to grapple with fundamental questions and create a meaningful life in a complex and often confusing world.

Prompt 3: List the most influential Existentialists in history.

Predecessors, existentialists, and close interlocutors

ChatGPT response · editorial edition

Here are some of the most influential existentialist thinkers in history:

  1. Søren Kierkegaard: A central nineteenth-century predecessor, he explores anxiety, despair, faith, and the difficulty of becoming a self. The familiar “leap of faith” shorthand should not replace attention to the different voices and arguments in his works.
  2. Friedrich Nietzsche: While not an existentialist in the strict sense, his ideas about nihilism, the “will to power,” and the creation of one’s own values are foundational to existentialist thought.
  3. Jean-Paul Sartre: An explicit advocate of existentialism, he develops accounts of freedom, bad faith, and responsibility. His humanism lecture argues against both passivity and the idea that choice excuses arbitrary conduct.
  4. Simone de Beauvoir: A key figure in existentialist philosophy, she explored the ethics of ambiguity and the concept of the “Other,” particularly in the context of women’s existential oppression.
  5. Albert Camus: Although he personally rejected the label of existentialist, Camus’s ideas about the absurd, exemplified in works like “The Myth of Sisyphus” and “The Stranger,” are closely associated with existentialist themes.
  6. Martin Heidegger: Although his association with existentialism is debated, his analysis of “Being” and concepts like “Being-toward-death” and “authenticity” have had a profound influence on existential thought.
  7. Fyodor Dostoevsky: Not a philosopher by profession, his novels deeply explore existential themes, questioning free will, morality, and faith through complex characters and narrative.
  8. Karl Jaspers: He focused on themes of existentialist philosophy such as freedom, transcendence, and the “limit situation,” a concept that explores how crisis moments bring profound moral and existential awareness.

These thinkers, through their writings and ideas, have shaped the development of existentialist philosophy and continue to influence discussions on human freedom, authenticity, and the meaning of life.

Gemini response · editorial edition

Existentialism isn’t a monolithic school of thought, and there isn’t a single definitive list of existentialists. Some thinkers explicitly rejected the label, while others incorporated existential themes into their broader philosophies. Here are some of the most influential figures associated with existentialism:

  • Søren Kierkegaard (1813–1855): His analyses of anxiety, despair, and religious commitment made him a major predecessor. Treating his Christian inquiry as a doctrine that each person simply invents a meaning would erase an essential difference.
  • Friedrich Nietzsche (1844-1900): Although not an existentialist in the strictest sense, Nietzsche’s ideas on overcoming nihilism, re-evaluating traditional morality, and embracing the will to power were highly influential to existentialist thought.
  • Martin Heidegger (1889-1976): Heidegger’s focus on human existence (Dasein) and our Being-in-the-world laid the groundwork for much of 20th-century existentialism. His complex and sometimes controversial work explored themes of alienation, authenticity, and temporality.
  • Jean-Paul Sartre (1905-1980): Sartre is probably the most well-known existentialist. His concept of “radical freedom” and the “absurdity” of existence resonated widely. Sartre’s prolific writings and political activism made him a major intellectual figure of the 20th century.
  • Albert Camus (1913–1960): Closely associated with existential themes but resistant to the label, he examines absurdity and revolt. His work asks how to live without a false resolution of the demand for meaning, not simply how to celebrate meaninglessness.
  • Simone de Beauvoir (1908–1986): A philosopher in her own right, she develops an ethics of ambiguity and analyzes how oppression restricts concrete freedom. The Second Sex connects existential questions to women’s social situation. The source’s dates were incorrect.

These thinkers, though not always in agreement, all helped shape existentialism into a powerful philosophical movement that continues to influence thought and culture today.

Prompt 4: Produce a 20-line hypothetical dialogue between an Existentialist and a 1st-year philosophy student.

Two dialogues following the original student questions

ChatGPT response · editorial edition

This hypothetical exchange preserves all twenty-one original speaker turns. Numbered entry 20 contains both closing turns so that no student response is lost; it is a twenty-entry presentation, not a claim that the source contained only twenty turns.

  1. Student: I’ve been reading about existentialism, and I’m a bit confused. Doesn’t the idea that life has no inherent meaning lead to nihilism?

  2. Philosopher: The inference is not inevitable. Sartre asks how commitments can give a life direction without a purpose assigned in advance. Religious existential thinkers approach the question differently. Where do you think meaning comes from?

  3. Student: I always thought meaning was something we discover, not something we make.

  4. Philosopher: Sartre would emphasize what we do with a possibility rather than a completed purpose waiting inside us. Caring for a friend can become meaningful through sustained action; calling it meaningful does not replace doing it.

  5. Student: So, are you saying we’re completely free to choose our path?

  6. Philosopher: Not completely free in the sense of having every option. Illness, money, and oppression constrain possibilities. Sartre’s point about freedom concerns our involvement in a response, though how far he pushes it is open to criticism.

  7. Student: But doesn’t that level of freedom cause anxiety? How do we handle knowing every choice shapes our existence?

  8. Philosopher: Recognizing responsibility can produce anguish. A rule or adviser can help you deliberate without living the decision for you. But anguish does not prove that every imagined possibility is actually available. How does that distinction feel?

  9. Student: Honestly, it’s overwhelming. It feels like standing at the edge of a cliff.

  10. Philosopher: Then begin with a particular decision rather than an entire life at once. Name the available options, the commitments involved, and the people affected. What matters to you most?

  11. Student: I’m not sure yet. I guess I’m worried about making the wrong choices.

  12. Philosopher: That concern is justified. Sartre does not make any chosen action right merely because it is chosen. Avoiding a decision can itself commit us, but deliberation must still face what our actions mean for others.

  13. Student: So making any choice is better than being passive?

  14. Philosopher: No. Acting impulsively can be worse than pausing to understand the situation. The issue is whether delay serves inquiry or disguises avoidance. Authenticity does not remove ethical judgment. Does that clearer sense of authenticity resonate?

  15. Student: It does. I want to live authentically, but how do I start?

  16. Philosopher: Examine your motives and what you actually do. Social expectations may be justified or harmful; rejecting them automatically is no more thoughtful than obeying them automatically.

  17. Student: I need to think about that. It sounds like living authentically requires constant self-reflection.

  18. Philosopher: Reflection matters when it changes conduct. There need not be a finished “true self” beneath your actions: commitments are also made and tested through them. What belief would you want your conduct to sustain?

  19. Student: I believe in always learning and growing. Does that count?

  20. Philosopher: Yes, if learning and growing become practices, including accepting correction. Next time, bring a decision in which that commitment conflicts with convenience. We can examine the tension together.

    Student: I’d like that. Thank you for helping me see existentialism in a new light.

Gemini response · editorial edition

  1. Existentialist: So, you’re taking Introduction to Philosophy. Why all the big questions this early on?

  2. Student (slightly nervous): It’s all so fascinating! Trying to figure out life, you know?

  3. Existentialist: “Life” with a capital L, eh? What makes you think there’s one grand answer waiting to be found?

  4. Student: Well, professors talk about different schools of thought, like a map to navigate reality.

  5. Existentialist: Maps can clarify alternatives, but no map makes the commitment for you. In Sartre’s atheistic approach there is no divinely assigned human purpose. That is a position within this tradition, not a premise every associated thinker shares.

  6. Student: That’s, uh, a lot of freedom. Scary too. Isn’t there some objective truth out there?

  7. Existentialist: Facts do not disappear. Whether a promise was made or someone was harmed can be investigated. The further question is what commitment you undertake in light of those facts. Meaning-making is not permission to invent evidence.

  8. Student: So, we’re just…alone in our choices?

  9. Existentialist: Others are already involved in our possibilities and responsibilities. Beauvoir stresses that oppression can close off concrete opportunities. Owning a choice must not become blaming someone for barriers they did not create.

  10. Student (eyes widening): I guess it is. But what if I make the wrong choice?

  11. Existentialist: You may. Authenticity means facing your reasons and situation honestly; it cannot guarantee a good outcome or make a harmful choice right.

  12. Student: Messy?

  13. Existentialist: A decision can involve incomplete information and conflicting obligations. We should acknowledge that difficulty, rather than pretend that uncertainty makes every answer equally good.

  14. Student [editorial continuation]: Suppose I promised to help a friend, but keeping the promise would cost me an opportunity. Does creating meaning let me disregard it?

  15. Existentialist: No. The promise and your friend’s dependence are part of the situation. You need to face them rather than invent an identity that makes them disappear.

  16. Student: But there might still be a serious reason to change my plan.

  17. Existentialist: Yes. Explain that reason, consider alternatives, and accept responsibility for the effects. Freedom does not require pretending commitments can never conflict.

  18. Student: Then the lack of a perfect rule makes attention to the details more necessary.

  19. Existentialist: Exactly. A responsible choice considers facts and others’ freedom while acknowledging that no description can remove every uncertainty.

  20. Student: I can see why authenticity is harder than simply doing whatever feels like me.

  1. How does existentialism challenge traditional notions of morality and ethics?
  2. Discuss the impact of the belief that “existence precedes essence” on one’s personal and social life.
  3. How does the concept of the “absurd” influence the existentialist’s approach to life’s challenges?
  4. In what ways do Søren Kierkegaard’s ideas about “existential dread” reflect in modern existential crises?
  5. How might Friedrich Nietzsche’s idea of the “will to power” be interpreted in a contemporary context?
  6. Evaluate the implications of Jean-Paul Sartre’s statement, “We are condemned to be free,” in today’s society.
  7. Discuss Simone de Beauvoir’s contributions to existentialist philosophy, particularly regarding feminism.
  8. How does Albert Camus’s rejection of the existentialist label affect the interpretation of his works?
  9. Analyze the role of authenticity in existentialism and its relevance to personal development.
  10. How does existentialism address the issue of nihilism and the search for meaning?
  11. Compare and contrast the existentialist views on freedom and responsibility with those of another philosophical school.
  12. How do existentialist philosophies manifest in literature and the arts?
  13. Discuss the relevance of existentialist philosophy in dealing with contemporary global crises.
  14. How can existentialist principles be applied to educational methodologies and practices?

Gemini response · editorial edition

Existentialist Encounter Discussion Questions:

  1. Do you agree with the existentialist notion that individuals create their own meaning in life? Why or why not?
  2. How does the concept of freedom and choice impact the way we approach our goals and dreams?
  3. The passage mentions life being “messy.” What are some challenges associated with the lack of a predetermined path?
  4. How does Existentialism compare to other philosophies you’ve encountered, such as Stoicism or Utilitarianism?
  5. Why does the lack of a predetermined purpose not imply the absence of objective facts, and how does that distinction affect the student’s objection?
  6. How does Camus’s account of absurdity differ from a claim that life is logically contradictory or that every choice is equally justified?
  7. Do you think the emphasis on individual freedom in Existentialism can lead to feelings of isolation? How can we connect with others in this context?
  8. Can the concept of “radical freedom” be empowering or frightening? Discuss both sides of the coin.
  9. How might Existentialist ideas influence our approach to social justice issues or ethical dilemmas?
  10. In what ways can literature, art, or other creative pursuits help us create meaning in an absurd world?
  11. What can existential reflection clarify about uncertainty and responsibility, and where would claims about treating distress require a different kind of evidence?
  12. Can Existentialist ideas be reconciled with religious faith? Why or why not?
  13. How do you think advancements in technology or artificial intelligence might impact the way we grapple with existential questions?
  14. In your opinion, what are the most valuable contributions of Existentialism to philosophical thought?
  15. After considering the ideas of Existentialism, how might you define the purpose of your own life?
