Prompt 2: Describe the interplay between human emotions and faulty cognitive skills that prevents humans from accurately assessing risks.

How emotion and judgment interact

ChatGPT response · editorial edition

Emotion directs attention and signals what matters; probability judgment asks how likely an outcome is. Errors arise when the strength of the feeling becomes a substitute for that judgment, especially when memory, comparison groups, or numerical interpretation are unreliable. The following mechanisms can interact, but none predicts that every person will err in the same direction. Paul Slovic’s research on risk perception examines how people’s judgments of hazards differ from technical assessments.

  1. Availability Heuristic: People tend to overestimate the likelihood of events that are more memorable or that have been recently reported in the media. High-impact events like plane crashes or shark attacks are more newsworthy and thus more readily come to mind, leading us to overestimate their frequency.
  2. Negativity Bias: Threatening information can command disproportionate attention. This may distort a probability estimate, but giving a severe loss substantial weight is not automatically irrational. Concern about consequences must be distinguished from a claim about frequency.
  3. Optimism Bias: Paradoxically, while we overestimate the risk of rare and dramatic events, we often underestimate the risk of common or familiar hazards. This is due in part to optimism bias, where individuals believe they are less likely than others to experience negative events, leading to an underestimation of everyday risks like driving accidents or lifestyle-related health issues.
  4. Confirmation Bias: People tend to seek out information that confirms their preexisting beliefs or fears and discount information that contradicts them. If someone is afraid of flying, they may focus on news stories about plane accidents while ignoring statistics about air travel’s safety, further entrenching their misperception of the risk.
  5. Affect Heuristic: Emotions play a crucial role in decision-making and risk perception. The affect heuristic refers to the reliance on good or bad feelings experienced in relation to a stimulus, rather than on objective analysis. For example, fear and anxiety about a potential health diagnosis can lead to an overestimation of its likelihood.
  6. Misunderstanding of Probability and Statistics: An increase from 1 in 1,000 to 2 in 1,000 is a 100% relative increase but an absolute increase of 1 in 1,000, or 0.1 percentage point. Either description can be correct; an assessment needs the baseline and the severity of the outcome, not only the more alarming number.
  7. Social Influence: Social norms and peer influences can also skew risk perception. If a significant portion of one’s social circle expresses fear or concern about a particular risk, an individual is more likely to overestimate that risk due to social conformity pressures.
  8. Cognitive Dissonance: When confronted with evidence that contradicts their fear-based or optimistic assumptions about risk, individuals often experience cognitive dissonance. To reduce this discomfort, they may dismiss or rationalize away the new information, maintaining their inaccurate risk assessments.

For example, a frightening crash report becomes easy to recall; fear then makes similar reports more noticeable; selective attention can make each new report feel like confirmation. Break that loop by stating an estimate before reviewing representative data, then updating it using a consistent denominator. Emotions can still influence how much protection you value without determining the probability.

Gemini response · editorial edition

Our emotions and some inherent biases in our thinking can create a significant interplay that hinders accurate risk assessment. Here’s a breakdown of how this happens:

Emotional Influence:

  • Fear and Anxiety: A vivid possibility can feel imminent even when the relevant base rate is low. Fear can also be an appropriate warning. The error is inferring probability from intensity of feeling without examining the situation.
  • Overconfidence: Conversely, feeling safe or in control can lead to underestimating risks. This is especially true for familiar situations, even those with statistically higher risks (like car accidents).

Cognitive Biases:

  • Availability Bias: Memorable events can seem frequent because they are easy to recall. Comparing plane and car travel requires a common unit of exposure and a specified type of travel; media attention alone cannot provide that comparison.
  • Neglect of Delayed or Accumulating Harms: Threats with gradual effects may receive little attention because each small exposure feels inconsequential. This is a description of a pattern, not a single standardized bias called “neglect bias.”
  • Optimism Bias: We tend to believe we’re less likely to experience negative events than others, leading to underestimating personal risk (e.g., “identity theft won’t happen to me”).

The Interplay:

The interaction can also work in the opposite direction. Familiarity produces a feeling of control, that feeling encourages a low estimate of personal risk, and repeated uneventful experiences seem to confirm it. But avoiding a rare bad outcome a few times is weak evidence that the underlying process is safe.

Breaking the Cycle:

  • Be aware of your emotions: Recognize when fear or overconfidence might be influencing your judgment.
  • Seek information from reliable sources: Check how the source defines the event, who was counted, and the period covered. Use a relevant comparison group, seek contrary evidence, and distinguish a measured frequency from a model’s projection.
  • Consider the long term: Don’t just focus on immediate dangers. Understand the potential consequences of both slow-burn and immediate threats.
  • Imagine different scenarios: Think about how a situation might play out, considering both positive and negative outcomes.

Awareness helps most when it changes the procedure: write down the initial estimate, compare it with suitable data, and record why it changes. Simply naming biases after a bad outcome can become another way to explain everything without testing anything.

Prompt 3: Provide 5 examples from history in which a faulty risk assessment lead to injury or loss, including lost opportunities.

Five historical cases from each response

ChatGPT response · editorial edition

Faulty risk assessment has been a recurring theme throughout history, leading to consequences ranging from personal tragedies to large-scale disasters. Here are five notable examples where miscalculation of risks resulted in injury, loss, or lost opportunities:

  1. The Titanic Disaster (1912): Continuing at high speed despite ice reports and carrying too few lifeboat places exposed passengers to hazards the existing arrangements did not adequately address. The disaster killed more than 1,500 people. The lesson concerns warning response and capacity for evacuation; the popular “unsinkable” story alone does not establish why each decision was made. See the U.S. Senate inquiry record.
  2. The Challenger Space Shuttle Disaster (1986): Cold-temperature concerns about the solid-rocket booster joints and O-ring seals were not adequately incorporated into the launch decision. The Rogers Commission identified failures in communication and decision-making as well as the technical fault. Seven crew members died. The warning is against interpreting unresolved safety concerns as acceptable simply because previous flights succeeded.
  3. The Mortgage and Financial Crisis (2007–2008): Weak lending, leverage, and exposure to related housing risks left institutions vulnerable when defaults rose and markets deteriorated. Treating many securities as diversified can understate risk when the same housing shock affects them together. The crisis had multiple institutional causes; the lesson is to test dependence and loss scenarios rather than blame one mistaken forecast. See the Financial Crisis Inquiry Report.
  4. The Chernobyl Nuclear Disaster (1986): Reactor design characteristics, inadequate information and procedures, and wider safety failures combined during a test. The IAEA’s INSAG-7 reassessment corrects an account centered too narrowly on operators. Risks cannot be managed properly when dangerous system behavior is poorly understood or concealed by assumptions about available safeguards.
  5. The Mars Climate Orbiter Loss (1999): Ground software supplied impulse data in pound-force seconds where the navigation interface required newton-seconds. NASA’s investigation announcement describes the failure to detect and correct this mismatch. The spacecraft and its planned observations were lost. This illustrates both asset loss and a lost scientific opportunity: interface checks must test actual values and units, not merely assume that teams interpret a specification identically.

These cases show different failures: inadequate protective capacity, suppressed or poorly transmitted warnings, dependent financial exposures, misunderstood system behavior, and an unchecked interface. A bad outcome alone does not prove a decision was irrational. The relevant question is what information and reasonable safeguards were available before the event.

Gemini response · editorial edition

  1. Charge of the Light Brigade (1854): A misunderstood order sent cavalry toward the wrong guns under heavy fire. The surviving order and National Army Museum account support a communication failure, rather than a confident diagnosis of every commander’s optimism. Clarifying the target and reconciling different views of the battlefield were crucial missing checks.
  2. Bay of Pigs Invasion (1961): The invasion plan relied on assumptions about Cuban opposition, operational conditions, and the viability of the exile force that did not hold. The JFK Library account describes the failed operation. Testing those assumptions against adverse scenarios mattered more than confidence in the desired political outcome.
  3. Northridge Earthquake (1994): The defensible lesson concerns underestimated structural vulnerability, not ignored precise warnings that a quake was imminent. USGS reports describe unexpected fractures in steel-frame connections. Previously trusted construction methods needed reassessment. Earthquake preparedness must account for uncertainty in both local shaking and how buildings will respond.
  4. The Dot-Com Bubble (late 1990s–2000): Enthusiasm for internet growth supported valuations that many firms’ later earnings could not justify. Losses illustrate the difference between correctly anticipating an important technology and correctly valuing a particular business. A risk assessment must test financing needs, competition, and adverse revenue assumptions, not merely the appeal of a sector. The Federal Reserve’s retrospective discussion describes the collapse and its wider effects.
  5. The Challenger Space Shuttle Disaster (1986): The Rogers Commission documented a decision made with incomplete and sometimes misleading information about a known joint problem and cold-weather concerns. This duplicates a case in the other response but emphasizes organizational handling of uncertainty: a warning must reach the people authorized to stop the activity, with its strength and limitations intact.

Both losses and missed opportunities matter. Challenger cost lives and prevented the planned mission; the failed invasion lost an attempted political outcome as well as lives and resources. These are consequences, not claims that the hoped-for outcomes were otherwise guaranteed. Assess the decision process using what was knowable at the time.

Prompt 4: Provide a step-by-step process one can follow to ensure a highly accurate assessment of risks.

Two step-by-step assessment processes

ChatGPT response · editorial edition

No procedure can ensure highly accurate estimates when evidence is sparse or conditions change. A structured process can make assumptions visible, improve estimates, and reveal when a decision is sensitive to uncertainty. The eight steps below preserve the original sequence and separate estimating risk from choosing what to do about it.

1. Define the Context

  • Objective Setting: Clearly define the objectives of your risk assessment. Understand what you’re trying to achieve or protect.
  • Scope Identification: State the population or system, the outcome, and the time horizon. “Chance of equipment failure during one year of specified use” is testable in a way that “this equipment is risky” is not.

2. Identify Risks

  • Brainstorming: Use brainstorming sessions with stakeholders to identify as many risks as possible.
  • Consult Experts: Engage with experts who can provide insights into potential risks based on their experience and knowledge.
  • Review Historical Data: Start with comparable cases, including uneventful exposures and near misses. Record why the comparison may fail—for example, different operating conditions or missing reports.

3. Analyze Risks

  • Qualitative Analysis: Use likelihood-and-impact categories to organize incomplete information, with written definitions. A red box on a risk matrix is a screening signal, not a measured probability; do not treat multiplication of arbitrary category numbers as precise arithmetic.
  • Quantitative Analysis: Estimate probabilities and consequences with ranges where warranted, and model shared causes. For illustration, a 2% annual chance of a $10,000 loss gives a $200 expected annual loss, but the possible $10,000 loss still matters if it would be unaffordable. The average is not a promised outcome.

4. Evaluate Risks

  • Compare Against Risk Criteria: State the acceptable losses and constraints before selecting a favored option. Tolerance expresses a decision preference; it must not be used to alter the probability estimate until a preferred action appears safe.
  • Prioritize Risks: Consider severity, probability, reversibility, and who bears the harm. Test whether plausible changes in uncertain estimates alter the priority. A rare catastrophic loss may warrant protection even when its expected monetary loss is modest.

5. Develop Risk Treatment Plans

  • Identify Control Measures: Compare avoidance, reduction, insurance or contractual transfer, and acceptance with the consequences of doing nothing. Financial transfer does not remove the physical hazard, and a safeguard can introduce a new failure mode.
  • Allocate Resources: Determine the resources required for implementing control measures and allocate them accordingly.

6. Implement Risk Treatment Plans

  • Action Plan Execution: Implement the control measures according to the developed plans.
  • Communication: Ensure that all stakeholders are informed about the risks and the measures being taken.

7. Monitor and Review

  • Continuous Monitoring: Assign responsibility, track whether controls actually operate, and look for changes in exposure. A period without incidents does not by itself validate an estimate of a rare event.
  • Review and Update: Set review dates and triggers, such as a near miss, changed operating conditions, or new evidence. For repeated predictions, compare forecasts with outcomes across enough cases to assess calibration—whether events assigned similar probabilities occur at roughly those frequencies.

8. Document and Report

  • Documentation: Record sources, denominators, assumptions, uncertainty ranges, competing explanations, and the reasons for choosing a control. This allows a later reviewer to distinguish a poor decision from an unlucky outcome.
  • Reporting: Report the findings to relevant stakeholders, including management, investors, or regulatory bodies, as required.

Repeat the cycle when new information changes either the hazard or the confidence in its estimate. The aim is an assessment that can be inspected and corrected. When precise estimates remain unavailable, examine whether an affordable, reversible precaution works across several plausible scenarios rather than manufacturing certainty.

Gemini response · editorial edition

Here’s a step-by-step process to improve your risk assessment accuracy:

1. Identify the Risk:

  • Clearly define the decision or situation you’re evaluating.
  • Identify adverse outcomes, including losses caused by delay or inaction. Separate plausible scenarios from an unlimited list of imaginable events, and state the period and activity being assessed.

2. Gather Information:

  • Gather data with matching definitions, time periods, and exposure units. Include a range when the evidence does not support one precise estimate; record important gaps.
  • Look for historical data on similar situations, industry reports, and expert opinions from credible sources.

3. Analyze the Risk:

  • Likelihood: Begin with the frequency in genuinely comparable cases, then adjust for differences you can explain. Distinguish the uncertainty in your estimate from the probability of the event itself.
  • Impact: Describe the size, duration, and reversibility of the consequences and who bears them. Include a severe plausible scenario; an average loss can conceal a rare unaffordable outcome.

4. Consider Biases:

  • Be aware of how your emotions and cognitive biases might be influencing your judgment.
  • Seek a credible opposing interpretation and ask what evidence would change your estimate. This is stronger than simply asserting that you have “allowed for bias.”

5. Develop Control Measures:

  • Based on the likelihood and impact of each risk, brainstorm strategies to mitigate or eliminate them.
  • Can you avoid the risk altogether? Can you reduce the likelihood of it happening? Can you minimize the potential impact if it does occur?

6. Evaluate and Document:

  • Compare controls with the original risk, residual risk, cost, and new risks introduced. Check whether a plausible change in an uncertain assumption reverses the preferred decision.
  • Document your entire risk assessment process, including the identified risks, your analysis, and the chosen control measures. This will help with future monitoring and adjustments.

7. Monitor and Update:

  • Set dates or evidence thresholds for review. Compare repeated forecasts with outcomes, allowing for sample size and changes in conditions rather than treating one success as proof.
  • Are the control measures still effective? Do any new risks need to be considered?

These steps improve transparency and correctability; they cannot guarantee accuracy. If an important estimate remains uncertain, say so and examine decisions that remain acceptable across that uncertainty.
