Prompt 3: Was individual C rational to wait until the evidence reached a particular threshold to flip from disbelief to belief?

Confidence, action thresholds, and the curator’s corrections

ChatGPT response · editorial edition

Earlier answer under examination: The original response defended C’s threshold as potentially rational when a false belief would be costly. That defense conflated a degree of belief with a decision to act. The curator’s follow-ups below expose the distinction. A high action threshold can be appropriate while confidence continues to change.

For example, a hypothetical forecast might justify a 30% chance of rain. That estimate could support carrying a cheap umbrella while leaving an expensive outdoor event uncancelled. Different action thresholds do not require inventing different probabilities for the same forecast.

C’s line is labeled degree of belief, not action or public commitment. Read that way, staying near zero while relevant reliable evidence accumulates fails to represent the support already available. The subsequent jump also should not manufacture certainty beyond that support.

So C’s displayed confidence is not justified by the cost of error. A binary decision may wait for a threshold; a credence—a degree of confidence that a proposition is true—should still reflect the evidence. The following examples distinguish those two questions.

Provide three scenarios in which the cost of belief would be high, epistemically justifying disbelief.

  1. Medical diagnosis: In a hypothetical case, an unnecessary intervention could be costly. That supports careful assessment of whether to intervene, not deliberate disbelief in a condition that remains possible. Recognizing uncertainty can also matter because delay may have costs. This illustrates the belief/action distinction, not a clinical decision rule.
  2. Criminal justice: In United States criminal proceedings, conviction requires proof beyond a reasonable doubt; In re Winship grounds that protection in due process. Not meeting that standard is not equivalent to establishing that the accused certainly did not commit the act. The example concerns the difference between a verdict standard and a factual assessment.
  3. Scientific research: A premature causal announcement can misdirect further work. A researcher may assign a plausible but uncertain causal explanation some weight while withholding a strong public claim. Costly error justifies care about evidence and communication, not assigning zero credibility until a preferred threshold is crossed.

In your three secnarios, what would be the actual danger of mapping one’s belief to the degree of the evidence?

There is no inherent danger in accurately mapping confidence to the evidential support. The original examples described errors in assessing evidence or acting on it, not a defect in accurate credence. In the same three settings:

  1. Medical diagnosis: Treating a tentative estimate as certainty can produce an ill-judged decision. The mistake is the unjustified certainty or the action rule, not a probability that faithfully represents current evidence.
  2. Criminal justice: Ignoring exculpatory evidence or treating repeated dependent testimony as independent confirmation distorts the assessment. A careful evidence-based assessment can coexist with the required protection against conviction on insufficient proof.
  3. Scientific research: Announcing a tentative association as an established cause confuses a limited finding with a stronger conclusion. Updating confidence accurately while reporting uncertainty avoids that error.

But a credence is not a decision. Wouldn’t more accurate credences better inform the decisions?

Yes. Your distinction is decisive: a credence is not a decision. Better grounded confidence gives a decision-maker a better representation of uncertainty; it does not itself choose an action. My earlier defense of disbelief on the ground that a mistaken action would be costly did not establish what it claimed.

Evidence quality is part of assessing what confidence is warranted. Introducing unreliable evidence after the scenario has stipulated reliability changes the problem rather than answering your objection.

For instance, in the scenarios I described:

  1. Medical diagnosis: A realistic assessment of uncertainty informs the comparison of possible actions. Inflating or suppressing confidence to make an action feel comfortable makes that comparison less informative.
  2. Criminal justice: Assessing the evidence and applying the standard for conviction are distinct tasks. Caution about the latter does not require pretending that the evidence provides no information.
  3. Scientific research: A finding can raise or lower confidence before it warrants a settled conclusion. Maintaining the distinction allows further investigation without either premature certainty or blanket disbelief.

More accurate credences improve the information available for choice. Costs then bear on what to do with that information. They do not, just by being high, become evidence that a proposition is false.

All things being equal, including the credibility of the evidence, is it not better to update your priors as new confirming and disconfirming evidence arrives?

Yes. When new evidence changes the support for a claim, confidence should respond in the appropriate direction. In Bayesian updating, prior odds are multiplied by how much more likely that evidence is under the claim than under its alternative; this is not a rule to raise confidence merely because another report arrived.

For a hypothetical illustration, prior odds of 1:1 become 3:1 if new evidence is three times as likely under the claim as under its alternative. The resulting probability is 75%. Repeating the same report adds no independent evidence and does not warrant applying the multiplier again.

The benefit is responsiveness to support, not constant motion for its own sake. Evidence equally likely under both alternatives may leave confidence unchanged. The chart stipulates that the relevant balance changes, so C cannot invoke that exception.

Then, based on your last response, it would have been more rational for C to have updated their credences to map to the changing balance of evidence, right?

Yes. Because the chart depicts changes in warranted confidence, C should have updated as that balance changed. A high threshold for taking some action would not excuse keeping the degree of belief near zero.

C could remain cautious about acting while assigning the claim intermediate confidence. This is the distinction your follow-ups establish: evidence governs credence; the consequences of acting or waiting also enter the separate decision.

Provide a salient reformulation of the notion that rational belief is a degree of belief that maps to the degree of the relevant evidence.

Here is a salient reformulation of the notion that rational belief is a degree of belief that maps to the degree of the relevant evidence:

Rational belief calibrates conviction proportionally to justification.

The confidence assigned to a claim should fit the evidence bearing on it. Excessive confidence and excessive disbelief can both be mistakes. A low probability is not automatically more rational merely because it sounds cautious.

When new evidence changes the balance, revise confidence accordingly. That requires evaluating the evidence’s bearing on the claim; it is not mechanically copying the amount of information or the number of people repeating it.

The concise formulation expresses the chart’s central ideal. It does not promise that every real disagreement has a uniquely measurable evidential level or that people can read exact probabilities directly from observations.

The practical lesson is to ask two questions separately: “How well supported is this claim?” and “Given that uncertainty, what should I do?” The graphic addresses the first; the attempted defense of C repeatedly substituted the second.
