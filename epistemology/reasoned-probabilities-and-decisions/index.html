Prompt 1: Should I increase my confidence in a claim if a higher confidence in the claim increases my chance of acquiring the benefits behind the claim? For example, many religions suggest I should increase my belief in God, not based on the arrival of greater evidence for God, but based on the notion I won’t be able to otherwise acquire the “salvation” of that God. Is this rational?Should the act of making a decision change the probability assessments on which the decision was made? In other words, if I assess I have a 10% chance of surviving a fight with the bear chasing me and a 15% chance of surviving an attempted jump over a gorge to escape the bear, should my decision to jump over the gorge increase my initial 15% confidence I’ll survive the gorge jump?

Confidence, benefits and choices

ChatGPT response · editorial edition

Increasing Confidence Based on Potential Benefits

The proposition suggests increasing belief in a claim (e.g., religious belief) not based on new evidence but because of the potential benefits (e.g., salvation). From a purely rational perspective focused on evidence and truth, it is not traditionally rational to adjust the confidence in a claim based solely on the benefits of believing that claim. Rational belief formation is ideally guided by evidence and the likelihood of the claim being true, not by the potential outcomes of holding that belief.

Practical reasons can justify choosing an action or commitment under uncertainty without increasing the probability assigned to its underlying claim. A promised reward for belief is not evidence that the promise is true.

Decision-Making and Probability Assessments

In the scenario of deciding between facing a bear and attempting a jump over a gorge, the act of making a decision itself does not logically alter the initial probability assessments. If you assess a 10% chance of surviving the bear and a 15% chance of surviving the jump, choosing to jump does not inherently increase the 15% survival probability. Your assessment is based on your understanding of the situation before making the decision.

Hold the information and action conditions fixed. Choosing the jump does not raise an already assessed P(survival if jumping)=0.15 merely through commitment. A decision that changes preparation or reveals new information may justify a new conditional assessment; those are different cases.

Conclusion

Within the constraints of the scenarios provided:

  1. It is not traditionally rational to increase confidence in a claim solely based on the benefits of believing that claim, without new evidence supporting its truth.
  2. The bare choice does not warrant inflating the probability already conditional on that choice. Changes in causal conditions or information can warrant updates.

Gemini response · editorial edition

Modifying your probability assessment without new evidence based on a decision is generally considered irrational.

There’s a key distinction between making a decision and acquiring new information. When you make a choice, you’re acting on the information you already have. But that information itself shouldn’t change simply because you’ve acted.

In the stipulated example, choosing the jump does not by itself raise its 15% survival chance. Selecting between actions with different conditional risks does change which risk you face; that is different from improving the selected action’s conditional chance.

Here’s a breakdown of why modifying probability this way is irrational:

  • Inaccurate representation of reality: It doesn’t reflect the actual likelihood of the event.
  • Inconsistent decision-making: It can lead to changing probabilities for similar situations in the future, making your choices less reliable.

However, there are nuances to consider:

  • New emotional information: Fear in the face of the bear might feel like “new information,” even though it doesn’t directly change the jump’s risk. This can influence your perception of the situation.
  • Limited initial assessment: Your initial probabilities might not have captured all factors. Maybe you underestimated your jumping skills upon re-evaluation.

Here’s a better approach when making decisions:

  • Separate assessment and action: Acknowledge your initial probability assessments and make decisions based on that information.
  • Reassess when relevant information or causal conditions change, including during the current decision. Correcting a discovered calculation error can also justify revision.

In conclusion, stick to modifying probabilities based on actual new evidence, not just because you made a decision. This will lead to more consistent and reliable decision-making in the long run.

Prompt 3: How can we describe in psychological terms the drive to increase the assessed probabilities in the direction of a decision based on those probabilities?

Post-decision biases

ChatGPT response · editorial edition

The phenomenon you’re describing, where individuals adjust their probability assessments in favor of a decision they have made, can be understood through several psychological concepts and biases:

1. Confirmation Bias

Confirmation bias is the tendency to search for, interpret, favor, and recall information in a way that confirms one’s preexisting beliefs or hypotheses. After making a decision, individuals are prone to unconsciously increase their perceived probability of success because they seek out information that supports the decision they have made and ignore information that contradicts it.

2. Post-Decision Rationalization or Choice-Supportive Bias

This is a type of cognitive bias where people tend to retroactively ascribe positive attributes to the option they chose and negative attributes to the options they did not choose. This can lead to a distorted memory of why a decision was made, making the chosen option seem more desirable than it was at the time of the decision. This bias can manifest as an increased belief in the likelihood of success of the chosen option.

3. Cognitive Dissonance

Cognitive dissonance refers to the discomfort experienced when holding two or more conflicting cognitions (beliefs, attitudes, or values). After making a decision, especially a difficult one between closely matched alternatives, individuals might experience dissonance between the recognition of the risks involved and their desire to have made the right choice. To reduce this discomfort, they may increase their confidence in the probability of the decision leading to a positive outcome.

4. Self-Justification

Self-justification theory explains how individuals justify and rationalize their decisions, often in the face of contradictory evidence. This can lead to a reevaluation of the probabilities associated with the decision to make it appear more rational and justified in hindsight.

5. Optimism Bias

Optimism bias: A tendency to overestimate favorable outcomes in some contexts. It may contribute to inflated confidence after a choice, but it is not a universal innate response and cannot be diagnosed from the hypothetical alone.

Conclusion

These psychological mechanisms can lead individuals to adjust their perceived probabilities in favor of a decision after it has been made. It’s a way of aligning one’s beliefs and expectations with their chosen course of action, often unconsciously, to maintain consistency, reduce cognitive dissonance, and support their self-image as a rational decision-maker.

Gemini response · editorial edition

The tendency to increase the assessed probabilities in the direction of a decision based on those probabilities can be described by several psychological terms:

1. Confirmation bias: This is a well-documented cognitive bias where people favor information that confirms their existing beliefs or decisions, while disregarding or downplaying information that contradicts them. In your example, after choosing to jump the gorge, someone might subconsciously focus on positive aspects of their decision, like their athletic ability, while neglecting factors like potential hidden obstacles that could increase their risk.

Motivated reasoning: Desire for a good outcome can shape interpretation of evidence—for example, inflating the jump’s survival estimate or selectively emphasizing reasons the alternative looks worse. This is a possible mechanism, not a justification for changing the estimate.

3. Belief perseverance: This refers to the tendency for people to cling to their existing beliefs even in the face of contradictory evidence. After making a decision, individuals might exhibit a stronger conviction in the chosen option, even if the initial probabilities remain unchanged. This can stem from a need for cognitive consistency or a desire to avoid admitting potential errors in judgment.

4. Self-serving bias: This bias leads people to attribute positive outcomes to their own skills and abilities, but negative outcomes to external factors beyond their control. If someone survives the jump, they might attribute it to excellent jumping skills, further solidifying their initial belief in their higher chances of survival, even if the original assessment might have been inaccurate.

It’s important to note that these psychological tendencies are not always conscious or deliberate. They often operate subtly and unconsciously, influencing our decision-making processes and potentially leading to biased assessments of probabilities.

By being aware of these biases, we can become more critical thinkers and make better decisions by consciously seeking out and considering all available information, not just the information that confirms our initial choices.

Kierkegaard and commitment

ChatGPT response · editorial edition

Subjectivity is Truth

Against Objective Certainty

Kierkegaard critiqued the excessive emphasis on objective certainty and rational evidence in understanding faith and existential choices. He argued that such an approach misses the essence of faith, which involves embracing uncertainty and making a personal commitment beyond what can be empirically verified or logically deduced.

Relevance to the Notion of Decision and Credence

The relevant comparison is commitment despite uncertainty. It does not follow that choosing to commit supplies new evidence or legitimately raises a numerical credence.

Kierkegaard can illuminate the existential meaning of commitment. Whether that commitment warrants belief beyond the evidence is a separate philosophical dispute, and the bear example does not resolve it.
