Prompt 1: Is there any better measure of rationality than predictive power?

Prediction and rationality

ChatGPT response · editorial edition

The question of whether there’s a better measure of rationality than predictive power touches on a complex debate within philosophy, cognitive science, and decision theory. Rationality is a multifaceted concept that can be understood and measured in different ways, depending on the context. Predictive power—how well one’s beliefs or models can forecast outcomes—is indeed a crucial aspect of rationality, particularly in empirical and scientific contexts. It emphasizes the alignment of one’s beliefs with observable reality and the ability to make accurate predictions about future events.

However, considering rationality solely through the lens of predictive power might be limiting in capturing its full essence, especially in contexts where prediction is less central or more challenging. Here are some alternative or complementary measures and aspects of rationality to consider:

  1. Coherence: This aspect looks at the internal consistency of a person’s beliefs, decisions, and actions. Are they logically consistent with one another? Coherence is fundamental to rational thought and decision-making, ensuring that one’s beliefs do not contradict each other.
  2. Bayesian Rationality: Beyond just making predictions, Bayesian rationality involves updating one’s beliefs with new evidence in a principled way. It emphasizes the process of belief revision as new information becomes available, which is a key component of rational thinking.
  3. Ethical evaluation: Whether goals or actions are morally acceptable is a distinct question from whether beliefs fit evidence or means efficiently serve goals. Ethical goodness should not silently become a measure of predictive or epistemic rationality.
  4. Instrumental Rationality: This aspect focuses on the effectiveness of actions in achieving one’s goals. It’s not just about predicting outcomes but also about choosing the means that most effectively lead to the desired ends.
  5. Epistemic Rationality: This is about having beliefs that are justified and well-supported by evidence. It goes beyond predictive accuracy to consider the quality and source of the evidence underlying one’s beliefs.
  6. Practical Wisdom (Phronesis): In some philosophical traditions, rationality is also about the practical wisdom to navigate complex social and moral situations, where strict logical consistency or predictive power may not be the most relevant measures.

Each of these aspects highlights a different dimension of rationality, suggesting that a comprehensive understanding of rationality might require considering multiple measures and perspectives. The choice of which aspect to emphasize may depend on the specific context or domain in question.

Gemini response · editorial edition

Predictive power is a strong indicator of rationality, but it’s not the whole picture. Here’s why:

  • Limited scope: Predictive power focuses on outcomes, not the reasoning process. A lucky guess or good intuition can lead to accurate predictions, but it doesn’t necessarily reflect a well-thought-out, logical approach.
  • Missing factors: External factors can influence outcomes besides the decision itself. Someone might make a perfectly rational choice based on the information available, but things outside their control lead to an unexpected result.

Here are some other aspects to consider when evaluating rationality:

  • Consistency: Assess beliefs and choices under comparable information and aims. Changing a choice after learning relevant facts is not itself inconsistency.
  • Updating beliefs: Does new information lead to adjustments in thinking? Clinging to outdated beliefs despite evidence to the contrary suggests a lack of rationality.
  • Considering all options: Were all relevant factors weighed before a decision? Jumping to conclusions without a thorough analysis is not a hallmark of rationality.

In conclusion, predictive power is a valuable tool, but a multifaceted approach that considers reasoning, consistency, and adaptability is likely a better measure of true rationality.

Prompt 2: Let me be more precise: Superior predictive power over the long term is the best measure of the degree of rationality one possesses. Correct?

Long-term performance

ChatGPT response · editorial edition

Positioning superior predictive power over the long term as the best measure of rationality is a compelling argument, especially within contexts where outcomes can be clearly defined and measured. This view aligns well with a pragmatic approach to rationality, emphasizing outcomes and the ability to accurately forecast future events based on one’s beliefs and models of the world. Here’s why this perspective can be considered strong, along with some nuances to consider:

Arguments in Favor

  1. Empirical validation: Sustained predictive performance supports the forecasting method in the tested domain. Correlation-based prediction can work without correct causal understanding, so success does not by itself establish a causal model.
  2. Adaptive Success: From an evolutionary perspective, the ability to make accurate predictions is crucial for survival and adaptation. In this sense, predictive power could be seen as a fundamental aspect of rationality, as it directly impacts one’s ability to navigate and succeed in various environments.
  3. Measurement: Register forecasts before outcomes, specify resolution rules, include failures, compare equivalent tasks and information, and use a suitable scoring rule. Long duration alone does not remove selection bias or unequal access to data.

Nuances and Considerations

While superior predictive power is a strong indicator of rationality, particularly in empirical and scientific contexts, there are some important nuances to consider:

  • Complex Systems: In highly complex or chaotic systems, predictability may be inherently limited, not necessarily reflecting a lack of rationality but the limits of current knowledge or the system’s complexity.
  • Epistemic Rationality: Rationality also involves the process of forming beliefs based on evidence and reasoning. Even if predictions fail in the short term, the rationality of the underlying process—how beliefs are formed and updated—remains significant.
  • Value-Driven Decisions: In decisions involving ethical, moral, or personal values, predictive power might not be the sole or most appropriate measure of rationality. How well decisions align with one’s values and principles can also reflect a form of rationality.
  • Short-Term vs. Long-Term Predictions: Some decisions or beliefs might not demonstrate their predictive power in the short term but prove to be rational in the long run. Distinguishing between short-term and long-term outcomes is crucial in evaluating rationality from this perspective.

In summary, while superior predictive power over the long term is a compelling measure of rationality, especially in contexts where outcomes can be clearly forecasted and evaluated, it’s also important to consider the broader and more nuanced aspects of rationality. These include the processes of belief formation and revision, the complexity of the systems involved, and the role of values and ethics in decision-making.

Gemini response · editorial edition

Even with a long-term focus, superior predictive power isn’t the absolute best measure of rationality. Here’s why:

  • Unforeseen events: The world is complex and unpredictable. Black swan events, or completely unexpected situations, can throw off even the most well-reasoned predictions.
  • Access to information: A forecaster with private data may outperform an equally careful reasoner with less information. Evaluate the process relative to available evidence and keep domain skill distinct from a universal personal ranking.
  • Uncertainty: Some forecasting tasks involve irreducible randomness or practical limits on information. Rational probability forecasts need not predict every individual outcome correctly.

However, focusing on long-term predictive power does strengthen the argument. Here’s why it’s a strong indicator:

  • Repeated success: Out-of-sample performance on relevant, comparably selected cases is stronger evidence than a few hits. It supports predictive skill without proving universal rationality or causal understanding.
  • Adaptability: Long-term success often requires adjusting predictions based on new information and changing circumstances. This highlights a key aspect of rationality – the ability to learn and adapt.

While not perfect, long-term predictive power is a very good indicator of rationality.

Here’s the takeaway: Look for a combination of factors. Does someone consistently make accurate predictions based on a sound reasoning process? Are they open to new information and willing to adapt their thinking? This multifaceted approach will give you a more complete picture of rationality than just focusing on outcomes.

Prompt 3: If there are competing measures of rationality in addition to the predictive power one displays, what are those measures, and how do they allow us to quantify the degree of rationality one has?

Quantifying alternative measures

ChatGPT response · editorial edition

Competing measures of rationality extend beyond predictive power to encompass various aspects of thought and decision-making processes. These measures offer different lenses through which to assess rationality, each with its own criteria for what constitutes rational thought or behavior. While quantifying the degree of rationality can be more straightforward in some domains (like predictive accuracy), in others, it often involves qualitative assessments or comparisons against normative standards. Here are key measures and how they might allow for quantification or assessment:

1. Coherence

  • Definition: The internal consistency of a person’s beliefs, desires, and actions. A coherent system of beliefs does not contain contradictions.
  • Quantification: For complementary events, measure |P(A)+P(not A)−1|. Assigning 0.8 to A and 0.7 to not-A gives a coherence discrepancy of 0.5, detectable without observing an outcome. This is a limited diagnostic, not a complete rationality score.

2. Consistency with Normative Models

  • Definition: Adherence to established normative models of decision-making, such as expected utility theory in economics or Bayesian updating in statistics.
  • Quantification: With specified priors, likelihoods and utilities, compare a reported posterior or chosen action with the model’s result. For example, report absolute posterior error or expected-utility loss. The benchmark assumptions must be justified separately.

3. Epistemic Rationality

  • Definition: Holding beliefs that are well-supported by evidence and reasoning.
  • Quantification: In a controlled task with explicit evidence and assumptions, compare stated confidence with the supported posterior. Counting sources is not enough: sources can share errors or differ in relevance and reliability.

4. Instrumental Rationality

  • Definition: The effectiveness of actions in achieving one’s goals, considering the available information and resources.
  • Quantification: Compare expected utility of the chosen action with the best available action under stated probabilities and preferences. Realized success also matters as evidence, but one lucky outcome does not establish a good choice.

5. Decision Quality under Uncertainty

  • Definition: Making optimal decisions when outcomes are uncertain, often requiring balancing risks and rewards.
  • Quantification: This can be assessed through methods like decision analysis, which quantifies the expected outcomes of different decisions under uncertainty, and can compare actual decisions made to the theoretically optimal ones.

6. Practical Reasoning and Pragmatism

  • Definition: The ability to make decisions that are not only logically consistent but also practical and applicable in real-world situations.
  • Quantification: More qualitative by nature, though one could look at outcomes and adaptability of decisions in real-world contexts to assess practical reasoning skills.

7. Meta-Rationality

  • Definition: The capacity to reflect on and improve one’s own decision-making processes and cognitive strategies.
  • Quantification: This involves assessing one’s awareness of their cognitive biases, willingness to update beliefs, and ability to choose between different reasoning methods appropriate to the context. Measures might include self-assessment tools, behavioral experiments, or peer evaluations.

Quantifying rationality across these dimensions often involves a mix of quantitative and qualitative methods, from formal mathematical modeling to psychological assessments and performance evaluations in specific tasks. The challenge lies in the multifaceted nature of rationality, requiring a holistic approach that acknowledges its various dimensions.

Gemini response · editorial edition

You’re right, predictive power isn’t the only measure of rationality. Here are some other important aspects to consider, though quantifying them perfectly can be challenging:

1. Coherence:

  • Description: Do a person’s beliefs and actions align with each other? Someone who holds contradictory beliefs or makes choices that undermine their stated goals displays a lack of coherence.
  • Quantification: This can be measured through scenario-based tests where individuals are presented with situations that might reveal inconsistencies in their thinking. Scoring can involve identifying contradictions and assessing the overall logical flow of their responses.

2. Epistemic Humility:

  • Description: This refers to the awareness of the limitations of one’s knowledge and the willingness to consider alternative viewpoints. Someone who is rigidly attached to their beliefs and dismissive of new information lacks epistemic humility.
  • Quantification: This is more subjective but can be assessed through surveys or interviews that gauge a person’s openness to opposing viewpoints and their comfort level admitting they might not have all the answers.

3. Efficiency:

  • Description: This refers to the ability to make sound decisions with limited information and resources. It involves considering all relevant factors and choosing the course of action that maximizes desired outcomes.
  • Quantification: Efficiency can be measured through simulations or games where individuals make choices with limited information and clear goals. Scoring can involve tracking the effectiveness of their actions and the resources they used.

It’s important to note that these measures are often intertwined. For example, someone with high epistemic humility might be more likely to consider multiple perspectives and thus arrive at more coherent beliefs.

Challenges in Quantification:

Quantifying these measures perfectly is difficult. Here’s why:

  • Subjectivity: There’s an element of subjectivity in interpreting responses and assigning scores.
  • Limited scenarios: Tests and simulations can’t capture the full complexity of real-world decision-making.
  • Individual differences: People have different cognitive styles and approaches to problem-solving, making it hard to create a one-size-fits-all measure.

The Ideal Approach:

The best approach likely involves a combination of methods. We can use:

  • Predictive tracking: Record probability forecasts in advance and score all resolved events. For binary outcomes y ∈ {0,1}, the mean of (p−y)² is the binary Brier score; lower is better. A forecast p=0.8 scores 0.04 if the event occurs and 0.64 if it does not. This convention is half the original two-category sum; see Brier’s paper.
  • Standardized tests: Use scenario-based assessments to gauge coherence and efficiency.
  • Interviews and surveys: Assess epistemic humility through discussions about the limitations of knowledge and receptiveness to new information.

By looking at all these aspects, we can get a more complete picture of someone’s rationality, even though a single, definitive score might not be achievable.

Prompt 4: It appears all of the “competing measures of rationality” you introduced rely on predictive power for their own measurements of usefulness, right?

Measurement versus usefulness

ChatGPT response · editorial edition

The relationship between predictive power and other measures of rationality is nuanced. While predictive power is indeed central to many aspects of rationality, especially in empirical and scientific contexts, not all measures of rationality rely solely on predictive power for their assessment of usefulness. Let’s explore this relationship in more detail:

Coherence and Consistency with Normative Models

  • Coherence can be measured directly without predictive outcomes, as with complementary probabilities that fail to sum to one. Whether teaching that constraint improves real forecasting is a separate empirical question about the measure’s practical usefulness.

Epistemic and Instrumental Rationality

  • Epistemic rationality focuses on holding beliefs that are justified by evidence. While effective prediction can be a result of epistemically rational beliefs, the measure here is more about the process of belief formation and adjustment in light of evidence, rather than the predictive outcomes per se.
  • Instrumental rationality is about choosing effective means to achieve one’s ends. Predictive power can play a role in determining the effectiveness of these means, but the measure itself is about the suitability and efficiency of chosen actions towards achieving goals, which might not always be assessed purely through predictive success.

Decision Quality under Uncertainty and Practical Reasoning

  • Decision quality under uncertainty involves making optimal decisions where outcomes are uncertain. This measure evaluates how well decisions are made given the available information and the uncertain nature of outcomes. Predictive power is relevant, as better predictions can lead to better decisions, but the focus is also on how uncertainty is managed and navigated.
  • Practical reasoning and pragmatism emphasize applicability and adaptability in real-world contexts. The utility of decisions in practical terms may depend on successful outcomes (which prediction can influence), but it’s also about how decisions are implemented and adapted based on changing circumstances.

Meta-Rationality

  • Meta-rationality involves reflecting on and improving one’s reasoning and decision-making processes. While improved predictive power can be a result of better meta-rational processes, the measure itself is more about the capacity for self-reflection and cognitive flexibility.

The distinction is between measuring a property and demonstrating its usefulness. A formal inconsistency can be identified without a forecast outcome; evidence about whether a training method improves forecasting requires performance evaluation. Neither point establishes a single universally best measure of rationality.

Gemini response · editorial edition

That’s a very good point. You’re right, on closer inspection, many of the proposed measures do rely on predictive power to some extent. Here’s why:

  • Coherence: Mutually exclusive and exhaustive probabilities that sum to more than one reveal a formal inconsistency without waiting for outcomes. Whether a person’s inconsistent-looking purchases conflict with their aims requires knowing their other goals and constraints.
  • Epistemic Humility: While directly assessed through openness to new information, epistemic humility can also indirectly influence long-term predictive power. Someone who considers alternative viewpoints is more likely to avoid biases that could lead to inaccurate predictions.

So, predictive power does seem to be a foundational element in many ways to assess rationality. However, there might still be some wiggle room:

  • Efficiency: This can be measured through simulations that don’t necessarily rely on real-world outcomes. For instance, a puzzle could be designed to assess how well someone allocates resources to reach a solution.
  • Direct assessment of reasoning: Logic puzzles or tests that evaluate critical thinking skills could be used, although these might not fully capture how someone reasons in real-world situations.

The Challenge:

The challenge is not that no direct measures exist. It is how to justify their normative importance and establish how well they transfer to real tasks. Predictive performance is central to the latter question when forecasting is the objective.

Here’s an alternative way to think about it: Maybe predictive power is a core aspect of rationality, but it’s not the whole story. Other factors like epistemic humility and efficiency influence how well someone translates their rationality into successful outcomes.

Ultimately, the goal is to get a comprehensive picture. While a perfect, universally accepted measure of rationality might be elusive, using a combination of approaches can help us understand someone’s decision-making process and the underlying thought patterns that guide them.

Prompt 5: If I apply the other factors you mention such as “logical coherence”, “adherence to normative models”, and “the process of belief formation and revision” arbitrarily without assessing the predictive success of such factors, I am acting irrational, correct?

Arbitrary application

ChatGPT response · editorial edition

Arbitrary application without a relevant justification is open to criticism. But not every justification requires first measuring predictive success: a proof can establish a formal constraint, while empirical claims about the usefulness of a method need empirical testing.

Logical Coherence

  • While logical coherence is crucial for a rational set of beliefs or actions (since contradictory beliefs are not rational by most standards), coherence alone does not guarantee that those beliefs or actions will be effective or true in the real world. Rationality also involves an engagement with empirical reality, where predictive success can serve as a key test.

Adherence to Normative Models

  • Normative models provide conditional benchmarks. Poor outcomes can reveal mistaken probabilities, utilities, model assumptions, or implementation; they do not automatically refute probability rules or show that a single decision was irrational.

The Process of Belief Formation and Revision

  • A rational process of belief formation and revision involves not just the internal logic of one’s reasoning or its alignment with certain standards, but also how this reasoning stands up to empirical tests. A process that never adjusts beliefs in the face of contradictory evidence, or fails to anticipate future events or outcomes, may not be fully rational.

Acting Without Assessing Predictive Success

  • Ignoring the predictive success of your beliefs and actions means ignoring an important feedback mechanism about the accuracy and utility of your models of the world. Rationality involves a feedback loop where beliefs are adjusted based on outcomes, and decisions are informed by past successes and failures. Without this loop, there’s a risk of adhering to beliefs or strategies that are ineffective or disconnected from reality.

Conclusion

A formal rule should have an explicit rationale and be applied under suitable assumptions. Claims that following it improves empirical forecasts need fair performance tests. Logical conformity alone and realized outcomes alone answer different, incomplete questions.

Gemini response · editorial edition

That’s not necessarily true. Here’s why:

  • Independent Value: Logical coherence, adherence to normative models, and the process of belief formation and revision all have independent value in understanding rationality, even if they don’t directly assess predictive success.
    • Logical Coherence: Ensuring beliefs and actions don’t contradict each other is a core principle of rationality. Someone can be logically consistent even if their predictions turn out wrong due to unforeseen circumstances.
    • Normative models: Adherence is informative only when the model and its application are justified. Merely following a rule labeled “rational” does not establish sound thinking.
    • Belief Formation and Revision: Rationality involves being receptive to new information and adjusting beliefs accordingly. Analyzing this process provides insights into how someone reasons and learns, regardless of the specific outcome.
  • Predictive Power as a Byproduct: While not the direct focus, these factors can indirectly contribute to predictive power.
    • Logical Coherence: Leads to clear and consistent plans, which can be a foundation for accurate predictions.
    • Normative models: Some are defended through explicit axioms or mathematical arguments rather than only past success. Whether they improve outcomes in a particular application remains an empirical question.
    • Belief Formation and Revision: Allows for adaptation to changing circumstances, crucial for long-term predictive success.

However, there are limitations to consider:

  • Limited Scope: These factors may not capture the full picture. Someone might be logically consistent but fail to consider crucial information, leading to inaccurate predictions.
  • Real-World Application: Normative models might not always be practical in complex situations.

The Best Approach:

The most effective approach likely involves a balance:

  • Consider both process and outcome: Analyze the reasoning process (coherence, adherence to models, belief revision) and track the long-term predictive power of those decisions.
  • Context matters: Consider the specific situation and the limitations of each measure.

In conclusion, while focusing solely on these factors without any consideration of predictive success might not be the most comprehensive approach to evaluating rationality, they still provide valuable insights into the thought processes that underlie decision making.

Prompt 6: Please be clear. How is the value of those other factors you propose as essential to assessing rationality themselves assessed if not through predictive power?

Answering the value challenge

ChatGPT response · editorial edition

There are two questions: how to measure a factor, and why to value it. Coherence can be measured by formal constraints; its value can be defended by arguments about consistency or accuracy under stated assumptions. Claims that a method improves real-world forecasting require empirical validation. These justifications should be stated, not hidden in the label “intrinsic.”

Logical Coherence

  • Intrinsically: The value of logical coherence is assessed through the consistency and non-contradictoriness of a set of beliefs or propositions. This assessment is internal and logical in nature. It’s a foundational principle that beliefs should not be mutually contradictory if they are to be considered rational.
  • Through Predictive Power: Indirectly, coherent belief systems are more likely to be reliable and useful in making predictions, because inconsistent beliefs can lead to erratic or contradictory predictions. However, the primary assessment of coherence is logical, not empirical.

Adherence to Normative Models

  • Formal assessment: Compare a response with the result of a specified normative model. Justification: explain the model’s axioms and why they fit the task. Agreement with a model is not its own independent justification.
  • Through Predictive Power: The utility of adhering to these models can also be validated by their success in guiding actions that achieve desired outcomes or accurately predict future states. However, the initial adherence is based on a normative judgment about what constitutes rational decision-making, rather than empirical success alone.

The Process of Belief Formation and Revision

  • Process assessment: In a task with explicit likelihoods and prior odds, calculate posterior odds and compare the participant’s update. The calculation can be checked before the event resolves; the adequacy of the likelihood model is a separate empirical issue.
  • Through Predictive Power: Over time, a sound process of belief formation and revision should lead to more accurate beliefs about the world, as it involves correcting errors and updating beliefs in light of new evidence. While the process itself is evaluated based on its rationality and adherence to good reasoning practices, its long-term value is also demonstrated through improved understanding and prediction of the world.

Conclusion

Predictive power is a strong practical test for forecasting methods. It is not required to identify every formal inconsistency or conditional mathematical error. Conversely, formal correctness does not establish a model’s empirical usefulness. This answers the challenge without asserting that all rival measures are independently validated universal scores.

Gemini response · editorial edition

Your challenge calls for a justification, not another list of labels. Formal constraints can be checked directly, while claims of practical usefulness require evidence appropriate to the claimed benefit.

Here are some alternative approaches, though they have limitations:

  • Thought Experiments: We can create hypothetical scenarios and evaluate if someone’s reasoning process aligns with principles of logic and sound decision-making. This wouldn’t involve real-world outcomes, but it tests their ability to apply rationality in a controlled setting.
  • Expert evaluation: Experts can apply published scoring criteria, but authority alone does not establish those criteria’s value. Report the rationale, agreement between assessors, and evidence of transfer to the target task.

Limitations:

  • Subjectivity: Both thought experiments and expert evaluation can be subjective. There might be different interpretations of “rational” responses to scenarios.
  • Limited Scope: These methods might not capture the complexity of real-world decision-making.

Here’s another way to think about it: Maybe these factors demonstrate the potential for rationality.

  • Logical coherence suggests someone can reason consistently and avoid contradictions.
  • Following a justified normative model demonstrates conformity to that model; whether it improves real decisions must be investigated rather than assumed.
  • Examining belief revision reveals openness to new information and the ability to adapt.

These qualities are all essential for making rational decisions, but they don’t guarantee perfect predictions. The actual outcomes depend on factors beyond just the rationality of the decision-making process.

The Importance of Context:

The “value” of these factors also depends on the context.

  • In a situation with perfect information and predictable outcomes, logical coherence is likely highly valuable.
  • In a complex situation with limited information and uncertainty, adaptability (reflected in belief revision) might be more important.

The Overall Picture:

Ultimately, assessing rationality is a multifaceted endeavor. There’s no single perfect measure. We need to consider:

  • Predictive power: Scores registered forecasts against resolved outcomes. Decision success additionally depends on goals, actions, information and chance.
  • Process analysis: Evaluates factors like logical coherence and belief revision using thought experiments or expert judgment.
  • Context: Considers the specific situation and the limitations of each measure.

By looking at all these aspects, we can build a more comprehensive picture of someone’s rationality, even if a single, definitive score remains elusive.
