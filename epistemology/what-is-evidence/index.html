Prompt 1: The concept of evidence seems to be too subjective in its usage to have any value in discussions. How might we approach the construction and proposal of a definition of “evidence ” that can be conventionally accepted and applied?

Defining evidence

ChatGPT response · editorial edition

To address the concern of subjectivity in the usage of “evidence” and to work towards a conventionally accepted and applicable definition, a structured approach can be adopted. This process would involve several key steps:

  1. Establish a Broad Understanding of Evidence:
  • A working definition: evidence is information that bears on how well a claim is supported relative to alternatives, given relevant background assumptions. Something can be evidence without settling the claim; alleged evidence can also be unreliable or irrelevant.
  1. Identify Core Characteristics:
  • Despite the varying contexts, certain core characteristics of evidence can be identified:
    • Relevance: Evidence must be directly related to the claim it supports or refutes.
    • Reliability: Assess how accurately the source or method represents the relevant facts. Reliability is a matter to evaluate, not a guarantee built into every item called evidence.
    • Objectivity: Evidence should be as free from bias as possible, recognizing that complete objectivity is often challenging to achieve.
    • Checkability: Independent corroboration is valuable where possible. A unique historical event or private experience need not be reproducible to have evidential relevance, though limits on checking affect the assessment.
  1. Categorize Types of Evidence:
  • Recognizing different types of evidence can help in understanding its application and limitations. For example, evidence can be empirical, anecdotal, statistical, or testimonial. Each type has its strengths and weaknesses depending on the context of the discussion.
  1. Context-Specific Standards:
  • Context-specific procedures: Different inquiries use different methods and thresholds. Peer review is a screening process, not a guarantee of truth, and eyewitness reports are not automatically stronger than indirect evidence.
  1. Education and Training:
  • Educate stakeholders on the nature of evidence and its proper use. This involves training in critical thinking, research methodologies, and the evaluation of sources. It’s essential for individuals to understand how to discern high-quality evidence from poor-quality or irrelevant information.
  1. Promote a Culture of Critical Inquiry:
  • Encourage a culture where questioning and critically evaluating evidence is standard practice. This includes being open to revising one’s own beliefs or claims in light of new, compelling evidence.
  1. Establish Forums for Dispute Resolution:
  • Create and maintain forums where disputes over the validity or interpretation of evidence can be resolved. This could involve peer review in scientific communities, judicial systems in legal contexts, or moderated debates in public discourse.
  1. Foster Interdisciplinary Dialogue:
  • Encourage dialogue across different disciplines to share insights and methodologies for evaluating evidence. This can lead to a more robust and nuanced understanding of evidence that transcends specific fields.
  1. Continuous Review and Adaptation:
  • Recognize that definitions and standards of evidence may evolve over time. It’s important to periodically review and adapt the criteria for evidence to reflect advancements in knowledge, technology, and societal values.

Through these steps, it becomes possible to construct and propose a definition of “evidence” that is less subjective and more universally applicable. This process emphasizes the importance of clarity, context, and critical evaluation in understanding and utilizing evidence effectively in discussions.

Gemini response · editorial edition

You’re right, the term “evidence” can be quite subjective depending on the context and field. However, it’s not without potential for a shared understanding. Here are some approaches to constructing and proposing a definition of “evidence” that could be more widely accepted:

1. Start with Common Ground:

  • Identify common threads: Look for characteristics of “evidence” that are generally agreed upon across different disciplines. For example, it might suggest something factual, be based on observations or data, and contribute to forming informed conclusions.
  • Avoid overly specific wording: Instead of defining evidence based on specific types (e.g., scientific data), focus on broader attributes like “information used to assess the validity of a claim.”

2. Consider Different Contexts:

  • Acknowledge multiple perspectives: Recognize that different fields might have specific expectations for evidence (e.g., legal system vs. historical research).
  • Develop a framework: Create a definition that allows for flexibility, encompassing various types of evidence while retaining core principles. For example, you could differentiate between “direct evidence” and “indirect evidence” based on how directly it links to the claim.

3. Foster Community Engagement:

  • Engage with diverse stakeholders: Get input from experts in different fields, philosophers of science, and even the general public to broaden the understanding of evidence.
  • Refine based on feedback: Use feedback to clarify uncertainties and address potential biases in the definition.

4. Promote Clear Use:

  • Provide examples and non-examples: Illustrate how the definition applies in various contexts, highlighting both valid and invalid uses of the term “evidence.”
  • Encourage reflection and analysis: Emphasize the critical evaluation of evidence, considering its source, reliability, and relevance to the specific claim.

Remember, building a universally accepted definition is an ongoing process. The key is to focus on shared characteristics, accommodate diverse perspectives, and promote its thoughtful application across different fields.

By combining these approaches, we can move towards a more robust and valuable understanding of “evidence” in our discussions and decision-making.

Prompt 2: Is a rigorous and coherent notion of “standards of evidence” available that can be applied to most inquiries into truth?

Standards across inquiries

ChatGPT response · editorial edition

A coherent general framework can ask about relevance, reliability, alternatives, uncertainty and transparency. It does not supply one universal threshold or procedure for every scientific, historical, mathematical and practical question.

  1. Empirical Adequacy:
  • Empirical grounding: Empirical claims should fit relevant observations. Not every event can be repeated, and falsifiability ordinarily concerns a hypothesis or testing arrangement rather than an isolated datum. Mathematical proof has different standards.
  1. Validity:
  • Validity: In deduction, validity means true premises cannot yield a false conclusion. In measurement, construct validity concerns whether an instrument measures its intended property. These senses should not be merged.
  1. Reliability:
  • Reliability: Check repeatability where appropriate, accuracy, dependence and conditions of collection. A consistently biased instrument or rehearsed testimony can be stable without being accurate.
  1. Relevance:
  • Evidence must be directly related to the claim or hypothesis it is intended to support or refute. Irrelevant evidence, no matter how accurate or reliable, does not contribute to establishing the truth of a claim.
  1. Sufficiency:
  • Sufficiency: An item can count as evidence while being insufficient for acceptance. The threshold for action or an institutional decision is separate from the strength of support the evidence provides.
  1. Objectivity:
  • The collection and presentation of evidence should strive to minimize bias. This involves acknowledging and mitigating the potential influence of personal biases, conflicts of interest, or the experimental design on the evidence gathered.
  1. Comprehensiveness:
  • The evidence should cover the relevant aspects of the claim or hypothesis. This includes considering counter-evidence and alternative explanations to ensure a comprehensive understanding of the issue at hand.
  1. Transparency:
  • The methods used to gather and analyze evidence should be transparent and documented. This allows others to evaluate the quality of the evidence, replicate findings, and assess the validity of the conclusions drawn.

These standards are not exhaustive and can be tailored to fit the requirements of specific disciplines or inquiries. For example:

  • Scientific inquiry uses observations, methodological scrutiny and independent checking where feasible. Peer review can help detect problems but does not ensure every standard is met.
  • In legal contexts, the focus might be more on the relevance, sufficiency, and reliability of evidence, with established legal procedures guiding the evaluation process.
  • In historical research, standards such as validity, comprehensiveness, and transparency are vital, given the reliance on primary and secondary sources to reconstruct past events.

Despite these differences, the overarching goal remains the same: to employ a set of criteria that ensures evidence is capable of supporting robust, reliable conclusions about the truth of various claims or hypotheses.

Gemini response · editorial edition

While there is no singular, “rigorous and coherent” notion of “standards of evidence” applicable to all inquiries into truth, there are frameworks and principles that can guide various fields in evaluating evidence. Here’s why a universal standard is challenging:

Different inquiries: Physics uses observation as well as experiment, and history uses material traces as well as documents. Procedures differ, but both can assess source quality and compare explanations.

Uncertainty: Uncertainty in our knowledge is distinct from a proposition lacking a truth value. Probabilistic assessment can express incomplete support for a definite claim.

Values and decisions: Values affect which questions are pursued, ethical collection methods, and action thresholds. They do not by themselves make the same evidence favor a factual claim more strongly.

However, this doesn’t mean we’re left in the dark. Here are some key aspects to consider for building robust evidence standards within specific fields or inquiries:

Clarity and Transparency: Clearly define the type of claim being examined and the desired level of certainty. Be transparent about the assumptions and biases influencing the chosen standards.

Objectivity and Reliability: Seek evidence that is as objective and reliable as possible within the field’s limitations. Evaluate sources for potential bias, limitations, and methodological strengths.

Replication and Verification: Where possible, strive for replicability of results and verification of findings through independent assessments. This strengthens the confidence in the evidence.

Openness to Revision: Acknowledge that evidence and understanding evolve over time. Standards should be flexible enough to incorporate new information and adapt to changing knowledge.

Critical Thinking and Contextualization: Don’t blindly accept any evidence at face value. Critically evaluate its origin, methodology, and limitations. Interpret evidence within its broader context, considering alternative explanations and potential biases.

Ultimately, effective standards of evidence require a balanced approach. They need to be rigorous enough to ensure reliability but flexible enough to adapt to diverse fields and inquiries. By focusing on clarity, transparency, objectivity, and critical thinking, we can build effective frameworks for evaluating evidence and pursuing truth across various domains.

Remember, the search for truth is often a collaborative and iterative process. Engaging in open dialogue, respecting diverse perspectives, and continuously reassessing the evidence are crucial components of any responsible pursuit of knowledge.

Prompt 3: Provide three historical examples of attempts to provide evidence that fail in some way. Elaborate on the reasons for the failures.

Historical examples

ChatGPT response · editorial edition

The history of science, medicine, and technology is replete with examples where attempts to provide evidence have failed due to various reasons. These failures often stem from methodological flaws, biases, or a lack of understanding of the phenomena being studied. Below are three notable historical examples:

  1. Phlogiston Theory (18th Century):
  • Background: The phlogiston theory was a scientific hypothesis that posited the existence of a fire-like element called “phlogiston,” which was believed to be contained within combustible bodies and released during combustion.
  • Evidential problem: Mass gain during metal calcination challenged straightforward phlogiston accounts, while oxygen-based explanations better organized relevant observations. Historical phlogiston theories were varied; their rejection was not one observation against one unchanging model.
  • Lesson: Observations must discriminate among competing explanations. Calling the episode simply a lack of proper experiments overlooks the experimental work performed by supporters and critics.
  1. Piltdown Man (Early 20th Century):
  • Background: The Piltdown Man was a paleoanthropological hoax in which skull fragments were presented as the fossilized remains of a previously unknown early human. These remains were supposedly discovered in Piltdown, England, in the early 20th century.
  • Failure of Evidence: The evidence failed because it was fraudulent. The skull fragments were a combination of a human cranium and the lower jawbone of an orangutan, deliberately altered to appear ancient. The hoax was not exposed until 1953, when new dating technology and a more critical examination of the fossils revealed the fraud.
  • Lesson: Altered specimens and insufficient authentication misled parts of the research community. Expectations may affect interpretation, but not every scientist accepted the find, and a single motive should not be assigned to all participants.
  1. Lysenkoism in the Soviet Union (1930s-1960s):
  • Background: Lysenkoism was a political and pseudoscientific movement led by Trofim Lysenko, who rejected Mendelian genetics in favor of a form of Lamarckism, a theory suggesting that acquired characteristics can be inherited.
  • Evidential failure: Political enforcement of favored claims and suppression of criticism undermined the testing of hereditary and agricultural assertions. Reported successes were not an adequate substitute for controlled, independently assessable evidence.
  • Lesson: Ideological control and restrictions on research damaged scientific inquiry. Attributing particular agricultural losses requires historical evidence rather than treating every Soviet failure as caused by one doctrine.

Each of these examples demonstrates different reasons for the failure of evidence, including methodological errors, deliberate fraud, and ideological interference. These cases underscore the importance of rigorous scientific methodology, critical scrutiny, and openness to revising theories in light of new evidence.

Prompt 4: It seems that the oft-quoted, “The absence of evidence is not evidence of absence,” is not technically correct. Please elaborate on this.

Absence as evidence

ChatGPT response · editorial edition

The statement “The absence of evidence is not evidence of absence” is often used in discussions about proving or disproving claims, particularly in contexts where it is difficult to gather empirical data. While the phrase communicates a valuable caution against concluding too much from a lack of information, it can be misleading if not properly contextualized. The technical correctness of this statement depends on the context and the nature of what is being investigated.

In Contexts Where It Is Technically Incorrect:

  1. When Evidence Should Exist:
  • If the presence of something is hypothesized to produce observable and measurable evidence, then the absence of such evidence can indeed be taken as evidence of absence. For example, if a large animal species were hypothesized to exist in a certain ecosystem, the complete absence of any direct evidence (like sightings, tracks, or other biological signs) over an extended period of careful and comprehensive searching could reasonably be considered evidence of its absence.
  1. Statistical and Probabilistic Reasoning:
  • A null result can count against an effect when the study was capable of detecting an effect of the relevant size. Failure to reach statistical significance alone is not proof of no effect; examine the estimate, uncertainty, design and the effects still compatible with the data.
  1. Bayesian Reasoning:
  • Compare the probability of no detection under the hypothesis and under its alternative. If no detection is less likely under the hypothesis, it counts against it. For example, with equal prior odds, P(no detection|present)=0.1 and P(no detection|absent)=1 yield posterior presence probability 0.1/1.1, about 9.1%.

Contexts Where It Holds More Weight:

  1. Exploratory Research and Unknown Phenomena:
  • In the early stages of exploring a phenomenon, the absence of evidence might not necessarily indicate the absence of the phenomenon itself but rather reflect limitations in current methodology, technology, or understanding. Here, the statement serves as a reminder of the limitations of current knowledge and capabilities.
  1. Non-Empirical Contexts:
  • Indirectly observable claims: Theoretical entities in physics can predict observable traces even when they cannot be seen directly. The significance of a missing trace depends on the specific prediction and detection method, not simply on labeling a claim theoretical or metaphysical.

Conclusion:

Absence can be evidence when the missing observation was more expected under one hypothesis than another. Its strength depends on detection capability and the comparison, and it need not be conclusive proof.

Gemini response · editorial edition

You’re right, the statement “The absence of evidence is not evidence of absence” requires some context and nuance to be accurate. While it highlights an important point, it can be misinterpreted and lead to incorrect conclusions if taken too literally. Here’s why:

The Fallacy of Assuming a Negative:

The statement points out the logical fallacy of denying a claim solely because evidence for it hasn’t been found. It reminds us that the failure to find evidence doesn’t automatically guarantee something doesn’t exist. This is particularly relevant in vast or unexplored areas like the universe, the depths of the ocean, or the past.

Limitations of “Absence”:

Distinguish evidence from conclusive proof. “We did not find it” may lower its probability even if it does not establish impossibility; the search conditions determine how informative that result is.

  • Scope and quality of the search: If the search wasn’t extensive or used flawed methods, the “absence” of evidence might not be meaningful.
  • Expected detection: The evidential weight of a non-detection depends on its likelihood under the competing hypotheses. Prior improbability affects the final probability but does not alone determine the strength of that new evidence.
  • Claim specificity: A clearly specified, detectable entity may be easier to search for than a vague claim. Universal claims are not uniformly harder to refute: a genuine counterexample can refute one.

Instead of “Absence,” Consider:

Instead of relying solely on the “absence” of evidence, a more nuanced approach involves:

  • Assessing the strength of the evidence searched for: Was it likely to be found if it existed?
  • Considering alternative explanations: Could the lack of evidence be explained by other factors?
  • Acknowledging the limits of current knowledge: Our understanding evolves, and what seems absent today might be discovered tomorrow.

A Call for Critical Thinking:

The statement, therefore, serves as a reminder to be cautious about both blindly accepting and categorically rejecting claims based on the absence of evidence. It encourages critical thinking, asking questions about the search itself and acknowledging the limitations of our current knowledge.

Ultimately, the statement is a guide for evaluating evidence, not a definitive rule. By understanding its nuances and limitations, we can approach the pursuit of truth with greater insight and avoid jumping to unfounded conclusions.

Prompt 5: The term “evidence” is sometimes changed to “proof” in an attempt to strengthen the perceived strength of the evidence. Why would those in honest discourse want to avoid the term “proof” for inductively-assessed inquiries?

Evidence and proof

ChatGPT response · editorial edition

In honest discourse, particularly within the realms of science and empirical investigation, the distinction between “evidence” and “proof” is crucial due to the fundamentally different implications of these terms. This distinction is especially important in inductively-assessed inquiries, where conclusions are derived from observations or experiments rather than from purely logical or mathematical derivation. Here are several reasons why using the term “proof” should be avoided in favor of “evidence” in such contexts:

  1. Nature of Inductive Reasoning:
  • Induction supports conclusions beyond what premises entail; valid deduction establishes a conditional guarantee from true premises. Calling an inductive result conclusive proof can overstate its support, even when that support is extremely strong.
  1. Evolution of Knowledge:
  • Scientific knowledge is not static but evolves over time as new evidence emerges. What is considered a robust conclusion today might be revised or overturned tomorrow with the discovery of new data. The term “proof” implies a finality that is at odds with the provisional nature of scientific knowledge. Using “evidence” acknowledges this ongoing process of revision and refinement.
  1. Misinterpretation and Miscommunication:
  • In public discourse and communication with non-specialists, the term “proof” can be misleading, suggesting an absolute certainty that science often cannot provide. This can lead to misinterpretation of scientific findings, where the public might see them as irrefutable facts rather than the most current understanding based on available evidence. Avoiding “proof” in favor of “evidence” helps mitigate this risk, promoting a more accurate perception of scientific conclusions.
  1. Scientific Humility and Integrity:
  • Choosing “evidence” over “proof” reflects a commitment to scientific humility and integrity. It signals an openness to new information and a recognition that scientific inquiry is an ongoing process. This approach fosters an environment where questioning, testing, and revising hypotheses are valued over claiming absolute certainty.
  1. Philosophical Consistency:
  • Philosophically, many argue that absolute proof is only attainable in abstract fields like mathematics and formal logic, where conclusions follow necessarily from their premises. In empirical sciences, which deal with the complexity of the natural world, such certainty is elusive. The preference for “evidence” reflects this philosophical understanding of the limits of empirical inquiry.
  1. Encouraging Critical Thinking:
  • Using “evidence” rather than “proof” encourages critical thinking and a more nuanced understanding of how scientific conclusions are reached. It prompts individuals to consider the quality, quantity, and context of the evidence supporting a claim, rather than accepting it uncritically as proven fact.

In summary, in honest and careful discourse, particularly in the context of inductively-assessed inquiries, it is important to use the term “evidence” to reflect the conditional, probabilistic, and evolving nature of empirical knowledge. This choice respects the principles of scientific methodology, promotes accurate communication, and embodies the intellectual humility fundamental to scientific inquiry.

Gemini response · editorial edition

There are several reasons why those engaged in honest, inductive discourse would avoid using the term “proof” when referring to evidence, especially in areas where conclusions are reached through observation and analysis rather than through deductive reasoning:

1. Proof implies certainty: In most areas of study, particularly those using inductive reasoning, absolute certainty is rarely achievable. Using “proof” implies a level of finality that simply doesn’t exist in most situations. Evidence strengthens an argument, but it can always be challenged by new information or interpretations, leaving room for doubt.

2. It can shut down further inquiry: Framing something as “proven” can discourage further investigation and critical thinking. This can be harmful, especially in disciplines where knowledge constantly evolves. Using “evidence” instead implies an open invitation to examine, challenge, and refine understanding.

Risk of misleading language: “Proof” can overstate certainty, but its use alone does not demonstrate dishonest intent. Ordinary and legal uses can mean sufficient demonstration under a stated standard rather than mathematical certainty.

4. It can be misused to promote agendas: Framing something as “proven” can be used to justify political, social, or economic agendas, even when the evidence is not robust enough to support such definitive claims. This can lead to harmful consequences if people accept these claims unquestioningly.

5. It doesn’t translate across disciplines: Some fields, like mathematics or logic, may deal with concepts that can be definitively proven. However, this doesn’t apply to most areas of scientific or humanistic inquiry, where evidence-based conclusions are constantly reevaluated and refined.

Prefer language that accurately conveys the strength and limits of support. The aim is clarity, not a blanket rule that anyone using the word proof in an empirical context is dishonest.
