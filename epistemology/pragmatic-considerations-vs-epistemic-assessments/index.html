1Original prompt · edited responses

Prompt 1: Some philosophers argue that we are justified in lowering our standards of evidence if an enticing claim cannot be realized unless we have belief in that claim. (This argument is often invoked in the context of a proposed resurrected Jesus that needs to be accepted for redemption to take place.) It is claimed that the pragmatic consideration that the desired effect can be accomplished only if we have belief in that effect justifies a lowering of the standards of evidence. Can pragmatic considerations legitimately lower epistemic standards of evidence?

Pragmatic and epistemic justification

ChatGPT response · editorial edition

The question of whether pragmatic considerations can legitimately lower epistemic standards of evidence touches on deep philosophical debates between pragmatism and more traditional epistemological views. The argument that you’ve outlined suggests that in certain contexts, the practical benefits of believing a claim might justify accepting it with less stringent evidence than would otherwise be required. This stance raises several key points for consideration:

  1. Pragmatic justification concerns whether believing or acting has desirable consequences. Epistemic justification concerns whether a belief is adequately supported as true. A practical benefit is not automatically evidence for the proposition believed.
  2. Context and stakes: Practical circumstances can alter how much inquiry is worthwhile or when action is justified under uncertainty. Whether stakes also affect knowledge or outright belief is philosophically disputed; they do not, merely by being important, increase a claim’s evidential probability.
  3. William James, in The Will to Believe, defends passional commitment for a genuine option—live, forced, and momentous—when intellectual grounds cannot decide it. This is not a demonstration that a desired outcome supplies additional evidence for a historical claim.
  4. Risks of Lowering Epistemic Standards: Critics of lowering epistemic standards for pragmatic reasons point out the risks of endorsing beliefs without adequate evidence. These include the potential for self-deception, the propagation of false beliefs, and the undermining of rational inquiry. Furthermore, determining which pragmatic considerations are sufficient to justify lowering standards can be highly subjective and contentious.
  5. Consequences of Belief: The argument also hinges on the assumption that the consequences of belief justify the belief itself. This raises questions about the nature of belief, the ethics of belief, and the relationship between belief and action. It challenges the notion that beliefs should be held purely based on evidence and truth, suggesting instead that the utility of a belief can be a factor in its justification.
  6. Religious application: The claim that redemption requires belief and the claim that a resurrection occurred each need their own assessment. Even if belief were necessary for a benefit, that condition would not establish either the benefit or the historical event. Religious traditions also differ in how they understand faith and evidence.

Under the evidential conception in the question, a promised benefit does not justify increasing confidence beyond the evidence. One may separately debate practical permission to commit or act. Evidence that belief causally changes a future outcome would be relevant evidence about that outcome, not a license to change the past.

Gemini response · editorial edition

The question of whether pragmatic considerations can justify lowering epistemic standards for evidence is a complex one with compelling arguments on both sides. Here’s a breakdown of the different perspectives:

Arguments for lowering standards based on pragmatism:

  • Pascal’s Wager is a practical argument about commitment under uncertainty, not evidence that its religious premises are true. Its expected-benefit reasoning faces questions about rival religious possibilities, assigned probabilities, costs, and whether belief can be voluntarily chosen.
  • Psychological benefits: Belief in certain propositions, even with limited evidence, can offer psychological comfort, hope, or motivation. Some argue that these benefits may justify a less rigorous approach to evidence evaluation in specific personal contexts.
  • Social cohesion: Shared beliefs, even without full epistemic justification, can be crucial for social order and cultural stability. This might be relevant in religious communities where belief in specific tenets forms the bedrock of their identity.

Arguments against lowering standards based on pragmatism:

  • Erosion of intellectual integrity: Sacrificing rigor and objectivity for desired outcomes can undermine the very foundation of knowledge and truth-seeking. Once we accept such an approach, it sets a dangerous precedent for accepting other unsubstantiated claims for purely pragmatic reasons.
  • Potential for harm: Belief in unverified claims, even with good intentions, can lead to harmful consequences in personal and societal spheres. For example, faith healing based on unfounded beliefs could discourage individuals from seeking proper medical care.
  • Risk of inconsistent standards: An exception for a desired outcome needs a defensible rationale; otherwise the same move could favor mutually incompatible claims. This is a possible pattern, not proof that one exception inevitably produces general irrationality.

Values and risk tolerance can affect what action is reasonable. They do not by themselves settle the truth of the claim or the confidence warranted by the evidence. Calling a commitment practically useful does not answer the epistemic question.

Here are some additional points to consider:

  • The strength and nature of the pragmatic considerations matter. Are the potential benefits significant and likely, or are they speculative and distant?
  • The reversibility of the consequences of accepting the claim is important. Can one easily abandon the belief if new evidence emerges?
  • The broader societal implications of lowering standards should be considered. Does it set a precedent that could harm others or undermine essential values?

The direct answer, on the evidential standard proposed here, is no: the desirability of X does not itself provide evidence for X. Practical commitment and evidential confidence must be assessed separately.

The proportionality challenge

ChatGPT response · editorial edition

Yes, under the evidential conception you stipulate: increasing confidence solely for a hoped-for benefit, without new relevant evidence, departs from that standard. This is a conditional conclusion within a clear framework, not a proof that every philosophical account of rationality has the same definition.

  1. Proportionality: Confidence should reflect the support of the total relevant evidence. This does not supply a unique numerical degree for every real-world claim; uncertainty in the evidential model and background assumptions may permit a range.
  2. Epistemic Irrationality: When the degree of belief does not correspond to the degree of evidence, this misalignment can be termed epistemic irrationality. If one’s belief is significantly stronger (or weaker) than the evidence warrants, it deviates from the ideal of forming beliefs in a rational, evidence-based manner.
  3. Pragmatic vs. Epistemic Goals: The tension between pragmatic and epistemic goals lies at the heart of the debate over the justification for beliefs. Pragmatic considerations prioritize the outcomes or consequences of holding a belief, which might necessitate a belief strength that does not align with the available evidence. This prioritization can lead to a form of rationalization where the desire for certain outcomes influences the level of belief beyond what the evidence alone would justify.
  4. Rationality and Irrationality Defined: In the strict epistemic sense, irrationality involves holding beliefs that are not adequately supported by evidence or reasoning. However, from a broader perspective, individuals might engage in what appears to be epistemically irrational behavior for reasons that are rational within a different framework (e.g., emotional, psychological, or pragmatic rationality).
  5. Consequences of Epistemic Irrationality: The divergence between belief and evidence, when adopted systematically or without critical examination, can lead to a range of negative outcomes, including the propagation of misinformation, poor decision-making, and the erosion of trust in rational discourse and scientific inquiry.
  6. Navigating Epistemic and Pragmatic Considerations: The challenge lies in navigating situations where epistemic and pragmatic considerations may conflict. This involves critically assessing the value of outcomes associated with pragmatic beliefs and the importance of maintaining epistemic standards. It requires a nuanced understanding of the contexts in which beliefs are formed and the potential impacts of those beliefs on oneself and others.

Keep the two assessments separate: desire can motivate action or further investigation, but it does not make the same evidence support a higher probability. If belief has a demonstrated causal effect, that effect belongs in the evidence and decision model.

Gemini response · editorial edition

Within your stated evidential standard, yes. My earlier appeal to practical benefits did not answer that specific question; a different account of practical rationality must not be substituted for epistemic rationality.

Here are some counterpoints to consider:

  1. Human limitations: Biases and cognitive constraints may explain a departure from an epistemic standard. An explanation of the departure does not by itself justify it.
  2. Practical reasoning: Acting on a low-probability possibility may be sensible when costs and benefits warrant it, while retaining an appropriately low belief in that possibility.
  3. Degrees of “irrationality”: Even within your definition, “irrationality” might not be a binary state but exist on a spectrum. Lowering standards due to pragmatism might be less “rational” than basing beliefs solely on evidence, but it doesn’t necessarily mean completely abandoning rationality.
  4. Context: The harm caused by a miscalibrated belief can vary without changing whether that confidence exceeds the evidential support.
  5. Epistemological vs. psychological perspectives: Your definition of rationality focuses on the epistemological aspect, ensuring beliefs align with evidence. However, a psychological perspective might consider factors like emotional well-being or social needs, influencing belief formation even if it deviates from the ideal evidence-based model.

Thus the response to your stipulated case is direct: knowingly inflating confidence for a desired benefit violates the stated epistemic standard. Understanding motives and evaluating practical consequences remain separate questions.

Norms and human limitations

ChatGPT response · editorial edition

Yes. A normative standard describes how reasoning should proceed; a descriptive account explains how people actually reason. Failures to meet a standard do not force us to redefine it, though the standard itself remains open to argument.

  1. Rigorous Definition of Irrationality: A rigorous definition of irrationality helps to establish clear criteria for what constitutes rational versus irrational thought and behavior. This can be based on principles of logic, evidence-based reasoning, and the coherence of beliefs with available evidence. Such a definition is crucial for evaluating claims, arguments, and decisions in a consistent and objective manner.
  2. Human Cognitive Limitations: Recognizing human irrationality involves understanding cognitive biases, emotional influences, social pressures, and other psychological factors that can lead to deviations from rational thinking. Humans are subject to a wide range of heuristics and biases, such as confirmation bias, availability heuristic, and affect heuristic, which can lead to irrational conclusions or decisions.
  3. Compatibility of Concepts: Acknowledging human irrationality does not undermine the concept of rationality; instead, it highlights the complexity of human cognition and the challenges of achieving rational thought. The existence of a rigorous standard for rationality serves as an ideal or benchmark against which human thought and behavior can be assessed.
  4. Educational and Corrective Measures: Understanding the distinction between the ideal of rationality and the reality of human irrationality is crucial for developing strategies to promote more rational thinking. Education, awareness of cognitive biases, and critical thinking training are tools that can help individuals recognize and correct irrational tendencies.
  5. Philosophical and Practical Implications: The recognition that humans do not always act rationally has important implications for philosophy, psychology, economics, and other fields that study human behavior. It informs approaches to moral and ethical reasoning, decision-making models, and the design of systems and policies that account for human irrationality.
  6. Normative vs. Descriptive: The distinction between what is rational (normative aspect) and what people actually do (descriptive aspect) allows for a critical analysis of human behavior without the need to compromise on the definition of rationality. It enables a critique of irrational behaviors and the development of methods to foster more rational decision-making processes.

In sum, maintaining a rigorous definition of irrationality while recognizing the prevalence of irrational thought and behavior in humans is not only possible but also essential. It allows for a nuanced understanding of human cognition and behavior, providing a foundation for efforts to enhance rational thinking and decision-making within the constraints of human psychological tendencies.

Gemini response · editorial edition

Absolutely, you’re right. We can certainly maintain a rigorous definition of irrationality while acknowledging that humans don’t always act accordingly. In fact, doing so provides a valuable framework for critical thinking and evaluating beliefs.

Here’s why the separation is important:

  1. Clear standards: Having a rigorous definition of “irrationality” sets a clear standard for assessing beliefs and actions. It helps us identify when our own or others’ thinking might deviate from logic and evidence.
  2. Clarity without overreach: An explicit standard makes a discussion assessable. It need not imply that all disagreement over rationality is mere relativism or that there is only one defensible account.
  3. Nuance and context: Even with a rigorous definition, we can still analyze and understand human behavior in its full complexity. By considering factors like cognitive biases, emotions, and social influences, we can explain why people sometimes act “irrationally” without sacrificing the importance of the concept itself.

So, acknowledging human limitations doesn’t negate the need for a clear definition of irrationality. It simply means recognizing that various factors can influence our thinking and lead us to deviate from the ideal, evidence-based model. We can explore these influences and understand them without abandoning the crucial standard provided by a rigorous definition.

Ultimately, striving for intellectual honesty and clarity around rationality, while simultaneously acknowledging and exploring the complexities of human thought, is essential for navigating the world effectively and engaging in meaningful discussions about beliefs and knowledge.

Belief, benefits and self-deception

ChatGPT response · editorial edition

Yes, your summary captures the essence of the issue regarding epistemic rationality and the adjustment of evidence standards based on the supposed necessity of belief for receiving a benefit or effect (X). Here’s a breakdown of the key points:

  1. Abandoning Epistemic Rationality: When an individual lowers their standards of evidence for a belief (X) because they are told that belief in X is necessary to receive X, they are indeed deviating from epistemic rationality. Epistemic rationality requires that beliefs are proportionate to the evidence. To alter this standard based on the desired outcomes rather than the strength of the evidence undermines the rational basis for the belief.
  2. Tie Between Belief and Evidence: For a belief to be considered rational in the epistemic sense, it must be based on the actual degree of evidence supporting it. This means that a rational individual’s degree of belief in X should correspond closely to the strength and quality of evidence for X. Any adjustment to the degree of belief that does not reflect the evidence undermines this principle.
  3. Self-deception: Knowingly allowing a desired outcome to override one’s acknowledged evidential standard is a departure from that standard. Whether to call the process self-deception depends on the account of self-deception; the argument does not establish a clinical delusion or that the person is irrational in every respect.

The critical issue here is the distinction between believing something because one wishes it to be true (or has been told it must be believed for certain outcomes) and believing something because the evidence supports its truth. The former approach risks engaging in wishful thinking or self-delusion, which can have broader implications for one’s ability to make rational decisions based on evidence and logic. It also raises ethical and philosophical questions about the nature of belief, the responsibilities of belief holders, and the impact of such beliefs on personal integrity and decision-making.

Gemini response · editorial edition

While it’s true that lowering evidence standards based solely on the desired outcome of receiving the claim itself deviates from a strictly evidence-based model of rationality, the situation isn’t quite as black and white as self-delusion. Here’s why:

Complexity of Rationality:

  • Humans operate under bounded rationality, meaning we often make decisions factoring in emotions, biases, and limitations in processing information. While evidence should ideally guide beliefs, other factors influence real-life decision-making.
  • Practical and epistemic reasoning can coexist: one can act to pursue a benefit without claiming that the benefit makes its underlying proposition more probable.

Degrees of “Lowering Standards”:

  • Degree matters: A smaller mismatch may be a smaller epistemic defect under the proposed standard. It is not converted into evidence by being comforting or harmless.

Context Matters:

  • Context can change moral consequences and action thresholds. It does not automatically remove the stipulated mismatch between confidence and evidence.

Epistemology vs. Psychology:

  • While a purely epistemological view of rationality emphasizes evidence-based belief, a psychological perspective acknowledges factors like emotional well-being and social needs, influencing belief formation even if it deviates from the ideal evidence-based model.

Self-Delusion? Not Necessarily:

  • The term “self-delusion” is unnecessary here. Self-deception need not be defined as consciously telling oneself a known falsehood; accounts differ. The clear point is that deliberately overriding an acknowledged evidential standard for a desired benefit is not evidential justification.

Nuance and Individual Judgment:

  • For your specified case, the epistemic judgment does not need to wait on every psychological detail: the confidence is being raised for a non-evidential reason. Psychological labels require additional care.

Remember, while striving for evidence-based beliefs is crucial, understanding human complexity and different perspectives on rationality helps us navigate real-world scenarios where emotions, desires, and limitations influence our thinking and decision-making.
