ChatGPT response · editorial edition

The teacher’s rationale for concluding that a ghost took the apple is problematic for several reasons. Here is an assessment of the argument provided:

  1. Misuse of probability: Equal treatment of thirty-three students does not by itself justify assigning each a 1/33 unconditional chance of taking the apple. First specify what is known:
  • If exactly one student took the apple and each is equally plausible, each has conditional probability 1/33, about 3.03%. The probability that some student took it is then 1 by assumption. If that premise is uncertain and the probability of a student taking it is q, equal exclusive shares are q/33.
  • The formula 1 − 0.97^33 ≈ 0.634, or 63.4%, applies only if thirty-three events each have probability 0.03 and are mutually independent. That is a different model, allowing several events to occur. It cannot be inferred from uncertainty about the identity of a single taker. If thirty-three mutually exclusive events each have unconditional probability 0.03, their union has probability 0.99. The prompt does not supply enough information to choose these assumptions.
  1. Aggregation error: Low probability for each individual alternative does not imply zero probability for their union. “Fallacy of division” is the wrong label for this move: division transfers a whole’s property to its parts, whereas this argument dismisses the whole family because its individual members are small.
  2. Assumption of Ghost Existence: The teacher assumes the existence of ghosts as a more likely explanation without sufficient basis:
  • The claim that not all ghost reports are fabricated would not establish ghosts. Sincere mistakes, misidentification, and other causes remain possible. Reports may also share sources, so they cannot automatically be treated as independent evidence.
  • The teacher both shifts the burden of support and appeals to ignorance: failure to disprove the ghost hypothesis is offered as positive evidence. Even evidence for ghosts in general would not establish that one moved this apple.
  1. Default Position: The default position in rational inquiry should be skepticism and reliance on evidence. The absence of evidence for the involvement of the students (or lack of evidence pointing specifically to any one student) does not automatically validate an alternative supernatural explanation.
  2. More Likely Explanations: There are more plausible explanations that should be considered before supernatural ones:
  • Misplacement or misremembering by the teacher or another staff member.
  • A student, or group of students, taking the apple without the teacher’s notice.
  • The probability of a union is at least as large as any component, but need not be much larger when events overlap. For mutually exclusive alternatives it equals their sum. In this classroom, the dependence structure must be specified.

The teacher has neither eliminated student involvement nor supplied evidence connecting a ghost to the apple. Investigate ordinary possibilities without accusing any particular student from a group-level probability alone.

Gemini response · editorial edition

The teacher’s reasoning has a few flaws:

  1. Ordinary alternatives: Student involvement, misplacement, or another person moving the apple are live possibilities. The prompt supplies no measured base rate establishing which is most likely, and no positive support for a ghost.
  2. Conditional versus collective probability: A small probability for a named student is compatible with a large probability for some student. The 1/33 calculation assumes equal shares conditional on a student being responsible; it does not independently prove that premise.
  3. Evidence: The teacher supplies no verified evidence of ghost involvement. An unexplained sighting is not automatically a fabricated report or a verified supernatural event.
  4. Burden of support: The person proposing ghost involvement must show evidence favoring it over alternatives. Familiarity or simplicity alone does not prove that a student took the apple.

Here’s a more logical approach:

  • Consider if the teacher saw a student near the apple before it disappeared.
  • Ask the students if anyone saw something or accidentally took it.
  • Look around the classroom for the apple (e.g., under a desk).

These steps address more likely explanations before resorting to the supernatural.

A rational approach involves:

  • Starting with natural explanations that are most likely based on everyday occurrences.
  • Using a probabilistic framework to weigh the likelihood of various scenarios.
  • Being ready to update beliefs as new evidence comes in, using principles of Bayesian reasoning.

This process makes assumptions and revisions explicit. It does not guarantee a correct conclusion, and uncertainty may remain. Avoid treating a numerical exercise as grounds for accusing a child.

Analysis

The toy weights favor the listed conventional group over the fictional alternative, but 75% remains unassigned. Neither 24% nor 1% is an empirical estimate, and the car’s already-observed absence must not be confused with the probabilities of its causes.

Pedagogical Explanation of the Flawed Rationale

Understanding Probability

Low individual weights can add to a larger group weight when the alternatives are disjoint. Here 24% is larger than each listed component and the stipulated 1% rival, but still below 50%. The example therefore does not establish that the four listed causes jointly are more likely than all remaining causes.

Disjoint addition and the law of total probability

Let M mean that the car is missing and H₁ through H₄ denote disjoint explanations. Then their conditional union probability is the sum of P(Hᵢ | M). By contrast, the law of total probability calculates P(M) = Σᵢ P(M | Hᵢ)P(Hᵢ) over an exhaustive disjoint partition. These are related but different calculations. See the Stanford probability review.

P(H₁ ∪ H₂ ∪ H₃ ∪ H₄ | M) = P(H₁ | M) + P(H₂ | M) + P(H₃ | M) + P(H₄ | M), assuming disjoint categories.

Thus the stipulated listed conventional group has weight 10% + 5% + 5% + 4% = 24%. Once M is observed, P(M | M) = 1, not 24%.

Dismissing High Probability Events

A recent science-fiction movie might make the dimensional-shift story easier to recall. That would illustrate availability influencing judgment, but the vignette does not establish a psychological diagnosis. The 1% is an invented weight, not evidence that such shifts occur.

Bayes’ Theorem and Updating Beliefs

Rational thinking would require updating beliefs based on evidence using Bayes’ Theorem:

P(H | E) = P(E | H) × P(H) / P(E), provided P(E) > 0. All probabilities must use the same background information.

Where:

  • ( H ) is the hypothesis (e.g., the car was stolen).
  • E is additional evidence, such as a towing record, if the starting weights already condition on the car being missing. Do not update a second time on evidence already included.
  • ( P(H|E) ) is the probability of the hypothesis given the evidence.

The current vignette supplies no evidence favoring the dimensional-shift hypothesis. A posterior comparison requires likelihoods and priors; the formula alone cannot supply them.

Practical Implications

Believing in the low-probability explanation without empirical evidence can lead to inappropriate actions or lack of action:

  • Failure to Investigate: Dismissing high-probability scenarios might prevent the owner from taking practical steps like checking with relatives, contacting the police, or verifying with the bank or city towing records.
  • Resource Misallocation: Resources might be wasted on seeking unlikely explanations (e.g., consulting fiction-based theories) rather than addressing plausible causes.
Conclusion

Keep mutually exclusive alternatives distinct from independent events. For overlapping A and B, P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A ∩ B); independence gives the product rule for the intersection, not permission simply to add. Conditional probabilities and evidence-sensitive updating help avoid both sensational explanations and unjustified certainty about ordinary ones.

Gemini response · editorial edition

The Flawed Allure of the Improbable: Analyzing Scenario 1 – The Vanishing Vehicle

Let’s dissect the flawed reasoning in Scenario 1: The Vanishing Vehicle. Here’s why the owner’s fixation on the “dimensional shift” theory is illogical:

  1. Probability versus possibility: Assigning an imagined mechanism a 1% toy weight neither establishes its physical possibility nor measures its likelihood. The listed 24% group outweighs that stipulated number but leaves most of the distribution unspecified.
  2. Occam’s razor: Prefer fewer unsupported assumptions when competing accounts otherwise explain the evidence comparably well. Simplicity is a defeasible guide, not a theorem that the simplest story is always most probable.
  3. Confirmation Bias: The owner might be experiencing confirmation bias. Their recent exposure to the sci-fi movie might make them subconsciously seek out evidence to fit that narrative. They might misinterpret details (e.g., no signs of forced entry) to support the dimensional shift theory, overlooking simpler explanations.
  4. Lack of Evidence: The sci-fi explanation lacks any supporting evidence. There’s no indication of futuristic technology or displaced objects, unlike the potential evidence associated with the conventional explanations (e.g., broken window for theft, witness for borrowing).

Here’s a more logical approach:

  • Gather Information: The owner should first investigate the more likely scenarios. This might involve checking with relatives, contacting the police or towing company, and verifying details with the bank.
  • Evaluate Evidence: Any evidence found (e.g., security camera footage, witness accounts) should be used to refine the possibilities.
  • Seek Expert Opinion: If no clear explanation emerges, consulting a mechanic or law enforcement might be necessary.

By focusing on evidence-based explanations and employing a systematic approach, the owner can arrive at a more rational conclusion than resorting to a highly improbable sci-fi scenario.
