Prompt 1: Assess the following essay for logical coherence and pragmatic value.

The curator’s essay and three assessments

Among non-philosophers, discussions on epistemology are fraught with denotative and connotative differences for core terms that make progress difficult. The following is a short list of the many terms that are defined vastly different among those with a keen interest in epistemology, but a shallow background in the field.

  • knowledge
  • belief
  • truth
  • fact
  • evidence
  • demonstration
  • proof
  • rationality
  • reasonable
  • credence
  • assent to
  • confidence

There are three ways we can approach the definition of terms in an attempt to arrive at a degree of denotative agreement adequate for a productive dialogue.

1: Demand that your interlocutor adopt your denotations.

This is the least likely way to accomplish your goal of a productive dialogue since it requires that your interlocutors has an accommodating personality and that you have the patience to educate them to employ terms in ways foreign to their default denotations.

2: Accommodate the denotations of your interlocutor.

This will require the following:

  • You learn the terms they assign to various epistemological concepts.
  • You assess their constellation of epistemological terms for internal logical coherence.
  • You engage in subsequent dialogues on epistemology with consistency in the way you employ terms.

Two problems with this method are 1) you must arrive at a rigorous understanding of your interlocutor’s denotation of every commonly used epistemological term that might enter the conversation, and 2) many of these terms have significant, intrinsic conceptual overlap, making equivocation and semantic creep very easy.

I propose a third method that may require a higher initial investment, but will yield far greater productivity in subsequent discussion.

3: In collaboration with your interlocutor, collapse as many 2nd-order epistemological concepts as possible into “1st-order” terms.

Definition: 2nd-order concepts are semantic derivatives of 1st-order concepts that are far more conceptually primitive.

Here are a couple examples:

The term knowledge that commonly reflects a 2nd-order concept can be replaced with a credence in the top decile along the epistemic gradient.

Here, 2nd-order term knowledge has been collapsed into an explanatory phrase that employs a 1st-order highly nuanced and rigorous term credence.

The term fact that commonly reflects a 2nd-order concept can be replaced with a high credence given to a proposition by the majority of relevant experts.

Here, the 2nd-order term fact has also been collapsed into an explanatory phrase that employs a 1st-order highly nuanced and rigorous term credence.

This method has the following advantages:

  • A far more parsimonious and less unwieldy collection of epistemological terms. Every secondary concept such as knowledge or fact will include a 1st-order term in its definition such as belief, reducing the number of “moving parts” and the accompanying potential for straying from the denotations you have mutually agreed on.
  • The very act of arriving at this parsimonious collection of rigorous terms will itself assist in 1) understanding the epistemological perspective of the other and 2) excluding terms that both parties agree are too semantically muddled to be of any value.

While this third method does have significant overhead such as a focused prelude to establish mutually agreed upon denotations, the subsequent discussions can avoid the paralyzing degree of semantic mire that many dialogues on epistemology suffer.

Logical Coherence
Introduction

The essay addresses the challenges faced by non-philosophers when discussing epistemology, particularly due to varying definitions of core terms. It offers three methods to align definitions for productive dialogue: demanding others adopt one’s definitions, accommodating others’ definitions, and collaboratively collapsing second-order concepts into first-order terms.

Analysis of Methods
  1. Demanding Adoption of One’s Definitions: The essay gives a plausible reason to expect resistance, not a logical impossibility. An agreed technical exercise can reasonably require a fixed vocabulary; demanding that someone’s ordinary usage is simply illegitimate is much less promising.
  2. Accommodating Interlocutors’ Definitions: Learning another person’s usage can expose disagreement that was only verbal. The proposed consistency check is valuable, though overlapping meanings are not automatically contradictory. Ask whether the speaker changes a term’s meaning within an inference.
  3. Collaborative Collapse of Second-Order Concepts: This is coherent as a negotiated convention. Its success does not follow merely from using fewer terms. “Knowledge” as top-decile credence reports confidence; ordinary factive knowledge also requires that the claim be true. The essay’s “fact” reports expert acceptance, which can itself be mistaken. Participants should record what their replacement preserves and what it deliberately sets aside.
Pragmatic Value
Practicality of Implementation
  1. Demanding Adoption of One’s Definitions: Unilateral insistence may make the dialogue a contest over authority. State the purpose of a proposed definition and ask for a trial use rather than treating acquiescence as evidence that the definition is correct.
  2. Accommodating Interlocutors’ Definitions While more feasible than the first method, this approach still requires significant effort in learning and applying others’ definitions accurately. The potential for misunderstanding and inconsistency makes it pragmatically challenging.
  3. Collaborative Collapse of Second-Order Concepts: The initial effort is most likely to pay off in recurring discussions with the same participants. Start with the two terms causing the current disagreement; writing a complete epistemological dictionary first could cost more time than it saves.
Benefits of the Third Method
  • Parsimony and Consistency: Fewer terms can reduce accidental shifts in meaning. A useful glossary should also mark its primitives: defining belief through credence and then credence solely through belief leaves the explanatory circle intact.
  • Enhanced Understanding: Test a proposed definition against a shared case. Two people might both assign 0.95 credence to a train arriving on time yet disagree about calling that “knowledge.” The number locates their agreement; it does not establish that the train will arrive.
  • Efficiency in Dialogue: Record the agreed usage and the remaining disagreement. Then discuss the evidence or reasons at issue. Reopen a definition when it produces a misleading inference, rather than renegotiating every word whenever the conclusion becomes unwelcome.
Conclusion

The essay offers a useful method for reducing verbal friction, provided its replacements are understood as explicit working conventions. A short glossary, one agreed example and one counterexample give the proposal a practical test: does it reveal a disagreement about evidence, or merely hide it behind a new term?

Gemini response · editorial edition

Logical Coherence: A workable proposal with substantive choices

The three methods are clearly distinguished, and collaboration offers an intelligible response to semantic confusion. However, the examples do more than simplify wording: replacing knowledge with high credence and fact with expert agreement changes what those terms claim. The argument needs that choice to be explicit, not treated as a neutral discovery about their meanings.

Pragmatic Value: Promising, with Considerations

The essay offers a valuable approach to tackling the issue of defining epistemological terms in non-philosophical discussions.

Here’s why it’s promising:

  • Addresses a real problem: Disagreements about terms like “knowledge” and “fact” can derail productive discussions.
  • Provides a practical solution: Collapsing complex terms into simpler ones can be a helpful strategy.
  • Acknowledges an implementation cost: The essay recognizes the effort needed before discussion begins. The further question is whether a stable local glossary saves enough later clarification to justify that effort.

However, some considerations remain:

  • Specificity of first-order terms: “Credence” needs an agreed subject, proposition and time. A scale from 0 to 1 can represent confidence without making someone’s actual confidence precisely measurable. Here “first-order” means basic within this glossary, not the separate technical distinction used in logic.
  • Universal applicability: The essay focuses on collapsing knowledge and fact, but it’s unclear if this approach would work for all epistemological terms.
  • Potential for loss of distinctions: Consider a sealed envelope containing a red or blue card. Everyone may confidently expect red while the card is blue. Consensus and confidence are features of the observers; the card’s colour is what makes their claim accurate or inaccurate.

Try the method on one disputed claim, with an agreed paraphrase and a case on which that paraphrase fails. Keep the simplification if it clarifies who believes what and why. Use an additional term when a single credence figure cannot express a distinction the discussion needs.

Which concepts can be unpacked without losing distinctions?

ChatGPT response · editorial edition

Collapsing 2nd-Order Epistemological Concepts into 1st-Order Terms

The following eleven entries keep the original sequence. Treat “first-order” as a locally agreed starting vocabulary, not as an established hierarchy of all epistemological concepts. Some entries can be unpacked around credence; others require evidence, inference or truth as separate anchors.

1. Knowledge

2nd-Order Concept: Knowledge
Working phrase: High credence supported by stated reasons, when using the curator’s practical convention.

Rationale: This makes the strength and basis of a claim visible. It is not equivalent to factive knowledge, which excludes falsehood. A person can be highly confident for apparently good reasons and still be wrong. For the broader philosophical dispute, see the analysis of knowledge.

2. Belief

2nd-Order Concept: Belief
Collapsed Phrase: Credence assigned to a proposition.

Rationale: This is useful for graded belief. When “belief” means categorical acceptance, separately state whether the person accepts the proposition for assertion or reasoning. The relation between those attitudes and graded confidence is contested; assigning a number does not settle it.

3. Truth

2nd-Order Concept: Truth
Working phrase: The proposition’s being as it says things are, rather than anyone’s confidence in it.

Rationale: This modest working use keeps correctness separate from an estimate of correctness. “The card is blue” can be true before anyone opens the envelope. It therefore cannot be faithfully collapsed into maximum credence or unanimous agreement. A complete theory of truth remains outside this glossary.

4. Fact

2nd-Order Concept: Fact
Working phrase: For the essay’s stipulated usage, a proposition assigned high credence by most relevant experts.

Rationale: The phrase identifies an expert-consensus claim. State the field, proposition, date and evidence for that consensus. When “fact” means an actual state of affairs or a true proposition, preserve that different meaning: acceptance by experts is evidence to assess, not what makes the state of affairs obtain.

5. Evidence

2nd-Order Concept: Evidence
Working phrase: Information that bears on how much credence a proposition deserves, given relevant background assumptions.

Rationale: Evidence may support or undermine a claim. A broken seal can reduce confidence that an envelope’s contents are unchanged. Persuasive rhetoric may raise actual confidence without providing evidence, so the phrase concerns warranted revision rather than any psychological effect.

6. Demonstration

2nd-Order Concept: Demonstration
Working phrase: An inspectable presentation of reasoning or a procedure offered to establish a specified claim.

Rationale: State what was shown and under which conditions. A demonstration of one device working is evidence about that trial; it does not establish that every device will work. Increased confidence is a possible result, not the criterion that makes the demonstration adequate.

7. Proof

2nd-Order Concept: Proof
Working phrase: In a specified formal system, a derivation from its premises or axioms using its permitted inference rules.

Rationale: Valid steps do the work, not the audience’s conviction. A persuasive invalid argument is not a formal proof. In empirical or ordinary uses of “proof,” state the relevant evidential standard instead of importing the formal sense automatically.

8. Rationality

2nd-Order Concept: Rationality
Collapsed Phrase: Consistency in assigning and updating credences based on evidence and logical principles.

Rationale: This addresses epistemic rationality: how well confidence responds to reasons. Mere consistency permits coherent but poorly supported beliefs. Decisions also involve goals and consequences, which this credence-based phrase does not by itself evaluate.

9. Reasonable

2nd-Order Concept: Reasonable
Collapsed Phrase: Holding credences that are proportionate to the available evidence.

Rationale: This is an evidential use of “reasonable.” Disagreement can remain over what the available evidence supports, so name the evidence and the standard rather than treating the adjective as a certificate of correctness.

10. Assent to

2nd-Order Concept: Assent to
Working phrase: Accept a proposition as true for the purpose under discussion, while separately reporting confidence.

Rationale: Acceptance is not just any significant credence. Someone can assign a proposition substantial probability yet withhold assent. An agreed threshold is a local rule, not a universal feature of belief.

11. Confidence

2nd-Order Concept: Confidence
Working phrase: Degree of credence in a specified proposition; for trust in a source, specify what performance is expected.

Rationale: Confidence can be low as well as high. Confidence in a report and confidence in the reporter’s general reliability answer different questions. Keeping the object explicit prevents one from standing in for the other.

Conclusion

Parsimony helps when each replacement exposes the claim’s components. It fails when truth, justification and agreement become different names for confidence. Use credence where it clarifies the attitude, and retain additional anchors where the argument depends on them.

Gemini response · editorial edition

  1. Truth: High expert credence can replace “accepted as true by these experts,” but cannot by itself replace “true.” An expert community can agree and still be mistaken. This proposed collapse therefore needs a scope label; otherwise it erases the possibility of correcting a consensus.
  2. Misconception: “An inaccurate conception of the matter.” If it is also strongly held despite counterevidence, describe that additional feature through credence. Lack of justification alone does not establish falsity, and a misconception need not be confidently held.
  3. Doubt: “Reservation about accepting a proposition, with its grounds and degree specified.” Conflicting evidence may cause doubt, but so may missing information. A person can retain fairly high credence while doubting enough to seek another check.
  4. Reasonableness: This can be collapsed into “using sound methods of reasoning with available evidence.” Reasonableness is about approaching claims in a logical and well-supported way. Therefore, it becomes the act of employing reliable reasoning methods (sound methods) to analyze the information at hand (available evidence).
  5. Agreement: “Matching judgments about the same specified issue.” Matching credences are one case; shared acceptance is another. Two people can agree on a probability while recommending different actions because they face different consequences. Specify what they agree about before claiming convergence.

Claude response · editorial edition

Here are some suggestions for collapsing 2nd-order epistemological concepts/terms into phrases anchored by 1st-order terms, along with the rationale:

  1. Justification → Reasons that warrant the assigned credence.
    Rationale: The fit between support and confidence matters, not merely the presence of a supporting reason. A low credence can be well justified. This formulation leaves the standards for good reasons open to further discussion.
  2. Rationality → Forming and updating credences through valid reasoning and evidence evaluation
    Rationale: Rationality is often defined as thinking, reasoning, or behaving in a way that accords with logic and evidence. By grounding it in the concepts of credence formation/updating and evidence evaluation, we relate it to more basic epistemological processes.
  3. A priori knowledge → Knowledge whose justification does not depend on experience.
    Rationale: The distinction concerns the source of justification, not a confidence threshold. Experience may still be needed to acquire the concepts. A credence-based discussion can describe confidence justified a priori, but should not call every extremely confident hunch a priori knowledge. See a priori justification.
  4. Certainty → Maximal credence, when psychological certainty is intended.
    Rationale: On a 0–1 probability scale, maximal means 1; a value close to 1 still leaves doubt. Evidential certainty is a separate claim about the adequacy of support. Feeling no doubt does not establish it.
  5. Validity → No possible case, under the specified interpretation, in which all premises are true and the conclusion false.
    Rationale: This describes deductive validity. It does not require that the actual premises are true, and it is not measured by persuasiveness. Other kinds of good reasoning may support a conclusion without deductively guaranteeing it.

These phrases reduce hidden commitments only if the anchors are themselves understood. The formal representation of belief distinguishes categorical belief from degrees of belief; neither should silently replace the other. A compact glossary can record that distinction without resolving every theory of belief.

Gemini response · editorial edition

Most Problematic Terms in Epistemology (Ranked by Semantic Dissent)

Here is a ten-term working ranking, prioritizing terms with competing definitions and different consequences for what speakers accept. It is a reasoned discussion aid, not an empirical estimate of how often each term causes disagreement:

  1. Knowledge: This is arguably the most contested term. Philosophers debate the necessary and sufficient conditions for knowledge, leading to disagreements about what counts as “knowing” something.
  2. Truth: Speakers dispute theories of truth and sometimes shift between “true,” “believed” and “accepted here.” A subjective subject matter, such as someone’s taste, does not by itself make every statement about it true for whoever asserts it.
  3. Justification: While seemingly straightforward, the level and type of evidence needed for justification is a point of contention. Some argue for strong justifications, while others accept weaker forms.
  4. Belief: While seemingly simple, the strength and basis of belief can vary. Is a hunch a belief? What about a deeply held conviction lacking strong evidence?
  5. Certainty: The spectrum of certainty is debated. Is there ever true certainty, or is it always a matter of degree?
  6. Reasonableness: The word can assess evidence, conduct or practical choices. A conclusion reached through a poor inference might nevertheless have good independent support; that would not repair the inference.
  7. Evidence: Disciplines and questions differ in what bears on a claim and how strongly. An experiment, an argument and testimony do different work; there is no single opposition in which philosophy simply demands more than science.
  8. Objectivity: The degree to which knowledge and justification can be objective is debated. Do our experiences and biases influence our understanding?
  9. Understanding: Understanding goes beyond simply knowing something is true. The depth and nature of comprehension can vary considerably.
  10. Fact: An established claim and the state of affairs it describes are easily conflated. Context changes what is being claimed or counted as established; it does not make the underlying state of affairs depend on our confidence.

Note: This is not an exhaustive list, and the degree of semantic dissent for each term can vary depending on the specific context and participants in the discussion.

Claude response · editorial edition

This seven-term ranking is a provisional judgment about potential for confusion, with truth placed first. It is not survey evidence. The subpoints explain sources of controversy; some concern theories and standards rather than the meanings of words alone:

  1. Truth – Extremely high degree of semantic dissent
    • Competing theories of truth (correspondence, coherence, pragmatic, etc.)
    • Debates over truth relativism vs. absolutism
    • Lack of agreement on truth criteria across domains
  2. Knowledge – Very high degree of semantic dissent
    • Diverse definitions (justified true belief, non-defeasible belief, etc.)
    • Debates over the possibility of genuine knowledge
    • Contextualist vs. invariantist interpretations
  3. Justification – High degree of semantic dissent
    • Foundationalism versus coherentism: whether justification ultimately depends on basic beliefs or on relations of support within a system.
    • Internalism versus externalism: whether justification depends only on factors accessible from the person’s perspective or can depend on external conditions such as reliability.
    • Differing views on the justificatory roles of reason, perception, testimony
  4. Rationality – High degree of semantic dissent
    • Competing theories (logical, epistemic, instrumental rationality)
    • Disagreements over the demandingness of rationality
    • Debates over the universality of rational norms
  5. Evidence – Moderate to high degree of semantic dissent
    • Differing views on what counts as evidence (empirical, testimonial, a priori)
    • How to distinguish and weigh evidence, including whether repeated reports are independent or copied from one source.
    • Debates over the ultimate sources of evidence
  6. Belief – Moderate degree of semantic dissent
    • Belief as a standing disposition, a presently entertained attitude or a degree of confidence.
    • Whether people can choose their beliefs directly; a substantive dispute that need not be a disagreement over the word’s meaning.
    • Differing views on the roles of belief in epistemology
  7. A priori – Moderate degree of semantic dissent
    • Disagreements over the scope and limits of a priori knowledge
    • The analytic–synthetic distinction: whether a claim’s truth depends on meaning alone or on how the world is, and how this relates to justification without experience.
    • Differing views on the epistemological status of a priori reasoning

The ordering highlights terms worth unpacking, not a settled hierarchy of dissent. Test it by asking participants for definitions and contrasting examples, then distinguish incompatible meanings from incompatible claims made with a shared meaning.

Hypothetical Dialogue Between Two Epistemologists

Epistemologist A: Let’s start by agreeing on some foundational terms for our discussion. The first term I’d like to address is credence. Can we define it as the degree of belief assigned to a proposition, ranging from 0 to 1?

Epistemologist B: That scale works as a representation of confidence, though we may only be able to give a range. For belief, shall we use credence here and flag occasions when we mean outright acceptance?

Epistemologist A: Yes. For knowledge, the essay’s practical convention is high credence with stated support. We should label that convention, because someone using knowledge to require truth is making a further claim.

Epistemologist B: Agreed. That also makes me cautious about truth: maximum credence cannot define it. We can be completely confident and mistaken. Shall we reserve true for the proposition’s being as it says things are?

Epistemologist A: Yes, as a working use rather than a complete theory of truth. For fact, the essay suggests high credence among relevant experts. We could use that phrase when we mean an expert-consensus claim.

Epistemologist B: Provided we distinguish it from a fact in the sense of an actual state of affairs. For evidence, let’s mean information bearing on how much confidence the proposition deserves, whether it supports or undermines it.

Epistemologist A: That includes evidence against our preferred claim. For epistemic rationality, let’s ask whether our credences and revisions fit the evidence and our stated inference rules.

Epistemologist B: And reasonable, in this discussion, can mean proportionate to the available evidence. We still need to explain why that evidence warrants a particular degree of confidence.

Epistemologist A: Agreed. For proof, can we distinguish a formal derivation using permitted rules from strong empirical support? Calling both proof without qualification would restore the ambiguity.

Epistemologist B: Yes. A formal proof does not depend on convincing us. A demonstration can instead be an inspectable presentation of reasoning or a procedure, with its scope stated.

Epistemologist A: Then a demonstration of one device working establishes less than a claim about every device. Finally, confidence can mean degree of credence, including low confidence; trust in a source needs its own specified claim.

Epistemologist B: That gives us a useful draft glossary. Let’s test it on one case before treating it as settled: if every expert expects a red card but the envelope contains blue, confidence and truth come apart.

Epistemologist A: And we can describe that case without abandoning the essay’s goal. Let’s retain the useful translations, mark their limits and revise the glossary when a new case exposes an ambiguity.

Emi: Perhaps we should discuss the idea of a “community of inquiry“. Our credence in some propositions might be influenced by the consensus of experts in a particular field.
