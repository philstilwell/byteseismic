Cromwell’s Rule: An Elaboration

Cromwell’s Rule was named by statistician Dennis Lindley after Oliver Cromwell, not after Lindley himself. It cautions against assigning certainty or impossibility to empirical hypotheses that evidence might reasonably challenge. Lindley discusses the rule in §6.8 of Understanding Uncertainty (2006). The slogan needs the qualifications below.

Understanding Cromwell’s Rule

In Bayesian statistics, prior probabilities represent our initial beliefs about the likelihood of events before considering new evidence. Cromwell’s Rule specifically addresses the dangers of assigning absolute certainty (1) or impossibility (0) to these priors.

  1. Prior Probabilities: These are the probabilities assigned to events before observing any new data. They reflect our initial state of belief or knowledge.
  2. Bayesian Updating: This is the process of adjusting our prior probabilities based on new evidence to obtain posterior probabilities.
Why Avoid 0 and 1?

For a particular hypothesis, assigning prior mass 0 or 1 excludes one side of the comparison within the model. This can prevent ordinary updating from correcting a mistaken exclusion. Probability zero does not universally mean logical impossibility: in a continuous uniform distribution on [0,1], every exact value has probability zero although some value must occur.

  1. Updating limit: P(H | E) = P(H ∩ E)/P(E). If P(H)=0 and P(E)>0, the numerator is zero; if P(H)=1, its complement remains zero. If P(E)=0, this elementary ratio is undefined, not a proof that a contradictory observation changes nothing. One may need a different conditional construction or a revised model.
  2. Practical Considerations: In the real world, few events are absolutely certain or impossible. Allowing for some degree of uncertainty (i.e., probabilities between 0 and 1) is more practical and realistic.
  3. Scope: Logical tautologies and contradictions receive 1 and 0 in a classical probability model. Continuous point probabilities and conditioning assumptions also require care. The rule is not a ban on every zero-probability event, nor does it settle human certainty about a complicated proof.
Implications of Cromwell’s Rule
  1. Flexibility in Belief Revision: By avoiding 0 or 1, we remain open to updating our beliefs in light of new evidence, fostering a more flexible and adaptive mindset.
  2. Avoiding Dogmatism: This rule helps prevent dogmatic thinking, where beliefs are held rigidly regardless of contrary evidence.
  3. Rationality: Leaving live hypotheses positive support preserves the possibility of learning from them. It does not ensure good calibration: an arbitrarily tiny positive prior can still make revision impractically difficult.
Applications of Cromwell’s Rule
  1. Scientific Inquiry: Scientists often adopt Cromwell’s Rule to maintain an open-minded approach to hypotheses, allowing them to be updated or discarded based on experimental data.
  2. Risk Assessment: In fields like finance and engineering, where decisions are based on probabilistic models, avoiding absolute priors helps in making more robust and adaptable predictions.
  3. Decisions: Revisable beliefs can inform decisions, but probabilities alone do not determine action. Costs, benefits, and available choices matter; decisive action does not require absolute certainty.
Examples
  1. Scientific Hypotheses: Suppose a scientist is testing a new hypothesis. Assigning a prior probability of 0 to the hypothesis would mean they are certain it is false, regardless of any evidence that may arise. By assigning a prior probability slightly above 0, the scientist remains open to considering evidence that might support the hypothesis.
  2. Diagnostic illustration: A model that assigns a disease zero prior mass cannot recover it by ordinary conditioning on positive-probability evidence. A clinician can nevertheless reconsider the model and its assumptions. The example illustrates a mathematical limitation, not a clinical rule or guaranteed outcome.
Conclusion

The practical lesson is to avoid excluding plausible empirical alternatives without justification. Preserve support for hypotheses that may deserve reconsideration, while distinguishing logical impossibility, probability-zero points, observation assumptions, and revision of the model itself.

Cromwell’s Rule in Statistics

Cromwell’s Rule was named by Dennis Lindley after Oliver Cromwell. It is a caution about dogmatic prior assignments, not an additional probability axiom forbidding every occurrence of 0 or 1.

The Core Idea: Avoiding Overconfidence

The core idea behind Cromwell’s rule is to avoid overconfidence in our initial beliefs, also known as prior probabilities. Assigning a probability of 1 to an event suggests absolute certainty, while 0 implies impossibility. In most real-world scenarios, such certainties are rare.

Why Not Absolute Values?

There are two main reasons to avoid absolute probabilities:

  1. Limited knowledge: Zero prior mass for a hypothesis prevents positive-probability evidence from giving that hypothesis positive posterior mass under ordinary conditioning. It does not physically prevent surprising evidence from arriving or stop a person from replacing the model.
  2. Prior sensitivity: A nonzero prior allows updating, but how much the posterior changes depends on the prior odds and likelihood ratio. Report sensitivity to reasonable prior choices rather than treating any number strictly between 0 and 1 as equally open-minded.

Exceptions: Logically True or False Statements

In standard arithmetic, 2 + 2 = 4 and 2 + 2 = 5 are respectively true and false, and can receive 1 and 0 within that framework. This mathematical assignment differs from a person’s confidence that they have interpreted a statement correctly. Probability-zero outcomes in continuous models are another important qualification.

Cromwell’s Rule in Action

For a coin with unknown head probability θ, distinguish P(heads next) from a prior distribution over θ. A prior concentrated entirely at θ=0.5 remains fixed there after three heads. A Beta(1,1) prior, spread over θ, updates after three heads to Beta(4,1), with next-head predictive probability 4/5. Thus a predictive probability of 0.5 does not itself show that uncertainty about fairness was modeled.

In conclusion, Cromwell’s rule encourages a more nuanced approach to probability judgements. By acknowledging the limitations of our knowledge and allowing for new information, we can make more reliable and robust inferences from data.

For ordinary conditioning with P(E)>0, prior mass zero or one for H remains zero or one. That is a property of the updating rule, not a claim that humans cannot change their assumptions. This is essential for making rational and evidence-based decisions in situations where there is inherent uncertainty or incomplete information.

When absolute credence blocks learning within a model

“Epistemic blindness” here is a metaphor for excluding an alternative that later evidence should make us reconsider. It is not a diagnosis entailed by writing 0 or 1. The mathematical restriction concerns ordinary conditioning in a fixed model; psychological rigidity and model revision are separate matters.

Understanding Epistemic Blindness
  1. Definition: Epistemic blindness is a state where one is unable to see or consider alternative possibilities due to rigid belief structures.
  2. Consequences: This rigidity can lead to a failure to recognize new information, resulting in a static and potentially flawed understanding of the world.
The Role of Credence in Epistemic Processes

Credence refers to the degree of belief or probability that we assign to a proposition. In Bayesian terms, this is represented as a value between 0 and 1.

  1. Non-extreme credence: Positive probability for both H and its complement allows their relative support to change with diagnostic evidence, but does not guarantee that a person will seek or fairly assess that evidence.
  2. Endpoint assignment: Zero or one fixes an event’s probability under positive-probability conditioning in the same model. It does not prohibit changing the model, and it is not always equivalent to logical impossibility or necessity.
Why Absolute Credence Leads to Epistemic Blindness
  1. Inflexibility to New Evidence:
    • Bayesian conditioning: When P(E)>0, conditioning preserves an event’s zero or unit prior probability. If P(E)=0, the elementary formula cannot be applied. A person may respond by revising the model rather than merely updating its existing probabilities.
    • Learning from Evidence: When we assign a non-absolute credence, we acknowledge that our current understanding may be incomplete or flawed, and we remain open to learning from new evidence.
  2. Dogmatic Belief Systems:
    • Dogmatism: Unjustified certainty can accompany resistance to evidence, but an endpoint assignment does not by itself establish a psychological bias. Some zeros are appropriate structural assumptions or continuous point probabilities.
    • Closed-mindedness: By refusing to consider alternative possibilities, individuals with absolute credence can become closed-minded, dismissing new information that could refine or correct their beliefs.
  3. Undermining Rational Decision-Making:
    • Rationality: Unwarranted exclusion of a live alternative can distort decisions. Whether an endpoint is defensible depends on the proposition, model, evidence, and purpose; the rule does not condemn every use of 0 or 1.
    • Error in Judgment: Decisions based on absolute credence can be flawed, as they ignore the probabilistic nature of most real-world events and the potential for error in our initial assessments.
Examples Illustrating Epistemic Blindness
  1. Scientific Rigor:
    • Hypothesis testing: An excluded hypothesis cannot gain posterior mass by ordinary conditioning in a fixed model. Scientists can also enlarge or replace their models; that is a different kind of revision.
    • Scientific Progress: Scientific advancements depend on the ability to revise theories. Epistemic blindness resulting from absolute credence stifles this progress.
  2. Medical Diagnosis:
    • Diagnostic flexibility: An omitted or zero-weight possibility may call for reconsidering the model when new evidence arrives. The mathematical restriction does not force a clinician to ignore symptoms or tests.
    • Outcomes: Openness to relevant evidence can improve reasoning, but does not ensure a correct diagnosis or better outcome in every case. Evidence quality and appropriate action also matter.
  3. Everyday Decision-Making:
    • Risk Assessment: When assessing risks, individuals benefit from maintaining flexible beliefs. An absolute credence of 1 in the safety of a particular activity, despite new evidence suggesting otherwise, can lead to dangerous outcomes.
    • Financial Decisions: Investors who assign absolute credence to market trends without considering new data risk significant financial loss. Keeping a non-absolute credence allows for adaptive strategies based on emerging market conditions.
Conclusion

Unjustified endpoint priors can block learning within a fixed model. Keep this conditional mathematical claim separate from the stronger, unsupported claim that every person assigning 0 or 1 is closed-minded. Good inquiry also checks prior sensitivity and revises inadequate models.

  • Excluded alternatives: A zero-weight hypothesis cannot recover through ordinary conditioning on positive-probability evidence. This is not the same as saying that every zero-probability event is impossible.

  • Limited Learning: By refusing to consider alternative possibilities (0 probability) or dismissing new evidence (1 probability), we hinder our ability to learn and update our beliefs. This limits our understanding of the world and our ability to adapt to changing circumstances.

By avoiding absolute credences, we:

  • Remain Open-Minded: We remain receptive to new information and changing circumstances.
  • Acknowledge Uncertainty: We recognize the inherent limitations of our knowledge.

For a hypothesis H in a fixed model, prior 0 means exclusion and prior 1 means full commitment—the original response reversed their order. Ordinary conditioning on evidence of positive probability preserves those values. This may impede learning when the exclusion was unjustified, but it does not make model revision impossible.

Epistemic blindness arises because:

  1. Inflexibility within a model: A zero or unit prior remains so under well-defined elementary conditioning. Evidence with model probability zero makes that formula undefined; it may expose inadequate assumptions.
  2. Ignoring uncertainty: In most real-world situations, there is inherent uncertainty and incomplete information. Assigning probabilities of 0 or 1 ignores this uncertainty and treats the proposition as if it were a logical certainty, which is rarely the case.
  3. Confirmation bias: A prior of 0 or 1 can reinforce confirmation bias, where one tends to seek out or interpret evidence in a way that confirms their existing belief, while ignoring or dismissing contradictory evidence.
  4. Closed-mindedness: By holding an absolute belief or disbelief, one becomes closed-minded to alternative perspectives, new information, or counterarguments that may challenge the existing position.

Epistemic blindness is problematic because it can lead to:

  1. Flawed decision-making: Decisions based on absolute certainty or disbelief may be sub-optimal or even detrimental, as they fail to account for potential new information or alternative possibilities.
  2. Missed opportunities: By dismissing propositions or hypotheses outright, one may overlook valuable insights or opportunities that could have been uncovered by remaining open to new evidence.
  3. Perpetuating biases: Epistemic blindness can reinforce existing biases and prevent the correction of erroneous beliefs or assumptions.

To avoid epistemic blindness, it is generally recommended to maintain a degree of uncertainty and openness to revising beliefs based on new evidence, even for propositions that seem highly plausible or implausible initially. This aligns with the principles of Cromwell’s Rule, which advises against assigning absolute probabilities (0 or 1) to non-logically certain propositions.
