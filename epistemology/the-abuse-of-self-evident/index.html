Prompt 1: When an idea is under scrutiny, its defenders occasionally claim the idea is “self-evident” and needs no argumentation or evidence. Is there any philosophical weight behind this notion of “self-evident”? Are there cases when ideas claimed to be “self-evident” were demonstrated to be false?

Self-evidence and apparent obviousness

ChatGPT response · editorial edition

The notion of “self-evidence” holds a significant philosophical weight, especially in epistemology, the branch of philosophy concerned with the nature and scope of knowledge. A self-evident proposition is one that is known to be true simply by understanding it; its truth does not require proof or further argumentation. This concept is deeply rooted in the history of philosophy, with thinkers like Aristotle, Thomas Aquinas, and René Descartes discussing the nature of self-evident truths.

  1. Philosophical Significance of Self-Evidence:
    • Aristotle introduced the idea of self-evident principles in his work, especially concerning logic and ethics, suggesting that some truths are foundational and do not require demonstration.
    • Aquinas distinguishes self-evidence in itself from self-evidence to us. He explicitly denies that God’s existence is self-evident to us and argues that it requires demonstration from effects. See Summa Theologiae I, question 2, article 1.
    • René Descartes famously sought indubitable foundations for knowledge, positing that the existence of the self as a thinking thing (“I think, therefore I am”) is self-evident.
  2. Criteria for Self-Evidence:
    • A proposition is self-evident if, upon understanding it, one recognizes its truth immediately.
    • Self-evidence often requires a certain level of intellectual maturity and understanding; what is self-evident to one person may not be so to another.
    • Accounts differ: some link self-evidence to understanding alone, while others discuss non-inferential warrant or rational intuition. Do not equate every account with necessity or with the claim that denial is formally contradictory.
  3. Controversies and Challenges:
    • A claim being disputed is not the same as its being demonstrated false. The Declaration of Independence illustrates explicit political use of “self-evident”; disagreement about equality or inconsistent implementation does not establish that moral equality is false.
    • The challenge with self-evident truths lies in the subjective nature of understanding and the different levels of insight among individuals. What one culture or individual may hold as self-evident, others may question or reject.
    • Relativity and quantum theory challenged familiar physical expectations. Without identifying a particular writer and text, these should be described as overturned intuitions, not documented uses of the exact philosophical claim of self-evidence.
  4. Conclusion:
    • While the notion of self-evidence plays a crucial role in the foundations of knowledge and ethics, its application is nuanced and complex. Philosophical scrutiny and the progress of human knowledge continually test the boundaries of what is considered self-evident.
    • It is important to approach claims of self-evidence with a critical mindset, acknowledging the context in which such claims are made and considering the possibility of alternative perspectives or new evidence.

In sum, while the concept of self-evidence is philosophically significant, history and ongoing inquiry show that its application requires careful consideration and openness to revision.

Gemini response · editorial edition

Absolutely! Here’s a breakdown of the idea of “self-evident” truths and some of the issues surrounding them:

The Concept of ‘Self-Evidence’

  • Immediate understanding: Some accounts propose that understanding a proposition can warrant belief without inference. That is stronger than merely feeling certain. Even 1+1=2 is a theorem rather than a primitive axiom in familiar formalizations of arithmetic.
  • Starting point: Accepting a premise does not make subsequent reasoning valid; the inference must still be checked. An axiom can be stipulated for a system without being declared self-evident.

Philosophical Debate

There’s significant philosophical disagreement around self-evident truths:

  • Existence: Do they actually exist? Some philosophers argue that nothing is truly self-evident, and all concepts require some level of justification. They maintain that what seems self-evident often reflects cultural conditioning rather than an absolute truth.
  • The Basis of Knowledge: Philosophers who do believe in self-evident truths still debate their role. Some believe they are the foundation of all knowledge, while others argue that even self-evident truths need some external justification or connection to experience.

Historical ‘Self-Evident’ Truths That Were Wrong

Several historical beliefs illustrate the fallibility of apparent obviousness. The examples here do not all document an explicit contemporary use of the words “self-evident.”

  • Geocentrism: The seemingly obvious idea that the Earth is the center of the universe needed a lot of arguing (and evidence) to be refuted.
  • Euclidean geometry: Non-Euclidean systems show that alternative geometric postulates can be studied consistently under appropriate assumptions. They do not refute Euclidean theorems within Euclidean geometry; application to physical space is a separate empirical question.
  • Intuitive Notions of Physics: Before Einstein, everyday observations made concepts like the absolute nature of time seem self-evident. Relativity radically changed those assumptions.
  • Social ‘Truisms’: Throughout history, many harmful beliefs about race, gender, and social hierarchy were widely accepted as self-evident. These have been challenged and debunked.

Why Studying This Matters

Investigating the concept of “self-evidence” is important for several reasons:

  • Critical Thinking: It encourages us to question our deeply held assumptions and think critically about the origins of our beliefs.
  • Intellectual Humility: Recognizing that “obvious” truths have been wrong before fosters intellectual humility and an openness to changing our minds.
  • Combating Dogmatism: Understanding how ‘self-evidence’ is used in arguments helps us see how it can be used to shut down discussion rather than encourage open inquiry.

You said, “The term “self-evident” captures the intuitive appeal of ethical egoism. It suggests that the principle resonates with an internal, almost instinctive understanding of human motivation.“

Yes. The original response confused an explanation of motivation with justification of a moral principle. A natural inclination does not by itself establish what one ought to do.

What seems intuitively true or self-evident to one person based on their motivations or experiences may not be so for another. This variability challenges the universality and objectivity typically associated with self-evident truths, which are supposed to be recognized as true by all rational beings.
  2. Cognitive Biases: Relying on internal motivation or intuition as a basis for self-evidence introduces the risk of cognitive biases. Human beings are susceptible to a range of biases that can distort their understanding and judgment, making it problematic to equate intuitive or instinctive feelings with epistemic justification.
  3. Public defense: A claim that motivation establishes a moral truth needs a defensible connection between those facts and the conclusion. Private experience can be evidence in some contexts, but desire alone does not supply that normative bridge.
  4. Philosophical Rigor: Philosophical inquiry strives for rigor and clarity in its arguments and justifications. While intuition and internal motivations are important aspects of human experience and can inform philosophical reflection, they alone do not suffice for the rigorous justification of knowledge claims. Philosophy often demands more than intuitive appeal; it seeks arguments that can withstand critical scrutiny and can be communicated, debated, and understood across differing perspectives.
  5. Empirical Evidence and Rational Argumentation: The strength of empirical evidence and rational argumentation lies in their ability to be shared, scrutinized, and validated across different observers and contexts. This external, objective approach to knowledge claims allows for a more reliable and universal foundation for establishing truths, compared to the inherently subjective and variable nature of internal motivations or intuitions.

In conclusion, while intuitive appeal and internal motivations play a significant role in shaping human beliefs and perspectives, relying on them as the sole basis for claims of self-evidence does present epistemically inappropriate challenges. This does not mean, however, that intuition or motivation has no place in epistemology or philosophy; rather, it underscores the need for a careful balance between internal experiences and the rigorous demands of epistemic justification.
