1Original prompt · edited responses

Prompt 1: Assess the following statement against the notion that rational belief is a degree of belief that maps to the degree of the relevant evidence.

Assessing the quoted claim

It seems to me that, if you have more evidence for one view rather than for another view, then you are intellectually obligated to believe the one that is most reasonable, that is most likely, and not believe the one that is least likely.

Greg Koukl — STR Podcast — June 28, 2023

ChatGPT response · editorial edition

The quoted statement invites the curator’s concern: it tells us which view to believe without saying how confidently to believe it. But “believe” does not necessarily mean “be absolutely certain.” A charitable reading is that we should favor the best-supported view while retaining uncertainty. The problem arises when that preference is treated as eliminating the remaining doubt.

On the prompt’s account, rational confidence should answer to the quality and relevance of the evidence. Counting favorable claims is insufficient: ten reports copied from one witness do not supply ten independent observations. Nor does being ahead establish a high probability. Among three mutually exclusive, exhaustive explanations assigned 40%, 35% and 25%, the leader is still more likely false than true.

For a proposition and its negation, 50% marks equal confidence, and moving above it reverses which is favored. That is a meaningful comparison, not a command to become certain. A move from 49% to 51% is a small confidence change even if a verbal label changes. The quotation itself specifies neither this cutoff nor certainty.

The strongest criticism therefore targets an omitted distinction: ranking alternatives does not settle how much confidence each deserves. The curator can insist on proportionate commitment without attributing an explicit claim of infallibility to the quoted speaker.

Claude response · editorial edition

There are two readings worth separating. “Prefer the most reasonable view” can express sensible comparative judgment. “Give it unqualified epistemic commitment merely because it leads” would overstate the evidence. The quotation leaves the strength of the proposed belief unspecified.

The gradient account measures confidence in a proposition, often called credence. Categorical acceptance is a different description of one’s attitude. Someone can accept an explanation while acknowledging a substantial chance of error; neither acceptance nor rejection automatically means certainty.

Suppose two exhaustive alternatives receive 51% and 49% confidence. Favoring the first does not warrant treating the second as impossible. With three or more alternatives, even the leader can remain below 50%. These examples expose the missing degree of commitment without assuming a precise number is available in every real dispute.

The curator’s requirement is best defended as a constraint on confidence: let its strength track evidential support. Whether ordinary belief language should be replaced by graded language, or used alongside it, is a further philosophical question. The quoted sentence alone does not resolve that question.

Prompt 2: How might you respond to such a statement to demonstrate that belief is not binary and that there is no special threshold along the epistemic gradient that demands a binary flipping of our epistemic commitment from disbelief to belief?

Confidence without a compulsory flip

ChatGPT response · editorial edition

A response can defend the curator’s gradient account while distinguishing confidence, acceptance and action:

  1. Explain the spectrum: A dimmer switch illustrates differing confidence levels. The analogy concerns our confidence, not degrees of truth: an ordinary factual proposition may be true or false while our confidence lies between certainty and complete rejection.
  2. Explain its practical value: “I favor this explanation slightly” and “the evidence strongly supports it” guide inquiry differently. A slight lead can warrant further checking; strong independent support can warrant relying on the conclusion while remaining open to correction.
  3. Challenge the compulsory flip: Ask what justifies converting a narrow evidential lead into unqualified commitment. The midpoint distinguishes which of two exhaustive alternatives is favored; it does not erase uncertainty. A threshold for acceptance needs its own account and must not be smuggled in as a threshold of certainty.
  4. Use a concrete example: A forecast moving from 49% to 51% rain changes confidence only slightly. You might nevertheless cross your personal threshold for carrying an umbrella. Its inconvenience and the cost of getting wet affect that choice, not how likely rain is.
  5. Connect the argument to Bayesian reasoning: Bayesian epistemology models confidence and its revision with evidence. It permits large revisions when evidence is highly informative; a range of possible confidence levels does not imply slow or smooth change over time. See the Stanford discussion of credence and updating.

The conclusion is limited but strong: evidence favoring one view does not by itself justify suppressing residual uncertainty. This defends proportional confidence without pretending to have refuted every philosophical account of categorical belief.

Claude response · editorial edition

One direct reply would be:

“I agree that our assessment should favor the better-supported explanation. But how strongly should it favor it? If the support is only slightly greater, our confidence should reflect that small advantage, not become unqualified commitment.

A gradient describes the available degrees of confidence; it does not require every update to be small. A decisive observation can justify a major change. What needs justification is the size of that change, not whether we have crossed a word such as “accept.”

Even when one view is more likely than its negation, uncertainty can remain substantial. Categorical language becomes misleading when it conceals that fact. People can also be dogmatic about a numerical estimate, so merely using percentages does not ensure openness.

Instead of framing it as an intellectual obligation to ‘believe the one that is most reasonable,’ I would suggest that we have an obligation to calibrate our degree of belief to the degree of supporting evidence, while remaining open to revising our views as the evidence evolves. This allows for a more nuanced, flexible, and rational approach to belief formation and epistemology.”

This reply preserves the curator’s focus on evidentially proportionate commitment. It challenges an unsupported leap to certainty while allowing practical choices and revisable acceptance.

Prompt 3: Create a dialogue featuring a gradient belief proponent and a binary belief proponent.

Two dialogues about commitment

ChatGPT response · editorial edition

Gradient Belief Proponent (GBP): I think it’s important to recognize that belief is more like a spectrum than a binary state. Wouldn’t you agree?

Binary Belief Proponent (BBP): Not necessarily. In my view, you either believe something or you don’t. There’s a clear line there.

GBP: Can that clear distinction coexist with degrees of confidence? I can accept a claim while being less confident in it than in another claim I accept.

BBP: Simple, yes, but also clear. You’re either convinced of something, or you aren’t. That clarity is crucial for decision-making.

GBP: Suppose I am 70% confident an investment will succeed and 30% confident it will fail. Those are complementary outcomes of one proposition. Shouldn’t the remaining risk affect my decision?

BBP: Sure, but if the available choice is invest or decline, I still have to make a definite decision.

GBP: It’s true that the decision itself might be binary, but your belief in the success of the investment isn’t. Your belief influences how much you invest or the risks you’re willing to take.

BBP: Then perhaps categorical belief means accepting a proposition for reasoning, without pretending it is certain. Why abandon that useful distinction?

GBP: We need not abandon a useful label. My objection is to letting it conceal confidence. A scientist can provisionally accept a theory while specifying how strongly the evidence supports it.

BBP: Yet, when making practical applications, scientists must decide based on the current belief—use it or not. That’s binary.

GBP: That decision is binary, yes, but the underlying belief that guides those decisions isn’t. It adjusts as more data becomes available.

BBP: So you’re saying that this gradient belief actually helps in making more nuanced decisions?

GBP: Yes. We can state the decision, the confidence and what would change our assessment. A categorical label alone leaves those distinctions unstated.

BBP: But isn’t there a risk of never really committing to a belief if it’s always seen as adjustable?

GBP: On the contrary, it means our commitments can be proportional to what we know, and can change appropriately as we learn more.

BBP: I suppose that permits revision. But sometimes new evidence should produce a drastic shift, shouldn’t it?

GBP: Yes. Degrees of confidence allow large as well as small changes. A gradient is not a rule that we must change our minds slowly.

BBP: So the proposition can have a yes-or-no answer even when our confidence in that answer is graded.

GBP: Exactly. The uncertainty belongs to our assessment; it need not belong to the truth of the proposition.

BBP: Perhaps it’s worth considering more deeply, especially in complex situations where the evidence is varied.

GBP: And the dimmer-switch analogy helps keep the distinction visible: the brightness represents confidence, not a partially true claim.

BBP: Well, you’ve given me a lot to think about. Maybe the spectrum of belief is more practical than I initially thought.

Claude response · editorial edition

Here is the original nineteen-turn exchange, editorially revised to give both positions substantive arguments:

Gradient Belief Proponent (GBP): Taking a stance need not conceal how confident we are. A narrow lead in the evidence warrants a different commitment from overwhelming support.

Binary Belief Proponent (BBP): But I either accept a claim or I do not. That distinction helps me reason and communicate without attaching a percentage to every sentence.

GBP: It can help. My concern is what the label leaves out: acceptance at modest confidence should not sound indistinguishable from near certainty.

BBP: Couldn’t we use an acceptance threshold while still recording confidence separately? A change in category need not mean a jump to certainty.

GBP: That would avoid the central mistake. The threshold would govern your use of “accept,” while your confidence would still need to reflect the evidence.

BBP: Then how do you avoid endless suspension of judgment when an answer is needed?

GBP: Not at all. We should calibrate our degree of belief to the degree of supporting evidence, while remaining open to revision.

BBP: I still need a definite policy. A committee cannot always enact a 60%-committed decision.

GBP: It can enact one policy and disclose uncertain predictions. The decision rule also depends on the consequences of error, which are separate from the probability estimate.

BBP: Does leaving room for error undermine strong convictions that guide our lives?

GBP: Strong confidence can be warranted. The relevant question is whether the evidence supports its strength, and what contrary evidence would lead us to reconsider.

BBP: I can agree to revisability. But ordinary belief never required an oath to ignore evidence; your criticism should address a misuse of the category.

GBP: Agreed. The target is treating a modest evidential advantage as if it erased doubt, not every use of “I believe.”

BBP: Then there is room for a firm, categorical belief whose holder admits fallibility?

GBP: Not at all. We can have strong beliefs when the evidence is overwhelming, but we should always remain open to revising them.

BBP: And if new evidence is decisive, the confidence may change sharply rather than gradually?

GBP: Yes. A range of confidence levels permits sharp changes when warranted. It rules out an automatic leap merely because a stipulated cutoff was crossed.

BBP: I would retain categorical belief for acceptance, but make the underlying degree of confidence explicit when it matters.

GBP: That preserves the practical label while meeting the curator’s central demand: epistemic commitment must not claim more support than the evidence supplies.

Prompt 4: What are other terms similar to “preponderance” that illegitimately suggest there is a threshold along the epistemic gradient at which disbelief should flip to belief?

Words that can conceal uncertainty

ChatGPT response · editorial edition

The following terms can conceal how much confidence is warranted, but none is intrinsically illegitimate. The curator’s next prompt rightly asks us to recognize appropriate strong language. The risk is an unsupported inference from a verbal commitment to certainty.

  1. Assent to: Expresses agreement; it becomes misleading if the audience is invited to infer certainty from mere assent. “I tentatively assent” can preserve the qualification.
  2. Accept: Can mean treating a claim as a working premise. Specify whether this is provisional acceptance or a claim of exceptionally strong evidence.
  3. Conclude: Can mark the result of present reasoning without making it unrevisable. State the assumptions and evidence on which the conclusion depends.
  4. Affirm: States a position positively. Qualification may be needed if the assertion is stronger than the support warrants.
  5. Endorse: Expresses support, sometimes for a practical proposal. It need not imply certainty about every prediction used to defend that proposal.
  6. Subscribe to: Can suggest identification with a theory. Clarify which claims are supported and whether the commitment survives contrary evidence.
  7. Adopt: Often describes a choice of method or working hypothesis. Adoption is not itself evidence that the hypothesis is true.
  8. Commit to: Can refer to an action or a belief. Distinguish a definite course of action from an unqualified factual judgment.

A useful test is whether the expression tells listeners more than the evidence warrants. The Ninth Circuit’s model instruction uses preponderance as a standard requiring the claim to be more likely true than false. That procedural standard does not purport to eliminate uncertainty.

The problem is the inference encouraged in context. Ask whether the speaker is expressing a practical commitment, a current judgment or certainty, and whether that strength is warranted. A list of forbidden words would miss the curator’s point about proportionate confidence.

Prompt 5: Some of the terms in the section above appear appropriate when the degree of belief is fairly high. Provide guidelines to when more nuanced terms might be necessary to encourage the mapping of our degree of certainty to the degree of the evidence.

The curator’s correction: when stronger language fits

ChatGPT response · editorial edition

The correction is sound: strong terms can fit strong evidence. Nuance is needed when the wording would obscure uncertainty, dependence on assumptions or the distinction between belief and action. Preserve that distinction in the following six situations:

1. In Early Stages of Evidence Gathering

  • Use terms like:
    • “Consider”
    • “Explore”
    • “Investigate”
  • Rationale: These terms are useful when evidence is still being collected and an initial hypothesis is being examined. They suggest openness to different outcomes and a withholding of firm commitment until more evidence is available.

2. When Evidence is Mixed or Conflicting

  • Use terms like:
    • “Lean towards”
    • “Tend to favor”
    • “Hypothesize”
  • Rationale: Use “lean toward” only when the evidence favors a direction. If it leaves you undecided, say so. “Hypothesize” identifies a possibility for investigation, not a measured level of confidence.

3. When Incremental Evidence Supports a Growing Trend

  • Use terms like:
    • “Increasingly convinced”
    • “Growing confidence in”
  • Rationale: These phrases describe a change, not a fixed confidence level. Confidence can rise from low to still low. State both the direction and the approximate strength when that distinction matters.

4. High Degree of Evidence but Still Below Certainty

  • Use terms like:
    • “Strongly believe”
    • “Highly probable”
  • Rationale: When evidence strongly supports a particular conclusion but falls short of absolute certainty, these terms convey a high level of confidence while still acknowledging the possibility of alternative explanations.

5. Communicating Statistical or Probabilistic Evidence

  • Use terms like:
    • “Likely”
    • “Unlikely”
    • “Probable”
  • Rationale: These words do not have universally precise numerical meanings. Where justified, give an estimated range, the model or evidence behind it and its limitations. Do not invent precision merely to sound calibrated.

6. Reflecting Revisions Based on New Information

  • Use terms like:
    • “Re-evaluating”
    • “Adjusting belief towards”
  • Rationale: When new evidence emerges that contradicts or modifies previous understandings, these terms effectively communicate the dynamic and revisable nature of belief.

Use language that fits the evidence rather than routinely adding hedges. Overstating doubt about a well-supported claim can mislead just as overstating confidence can. Where a practical choice is required, state the choice and the remaining uncertainty separately.

Claude response · editorial edition

Yes. The earlier list needed that qualification: none of those terms automatically means certainty. Strong evidence can warrant strong language. These seven guidelines identify when the missing qualification matters:

1) Mixed or inconclusive evidence: State whether you lean toward one view or suspend judgment. “Provisionally accept” can be appropriate; an unqualified “endorse” may conceal the unresolved conflict.

2) When new evidence is still emerging: If a body of evidence is still evolving and growing, nuanced language like “current assessment”, “present inclination”, “evidence so far suggests” etc. avoids overstating certainty prematurely.

3) Many interacting variables: Identify the assumptions most likely to change the result. Complexity alone does not establish low confidence, and “balance of probabilities” needs explanation if it hides a fragile estimate.

4) Inductive reasoning: Explain that the evidence supports the conclusion without guaranteeing it. Some inductive conclusions are extremely well supported; do not automatically weaken them to a mere guess.

5) Emotionally charged issues: Identify the actual evidence and possible sources of bias. More cautious phrasing is useful when uncertainty warrants it, but emotional controversy alone does not make a strong evidential case weak.

6) Strong prior commitments in the audience: Explain what new evidence changes and why. Respectful wording may help communication, but politeness does not determine how confident the conclusion should be.

7) Severe consequences: Make uncertainty and the cost of error explicit. High stakes can justify greater checking or a different action threshold; they do not strengthen the evidence. Reserve “compellingly indicates” for compelling evidence.

Calibration means matching expressed confidence to warranted confidence. It requires neither universal hedging nor universal percentages. Explain the support, uncertainty and revision conditions at the level the decision or discussion needs.
