Prompt 1: Prior to an examination of presuppositions, elaborate on the notion of inductive density in which our inductive experience regularly encounters invariance in phenomena that legitimately strengthen our induction-based confidence for subsequent regularity. This confidence trajectory through continued regularity approaches, in an asymptotic way, absolute confidence, but is prevented from ever reaching absolute confidence by the intrinsically subjective nature of human minds.

Inductive density

ChatGPT response · editorial edition

“Inductive density” is the conversation’s proposed name for the amount, variety, and relevance of experience supporting a regularity. It is not here a defined statistical quantity. Its value depends on sampling, dependence between observations, measurement quality, and competing explanations, not just frequency.

  1. Inductive Reasoning and Experience: At the heart of inductive reasoning lies our ability to observe patterns and regularities in the world around us. For instance, observing that the sun rises in the east and sets in the west on a daily basis forms a pattern that we expect will continue. This expectation is based on our accumulated experiences and observations, which guide our predictions about future events.
  2. Encountering Invariance: The invariance in phenomena refers to the consistency and predictability of certain patterns over time. When we regularly encounter such invariance, it reinforces our confidence in the reliability of these patterns. This process is a fundamental aspect of learning and understanding the natural world, where repeated observations of a consistent pattern (like the change of seasons) solidify our expectation that the pattern will persist.
  3. Strengthening confidence: A new observation raises confidence in a hypothesis only insofar as it favors that hypothesis over alternatives. Repeating the same report or observing a case equally expected under rival models need not add support.
  4. An asymptotic illustration: Under independent Bernoulli trials with a stable unknown success rate and a uniform prior, n successes without failures give next-trial success probability (n+1)/(n+2). This approaches one as n grows, but does not reach one at finite n. The formula depends on the model; it is not a universal law of human confidence or a proof that all propositions have this trajectory.
  5. Limits: Human subjectivity can introduce bias, but the inferential limitation is broader. Finite observations may fit several models, sampling can be unrepresentative, and future conditions can change. Those issues remain even for an ideal observer.

The useful insight is that relevant experience can strengthen provisional confidence. It does not follow that every observation increases confidence, that every confidence curve approaches one, or that subjectivity alone proves universal uncertainty.

Gemini response · editorial edition

Inductive Density and the Limits of Confidence

The concept of inductive density refers to the richness and consistency of our inductive experiences. Here’s a breakdown of your explanation and some additional points to consider:

  • Induction and Invariance: Induction is the process of drawing general conclusions from specific observations. Invariance refers to the repeated observation of a phenomenon under varying circumstances. High inductive density occurs when we encounter consistent regularities across many observations.
  • Strengthening confidence requires evidence that distinguishes the regularity from plausible alternatives, with dependence and selection effects taken into account.
  • An asymptotic approach is possible in specified models, such as the all-successes Bernoulli example. Persistent failures, changing conditions, or model uncertainty can instead make confidence level off or decrease.
  • Subjectivity can limit assessment, but finite evidence and uncertainty about the model are independent reasons not to infer unrestricted certainty from repeated observations.

Here are some additional points to ponder:

  • Counterexamples: A genuine counterexample refutes an unrestricted universal claim. For a probabilistic claim, an occasional failure may be expected; its evidential effect depends on the competing probabilities.
  • The Limits of Observation: Our observations are always limited by our senses and instruments. There could be hidden variables or unknown factors influencing the phenomenon that we haven’t accounted for.
  • The Risk of Overconfidence: High inductive density can lead to overconfidence in our predictions. It’s crucial to remember that induction is not deduction, and there’s always a chance that the future might violate our expectations.

In conclusion, inductive density is a powerful tool for building confidence in our understanding of the world. However, it’s important to acknowledge the limitations of induction due to subjectivity, the possibility of counter-examples, and the inherent limits of our observations.

Prompt 2: It appears to me that inductive density fully depicts why humans can never claim to have full certainty. There is nothing that would legitimately ground full certainty, or what is sometimes referred to as a presupposition. Is this reasoning solid?

Certainty and presupposition

ChatGPT response · editorial edition

Your reasoning supports caution about unrestricted empirical generalizations, but the stronger conclusion needs additional premises. “Presupposition” means something taken as a starting point in a context; it need not mean an absolutely certain belief.

  1. Inductive Reasoning and Its Limitations: Inductive reasoning builds on empirical observation and the identification of patterns and regularities in the natural world. While this method is powerful for developing hypotheses and theories, it inherently relies on the assumption that future occurrences will mirror past ones. This assumption, however robust based on historical data, cannot guarantee certainty because it is always possible, however unlikely, that future observations might deviate from past trends.
  2. Certainty and presuppositions differ. Psychological certainty is an absence of felt doubt; epistemic certainty concerns warrant that excludes error. A presupposition may be a revisable background assumption. An inductive limit on one kind of justification does not eliminate every useful starting assumption.
  3. More relevant evidence can support greater confidence within a specified model. There is no general theorem here that all human confidence increases asymptotically toward one.
  4. Acknowledgment of Subjectivity and Epistemological Humility: Recognizing the intrinsic subjectivity of human cognition and the limitations of our knowledge systems encourages epistemological humility. It acknowledges that our understanding of the world, no matter how refined or widely corroborated, is fundamentally provisional. This acknowledgment does not undermine the value or utility of scientific inquiry; rather, it underscores the dynamic and ever-evolving nature of knowledge and understanding.

The conclusion is defensible as fallibilism about ordinary empirical generalizations. Extending it to every mathematical, logical, or introspective claim requires a further argument, and identifying presuppositions with certainty would beg that question.

Gemini response · editorial edition

The argument makes a useful case for empirical caution, but does not yet establish that every kind of certainty is impossible or that all presuppositions claim certainty.

  • The Realm of Probability: Induction inherently deals with probabilities. No matter how high the inductive density, there’s always a tiny chance the pattern could break down in the future. This inherent probabilistic nature prevents absolute certainty.
  • The Unknown Unknowns: We can never be sure we’ve considered all relevant factors influencing a phenomenon. There might be unknown variables not yet discovered that could overturn our current understanding.

However, there are some nuances to consider:

  • Presuppositions and axioms: An axiom is a starting statement of a formal system, not automatically an assertion of infallible human knowledge. “Everything that exists occupies space” is not a mathematical axiom; abstract mathematical objects are not ordinarily assigned spatial locations.

Here, however, the “certainty” is relative to the specific system. These presuppositions might not hold true outside that system.

  • Valid deduction establishes what follows from assumptions within a specified logic. The validity of the consequence, the truth or applicability of the axioms, and a person’s fallibility in checking the proof are separate questions.

Overall, inductive density strongly suggests that full certainty for most real-world phenomena is elusive. While absolute certainty might exist in specific limited contexts like logic or specific axioms, these cases have limitations as well.

In most cases, the best we can achieve is a very high degree of confidence based on strong inductive evidence. This confidence allows us to make informed decisions and predictions, but we should always acknowledge the possibility of unforeseen circumstances.

And to say the beliefs warrant full certainty is logically incoherent. Interestingly, that also include logic since our confidence in logic also accrues to merely a high inductive density rather than to full certainty. Is this argument solid?

Logic, axioms and intuition

ChatGPT response · editorial edition

The proposal raises a serious fallibilist position, but its strongest conclusions are not established simply by restating the limitations of induction.

  1. Confidence should not exceed what its justification warrants. Identifying all justification with inductive regularity is an additional philosophical thesis, not a consequence of that proportionality principle.
  2. Unsupported certainty is not automatically a logical contradiction. To show incoherence, identify incompatible commitments. To show insufficient warrant, explain why the evidence falls short; these are different criticisms.
  3. The justification of logic is disputed. Experience can inform our trust in reasoning practices, but it does not follow that every logical principle is merely an inductive generalization. Using logic to assess an inductive justification of logic also raises a circularity question.
  4. Evaluation: The argument motivates caution about claims of infallibility. It needs independent support for the premises that all justification is inductive and that every certainty claim is logically incoherent.

However, it’s worth noting that some philosophers and logicians might argue that certain logical principles (such as the law of non-contradiction) are not inductive in nature but rather axiomatic or self-evident, serving as the necessary groundwork for any coherent epistemic or logical system. This counter-argument doesn’t necessarily invalidate your position but adds a layer of complexity to the discussion about the origins and justification of logical principles.

A defensible conclusion is that strong felt conviction alone does not establish infallibility. The universal thesis about logic remains a philosophical position requiring argument.

But axioms themselves cannot escape their dependence on induction. Axioms held by human minds have been acquired inductively. Right?

Your point touches on a nuanced debate within epistemology and the philosophy of logic about the nature and justification of axioms. Let’s explore the complexities of this issue:

  1. Axioms are accepted without proof within the system being studied. Their selection may reflect conceptual aims, consistency, mathematical fruitfulness, or empirical application; they need not be self-evident or claimed as absolute truths.
  2. Inductive vs. Deductive Justification: The distinction between inductive and deductive reasoning is crucial here. Deductive reasoning involves deriving specific conclusions from general principles or axioms, ensuring that if the premises (axioms) are true, the conclusions necessarily follow. In contrast, inductive reasoning involves generalizing from specific observations to broader generalizations, which are probabilistic rather than certain.
  3. Inductive Acquisition of Axioms?: The claim that axioms held by human minds have been acquired inductively suggests that our acceptance of these foundational principles is based on observed regularities or experiences. This perspective might argue that even our most fundamental logical or mathematical principles have been ‘tested’ against our experiences of the world and have proven consistently reliable, leading to their acceptance as axioms.
  4. Counterarguments: However, there are counterarguments to the notion that axioms are inductively acquired:
    • Self-evidence: Some accounts defend non-empirical warrant for logical principles. Distinguish excluded middle, P ∨ ¬P, from semantic bivalence, the claim that every proposition has exactly one of two truth values. Their relationship depends on the logical framework.
    • Conceptual and structural accounts: Euclidean postulates are not all consequences of definitions of point, line, and plane. Alternative geometries illustrate why choosing postulates and defining terms are different acts.
    • Necessity for Rational Thought: It’s also argued that certain axioms are necessary presuppositions for any rational inquiry, including induction itself. Without these axioms, the very process of reasoning, whether inductive or deductive, would be undermined.
  5. Acquisition versus justification: Experience may help someone learn an axiom without constituting its entire justification. Conversely, calling an axiom conceptual does not settle whether a human can be infallibly certain of it.

In summary, while it’s an intriguing proposition that axioms might be inductively acquired through a process of generalized observation and testing, there are significant philosophical arguments that challenge this view, emphasizing the non-empirical justification of axioms. This debate underscores the foundational questions about the origins of our most basic principles of thought and knowledge.

Gemini response · editorial edition

Your argument supports scrutiny of claims of self-evidence, but the conclusions about all knowledge and logic need more support.

  • Inductive support can be insufficient for certainty about unobserved cases. The claim that no other kind of warrant exists is an additional premise, not something established by induction’s limitations alone.
  • A belief stronger than its evidence warrants is epistemically under-supported. That is not by itself a formal contradiction; “logically incoherent” needs a separate demonstration.
  • Experience can influence confidence in logical practices. Whether logic’s warrant is fundamentally inductive is disputed, and an inductive defense already employs some inferential standards.

Here are some additional points to consider:

  • Cultural and Historical Context: What might seem self-evident in one culture or time period might not be so in another. Our understanding of the world evolves, and with it, so too could the “self-evident” truths we hold.
  • The Role of Intuition: Some argue for an intuitive grasp of certain truths beyond pure experience. This is a complex area, and whether intuition provides a reliable source of knowledge is still debated.

The fallibilist challenge is significant, but the thread has not established that all apparently self-evident truths originate in induction or that logic is justified only by observed success.

The concept of a “high degree of confidence” based on strong inductive evidence is a more realistic approach to knowledge in most areas. This allows us to learn and adapt as new information comes to light.

I would submit that even our intuitions deserve only as much confidence as their induction-derived track records justify. We can never claim our intuitions have been adequately tested to an degree that warrants their creation of self-evident presuppositions. Right?

Track records can be relevant to assessing intuition, but the universal claim that every intuition has only inductive warrant remains disputed. A feeling of obviousness alone does not establish infallibility.

  • The Black Box of Intuition: Intuitions arise from a complex web of experiences, biases, and subconscious processing. We often lack a clear understanding of how or why we arrive at a particular intuitive feeling.
  • The Risk of Confirmation Bias: Our intuitions can be heavily influenced by confirmation bias. We tend to focus on information that confirms our existing beliefs and downplay evidence that contradicts them. This makes it difficult to objectively assess the accuracy of our intuitions.
  • The Limits of Testing: It can be incredibly challenging to definitively test an intuition. Many things we intuitively believe are difficult to replicate in controlled settings.

Here are some additional points to consider:

  • Culturally Shaped Intuitions: Intuitions can vary significantly across cultures and individuals. What feels intuitively true to one person might seem completely wrong to another. This highlights the subjective nature of intuition.
  • The Value of Heuristics: Despite their limitations, intuitions can serve valuable heuristic functions. Quick judgments based on experience can be helpful for navigating complex situations.

Intuitions should be open to critical assessment and correction. Relevant track records can help, alongside arguments about content, context, and competing explanations; this does not settle every account of intuitive or a priori warrant.

Truth and human confidence

ChatGPT response · editorial edition

The distinction between truth and a person’s confidence is right. The further claim that every confidence trajectory must approach certainty without reaching it does not follow from that distinction alone.

  1. Objective Truth vs. Subjective Belief: The distinction between objective truth (the state of affairs in the world independent of human perception) and subjective belief (an individual’s conviction or acceptance of a proposition as true) is foundational in epistemology. This differentiation highlights the gap between what is (objective reality) and our understanding or beliefs about what is (subjective interpretations).
  2. Epistemic Limitations of the Human Mind: Human cognition is bounded by various limitations, including sensory capacities, cognitive biases, and the scope of our experiences. These limitations mean that even our most confident beliefs about the world are, in principle, fallible. Our knowledge claims, especially those derived from inductive reasoning, are always subject to revision in light of new evidence or better arguments.
  3. In a classical framework, bivalence concerns a proposition’s truth value, not an observer’s certainty about it. Alternative logics and semantic theories complicate the general claim; the epistemic point remains that being true and being known with certainty are different.
  4. An asymptotic model can illustrate some patterns of learning, but is not a general description of every belief. Distinguish an unobserved empirical generalization, a formal consequence of axioms, and human confidence in having checked the consequence correctly.
  5. Justification of Epistemic Humility: Given these considerations, your conclusion advocates for a stance of epistemic humility. Recognizing the subjective limits of our knowledge and the asymptotic nature of our approach towards certainty underscores the importance of being open to new information and willing to revise our beliefs. This stance does not undermine the pursuit of knowledge but rather frames it as a dynamic and ongoing process, one that is rigorously skeptical and constantly self-correcting.

In essence, your analysis underscores the critical distinction between what we believe to be true (based on the best available evidence and reasoning) and what is actually true in an objective sense. It highlights the importance of acknowledging our epistemic limitations and maintaining a commitment to intellectual humility and openness to new evidence. This perspective is not only philosophically robust but also foundational to the empirical sciences and rational inquiry more broadly.

  • True/False vs. Confidence: The distinction between a proposition being true or false and our level of confidence in its truth is crucial. A statement can be objectively true, but our understanding of it may be limited by our inductive experience.
  • Finite inductive support does not by itself entail an unrestricted generalization. That point does not require an exact universal curve for human confidence or establish that every kind of certainty is impossible.

Here are some additional thoughts:

  • The Value of Axioms: Despite their limitations, axioms play a vital role in building coherent systems of thought. In mathematics or logic, for example, the starting axioms allow us to derive other truths with a high degree of certainty within that specific system.
  • The Importance of Skepticism: Acknowledging the limitations of our knowledge, as you do, is a key aspect of good critical thinking. A healthy dose of skepticism helps us avoid mistaking strong beliefs for absolute truths.

Overall, your understanding of the interplay between objective truth, subjective limitations, and the role of axioms is insightful. The quest for knowledge is an ongoing process of building confidence through evidence and acknowledging the inherent limitations of our human minds.
