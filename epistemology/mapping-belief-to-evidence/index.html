Prompt 1: A good working definition of epistemic rationality might be the mapping of one’s degree of belief to the degree of the relevant evidence one perceives. Are there any weaknesses in this definition?

A good working definition of epistemic rationality might be the mapping of one’s degree of belief to the degree of the relevant evidence one perceives. Are there any weaknesses in this definition?

ChatGPT response · editorial edition

The definition identifies a useful norm: confidence should answer to perceived evidential support. Its limitations depend on whether it is intended as a minimal sincerity condition or a complete account of epistemic rationality.

  1. Interpretation: The word “mapping” does not entail a linear or objective numerical relationship. The genuine question is what counts as support and how a person checks that assessment. People can honestly interpret the same material differently.
  2. Measurement: Degrees of confidence can sometimes be represented by probabilities, but “amount of evidence” is not a single scale that mechanically determines belief. Relevance, reliability, dependence between reports, and prior information all matter.
  3. Intellectual habits: Open-mindedness and willingness to reconsider may help someone follow the proposed norm. Whether they belong in its definition or in the practices that sustain it is a further question; omission alone does not refute the norm.
  4. Dynamic Nature of Evidence: The definition does not explicitly address the dynamic nature of evidence and knowledge. As new information becomes available, the relevance and weight of existing evidence can change. A definition of epistemic rationality should ideally account for the need to update one’s beliefs in response to new evidence, emphasizing the processual aspect of rational belief formation.
  5. Complexity of Evidence: The definition simplifies the complex nature of how evidence is perceived and assessed. In many cases, evidence is not straightforward or clear-cut but involves interpreting data, weighing conflicting information, and dealing with uncertainty. The cognitive processes involved in assessing the relevance and reliability of evidence are complex and can be influenced by a multitude of factors, including expertise, the presentation of information, and cognitive biases.
  6. Scope: Coherence constrains whether a set of confidence judgments fits together. Practical or moral stakes can change which inquiries are worth pursuing or which actions to take, but a desirable consequence does not by itself make a proposition more likely to be true.

In summary, while the provided definition of epistemic rationality highlights an important aspect of rational belief formation—the alignment of beliefs with evidence—it may benefit from refinement to address its limitations, particularly regarding the interpretation and dynamism of evidence, the measurement challenges, and the role of epistemic virtues.

Yes, evidence is subjective to each individual, but rationality, at its most basic, is an honest mapping of the degree of belief to the degree of the evidence as one understands evidence. This understanding may be unknowingly flawed, but mapping one’s degree of belief to the degree of the evidence as one honestly perceives and defines it is what we might call core rationality, right?

Yes, “core rationality” can name that minimal condition for this discussion. It concerns an honest attempt to match confidence to the support one perceives, even where the perception is mistaken. This stipulation is not a claim that sincerity alone meets every philosophical standard of rational belief.

  1. Subjectivity and honesty: Assessment is made from a person’s perspective. That does not make every claim equally supported or imply that the world itself changes with the assessor. Honesty concerns how the person responds to what they take to be evidence.
  2. Updating: A continued commitment to matching confidence should motivate revision when perceived support changes. A sincere current judgment alone does not prove that the person will update appropriately later.
  3. Responsibility: The minimal stipulation distinguishes sincere error from knowingly overstating support. Whether an error is also careless depends on what checks were reasonably available, not sincerity alone.
  4. Limits: A person can satisfy this minimal condition while failing to recognize a bias. Awareness of that possibility supports humility, but humility is not logically guaranteed by an honest belief.
  5. Practical Application: This approach to rationality is practically applicable across diverse contexts, as it does not demand infallible knowledge or perfect objectivity but rather focuses on the sincere effort to align beliefs with evidence as understood by the individual. This makes the concept of core rationality accessible and actionable for people in varied domains and with different levels of expertise.

Further checks assess the quality of the perceived evidence itself. Comparing independent sources, testing predictions, and hearing informed objections can reveal mistakes. These extend the minimal condition rather than silently changing its stipulated meaning.

Gemini response · editorial edition

The proposed definition of epistemic rationality as “mapping one’s degree of belief to the degree of the relevant evidence they perceive” captures a core aspect of the concept, but it does have some weaknesses to consider:

Weaknesses:

  • Incomplete picture: Epistemic rationality involves more than just matching belief strength to evidence strength. It also includes:
    • Openness to new evidence: Being receptive to revising beliefs based on new information, even if it contradicts your current beliefs.
    • Critical thinking: Evaluating evidence critically, considering its source, reliability, and potential biases.
    • Coherence: Ensuring your beliefs are consistent with each other and avoid logical contradictions.
    • Awareness of cognitive biases: Recognizing how our thinking can be influenced by unconscious prejudices and mental shortcuts.
  • Degrees: The definition needs an account of evidential weight and confidence. Exact numerical measurement may be unavailable, but comparative judgments such as “less confident after this counterexample” can still be meaningful.
  • Prior information: The wording does not exclude prior beliefs. It should clarify which background assumptions are justified and how new evidence changes confidence, so that prior commitment does not merely shield itself from correction.
  • Doesn’t consider action: Epistemic rationality sometimes involves choosing to seek out new evidence or engage in specific learning activities, not just passively perceiving it.
  • Potential for cherry-picking: People might selectively focus on evidence that supports their existing beliefs, neglecting contradictory evidence.

Alternatives:

While the proposed definition has limitations, consider these alternative perspectives:

  • Intellectual virtues: Open-mindedness, humility, and diligence offer one way to discuss good inquiry. The original attribution of this list to “Foley’s virtue-based approach” was too loose: Foley’s work on intellectual self-trust and rational belief requires a more specific account than this list.
  • Probabilistic approach: Assigning probabilities to propositions based on available evidence and updating those probabilities as new information emerges.

Ultimately, there’s no single perfect definition of epistemic rationality. It’s a multifaceted concept requiring careful consideration of various factors and approaches.

Can we stipulate that “core” rationality is an honest attempt to map one’s degree of belief to the degree of the evidence as one honestly perceives it? We might have a flawed understanding of what constitutes legitimate evidence. Still, if we are honestly attempting to map our degree of belief to the relevant evidence as we perceive it, we are maintaining a core rationality. Agreed?

Agreed as a stipulation for this exchange. Calling it “core rationality” marks honest alignment with perceived evidence; it does not erase the distinction between that minimal condition and more demanding checks on evidence quality.

Pros:

  • Sincerity: The term separates honest confidence from knowingly claiming more support than one perceives. It does not establish that the confidence is accurate.
  • Acknowledges limitations: Accepts that perfect understanding of evidence and its interpretation is often unattainable.
  • Application: Ask whether someone’s expressed confidence matches their actual assessment. Active evidence-seeking is a further practice, not something guaranteed by the minimal definition.

Cons:

  • Subjectivity remains: Honesty and perceived evidence are individual interpretations, leaving room for bias and error.
  • Doesn’t address cognitive biases: Even honest individuals have subconscious biases influencing their perception of evidence.
  • Manipulation: A person can sincerely accept misleading material. That illustrates the difference between core rationality and reliable evidence assessment rather than contradicting the proposed stipulation.
  • Limited scope: “Core” rationality might underemphasize critical thinking and coherence aspects of full epistemic rationality.

Alternative Perspectives:

  • Critical assessment: Add a separate description of how someone checks sources and reasoning; do not redefine the minimal condition whenever a further virtue is desirable.
  • Distinguishing levels: Acknowledge different levels of “core” rationality based on effort and resources available.
  • Acknowledging manipulation: Consider how “honest attempt” applies in situations of misinformation or propaganda.

The stipulation is usable if its limits remain explicit. An honest but poorly informed judgment can be core-rational in this local sense while failing stronger epistemic standards. The next prompt proposes “deep” rationality for corrective practices.

Prompt 2: Perhaps we can go beyond the notion that the degree of belief must map to the degree of the perceived evidence if one is to maintain “core” rationality and expand the taxonomy. Can we call a practiced rationality in which one has filtered our logical fallacies, cognitive biases and distortions in the perception of evidence “deep” rationality for the purpose of our discussion?

Perhaps we can go beyond the notion that the degree of belief must map to the degree of the perceived evidence if one is to maintain “core” rationality and expand the taxonomy. Can we call a practiced rationality in which one has filtered our logical fallacies, cognitive biases and distortions in the perception of evidence “deep” rationality for the purpose of our discussion?

ChatGPT response · editorial edition

Yes. For this discussion, “deep rationality” can name sustained work on the reliability of evidence assessment in addition to core sincerity. Interpret “filtered out” as identifying and reducing errors, not certifying that all bias and fallacy have been eliminated.

  1. Bias checks: Search for a relevant counterexample, record predictions before seeing outcomes, and compare independent reports. Knowing the name of a bias is not evidence that one has corrected it; evaluate the practice by its results.
  2. Critical Evaluation of Logical Structures: Practitioners of deep rationality critically evaluate the logical structures underpinning arguments and beliefs. This involves identifying and avoiding logical fallacies, understanding the principles of sound reasoning, and applying these principles to assess the validity and coherence of arguments.
  3. Evidence assessment: Ask whether a source supports this particular claim and whether apparently separate reports share an origin. Ten copies of one unsupported story do not amount to ten independent confirmations.
  4. Emotional and Psychological Insight: A commitment to deep rationality involves recognizing the role of emotions and psychological factors in shaping beliefs and decision-making processes. Practitioners strive to understand and manage the influence of emotions on their reasoning, aiming for a balanced approach that acknowledges emotional insights without allowing emotions to unduly bias judgment.
  5. Continuous Learning and Self-Improvement: Deep rationality is characterized by an ongoing commitment to learning and self-improvement. This includes actively seeking out new information, being open to revising one’s beliefs in light of new evidence or arguments, and engaging in lifelong learning to enhance one’s rational capacities.
  6. Collaborative and Dialogic Engagement: Engaging with others in a constructive and open-minded manner is a key aspect of deep rationality. It involves participating in dialogues and debates, listening to alternative viewpoints, and considering diverse perspectives, all while maintaining a commitment to rational discourse.
  7. Epistemic Humility: Deep rationality is marked by epistemic humility—an acknowledgment of the limits of one’s knowledge and understanding. Practitioners of deep rationality recognize that no one is immune to error and that intellectual humility is crucial for growth and learning.

The distinction is a proposed vocabulary, not an established two-level scientific classification. A person may be strong at correction in one domain and weak in another; deeper inquiry remains fallible and can improve without reaching certainty.

Gemini response · editorial edition

Expanding the taxonomy of rationality definitely holds value in our discussion. Here’s my take on your proposition:

Introducing “Deep” Rationality:

Defining “deep” rationality as a form of practiced rationality where individuals actively refine their thinking processes by confronting logical fallacies, cognitive biases, and distortions in evidence perception resonates quite well. It acknowledges the inherent limitations of human cognition and emphasizes the ongoing effort required to cultivate rational thinking.

Strengths of “Deep” Rationality:

  • Dynamic and progressive: Captures the continuous learning and improvement aspect of attaining rationality.
  • Addresses limitations: Recognizes the influence of biases and distortions, promoting active mitigation strategies.
  • Emphasis on process: Focuses on the deliberate practice of critical thinking skills and self-reflection.
  • Complements “core” rationality: Provides a nuanced approach by building upon the foundation of sincere belief-evidence mapping.

Considerations and refinements:

  • Assessing progress: Specify a task and examine correction, prediction accuracy, or handling of counterevidence over time. Eloquence and self-ratings do not establish a general rank of “deepness.”
  • Accessibility and resources: Access to education, resources, and tools to practice critical thinking might vary, potentially introducing an inequality aspect.
  • Differentiating from expertise: While cultivating “deep” rationality can benefit overall thinking, it might not equate to expertise in specific domains.

Overall:

For example, someone sincerely trusts a repeated news report: that can meet the core condition. Tracing all repetitions to one unverified source and lowering confidence illustrates the added practice. The change improves the basis for belief without guaranteeing the conclusion is true.

Use the distinction to identify the next useful check, not to award yourself a permanent status as a superior reasoner.

Discussion Questions on Core and Deep Rationality:

Core Rationality:

  1. In a world of limited information and subjective interpretation, is “honest attempt” enough to ensure core rationality?
  2. Can cultural or societal biases influence what individuals perceive as “honest” in terms of belief-evidence mapping?
  3. Should core rationality consider the potential impact of our beliefs on ourselves and others?
  4. At what point does relying solely on personal perception become detrimental to core rationality?
  5. Does core rationality require any level of critical thinking, or is it purely about sincerity?

Deep Rationality:

  1. How can we effectively identify and mitigate the influence of logical fallacies and cognitive biases?
  2. Can technology play a role in assisting individuals in achieving deep rationality?
  3. Does continuous self-reflection and reevaluation of beliefs necessarily guarantee deep rationality?
  4. Is deep rationality attainable for everyone, or are there inherent psychological or cognitive limitations?
  5. How can we balance the effort required for deep rationality with the demands of daily life?

Comparison and Contrast:

  1. Does deep rationality necessarily build upon core rationality, or are they distinct concepts?
  2. Where should the line be drawn between core and deep rationality? Is it a continuum or a clear distinction?
  3. Which situations warrant additional checking beyond sincere judgment, given the stakes, uncertainty, and available time?
  4. How can excessive time spent checking become impractical without implying that a less accurate belief is thereby better supported?
  5. How can we promote both core and deep rationality in education, society, and individual practice?
