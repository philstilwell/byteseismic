Prompt 1: What is Doxastic Voluntarism?

Choosing belief and directing inquiry

ChatGPT response · editorial edition

Doxastic voluntarism concerns whether belief is subject to voluntary control. “Doxastic” derives from the Greek doxa, belief or opinion. The question matters for responsibility, but does not by itself settle when someone deserves blame: a false belief may be reasonable given the evidence available. Two kinds of control should be distinguished.

  1. Direct doxastic voluntarism holds that we can, at least in some circumstances, form a belief through an immediate act of will. Choosing to believe that it will rain is different from deciding to carry an umbrella or saying that it will rain. Strong versions allow broad choice; more limited versions concern cases of uncertain evidence. Failure to believe an obvious falsehood on command does not refute every limited version.
  2. Indirect doxastic voluntarism concerns influence through other choices: consulting a forecast, checking its reliability, or listening to a serious objection. These can change the evidence we encounter and how we assess it, without letting us prescribe the resulting belief. Access to information, time and freedom from coercion limit that influence. Deliberately avoiding contrary evidence can also shape belief, but influence is not the same as responsible inquiry.

The discussion around Doxastic Voluntarism is significant in ethics, epistemology, and philosophy of mind, as it touches on the nature of belief, the limits of human freedom, and the responsibilities we have regarding the pursuit of truth and avoidance of error.

Gemini response · editorial edition

Doxastic voluntarism is a theory in philosophy that deals with how much control we have over our own beliefs. It basically asks whether we can choose what to believe or not.

There are two main flavors of doxastic voluntarism:

  • Direct doxastic voluntarism: Can deciding make a proposition seem true to you? Try believing that a visibly empty glass contains water merely for a reward. Pretending, hoping and reporting belief do not meet that challenge. This is an objection to unrestricted direct control; defenders of more limited control need not claim that every belief can be switched at will.
  • Indirect doxastic voluntarism: You can inspect the glass in better light or ask someone else to check. Those actions may change your belief through new evidence. You choose an investigative step, not necessarily its result. Choosing only reassuring testimony would also influence the process, but could make it less reliable.

The ethical question is where responsibility belongs: in the belief itself, in avoidable neglect of evidence, or in a capacity to respond to reasons. These are different proposals. Lack of direct choice does not make a belief unjustified: seeing a glass clearly may justify believing it is empty even if that belief forms without a decision. Nor does discovering an error automatically establish culpability.

Prompt 2: What are the arguments for and against doxastic voluntarism?

Arguments about control and responsibility

ChatGPT response · editorial edition

The debate over Doxastic Voluntarism involves several arguments both for and against the notion that beliefs are subject to the will. Here are some of the key arguments on both sides:

Arguments For Doxastic Voluntarism

  1. Moral Responsibility: If praise or blame requires control, and some beliefs deserve it, there must be relevant control somewhere. This is a conditional argument, not proof of direct choice: one might instead locate responsibility in inquiry, defend another account of responsibility, or reject the proposed blame. Whether a person could reasonably have checked matters more than simply noting that the belief was false.
  2. Intuitive Examples of Belief Control: Self-deception and wishful thinking appear to show desire shaping belief. A person may repeatedly consult flattering reports and avoid criticism. That could involve chosen avoidance, unchosen distortion, or both. A belief’s serving a desire does not establish that the person consciously chose to believe it.
  3. Practical Rationality: A useful belief may encourage persistence or cooperation. This gives someone a reason to want it, but wanting a belief is different from being able to adopt it, and usefulness is different from evidence of truth. Deliberately attending to genuine reasons for confidence can influence belief; acting on a working assumption need not involve believing it outright.

Arguments Against Doxastic Voluntarism

  1. Evidentialism: Evidentialism concerns justification: roughly, whether a belief fits the person’s evidence. It is not the psychological thesis that all belief is involuntary or evidence-determined. The relevant objection to direct choice is that, when sincerely deciding whether a proposition is true, we normally look for reasons to regard it as true rather than reasons we would benefit from believing it. Keep that objection separate from the standard of justification.
  2. Psychological Compulsion: Some beliefs resist a contrary decision. Looking into the empty glass, you may find that a promised reward cannot make water seem present. This is evidence against unrestricted command over belief, not a demonstration that no belief can ever be voluntarily formed in more ambiguous circumstances.
  3. Conceptual Arguments: A familiar argument says that belief represents something as true, whereas merely deciding to treat it as true may amount only to acceptance or pretense. The disputed step is whether this truth-directed character rules out voluntary formation in every case. No general principle of sufficient reason establishes that all actual beliefs must be rational or compelled by adequate evidence.
  4. Limited Scope of Voluntariness: Choosing the conditions of inquiry does not guarantee control over its conclusion. Someone can choose to read a convincing refutation and then find belief changing unbidden. That limits the strength of indirect control, but does not refute the weaker claim that chosen inquiry can influence belief. Calling this an objection to all indirect influence would miss its target.

The dispute is not resolved by averaging the two sides. Specify the proposed control, its circumstances and what would count as exercising it. Then ask separately whether the person had fair opportunities to inquire, whether the resulting belief was justified, and what kind of responsibility those facts support.

Gemini response · editorial edition

Arguments for Doxastic Voluntarism

  • Intuitions of Control: Deliberation can feel like an exercise of agency: we compare reasons and settle a question. This supports taking reasoning seriously as something a person does. It does not establish that one can select either conclusion at will while finding the same evidence compelling.
  • Selective Attention: We can choose what information we pay attention to. Focusing on certain aspects of a situation and neglecting others can influence how we interpret it and ultimately what we believe. This ability to control our focus seems to indirectly influence belief formation.
  • Belief Revision: Changing one’s mind for a reason may be an exercise of rational agency without being a choice of belief on demand. In McHugh’s account of attitudinal control, responsiveness to reasons provides a distinct form of control. That is a philosophical proposal about agency, not evidence that people can freely choose any conclusion.

Arguments Against Doxastic Voluntarism

  • The Role of Evidence: Evidence can make an unwelcome conclusion hard to escape: checking an account may convince you that you cannot afford a trip. This challenges the idea that desire alone controls belief. It does not imply that everyone responds correctly to evidence or that the same evidence always produces the same judgment.
  • Unconscious Influences: Upbringing, habits and emotional responses can shape belief without a present decision. Their existence challenges complete conscious control. It leaves open whether someone can later recognize an influence, reassess the evidence or exercise more limited control.
  • The Paradox of Belief: Why do we retain distressing beliefs if we could choose comforting ones? This challenges unrestricted comfort-seeking control, but is not a decisive paradox. A person might value accuracy more than comfort, and limited voluntarism does not promise control in every case.

The central distinction is between being responsive to reasons, choosing how to investigate, and selecting a conclusion independently of what seems true. Evidence for one does not automatically establish the others. Responsibility must be argued for at the relevant level rather than inferred from a general feeling of control.

Prompt 3: What positions do cognitive scientists take on doxastic voluntarism?

What cognitive research can establish

ChatGPT response · editorial edition

Cognitive science studies processes that bear on belief control; it does not speak with a single position on the philosophical thesis. Experiments can test effects of distraction, goals, information and instructions on judgments. Interpreting those results as direct choice, indirect influence or a limit on control requires an additional argument. The following distinctions keep that argument visible.

  1. Belief Formation as a Cognitive Process: In Gilbert, Tafarodi and Malone’s 1993 experiments, false information affected judgments more under cognitive load or time pressure. The authors interpreted this as evidence for initial acceptance followed by effortful rejection. The result concerns those tasks and that interpretation; it does not show that every belief is automatically accepted or that direct control is conceptually impossible.
  2. The Role of Cognitive Biases: Biased evaluation can make a conclusion feel evidence-based even when the inquiry is selective. This complicates simple appeals to “what I chose to believe.” To establish voluntary influence, distinguish an unchosen bias from a deliberate decision about which evidence to examine; finding the first does not establish the second.
  3. Neuroscience of Belief: A neural correlate—a pattern of brain activity associated with a judgment—does not by itself show that the judgment is involuntary. Deliberate actions also involve brain activity. A study relevant to voluntarism would need to distinguish genuine belief change from changes in reporting or compliance and test the proposed kind of control. Merely locating a process in the brain cannot settle the issue.
  4. Influence of Social and Environmental Factors: Other people supply evidence and exert pressure, but those pathways differ. A person may report agreement to avoid conflict without privately believing the claim. To interpret a social influence as belief change, a study must distinguish public compliance from private judgment and consider whether the information itself was informative.
  5. Possibilities for Indirect Influence: Lord, Lepper and Preston’s 1984 experiments tested instructions to consider the opposite in biased assimilation and hypothesis testing. Their findings support the possibility of improving some judgments through a procedure. They do not establish that any desired belief can be produced, that all training generalizes, or that merely encountering disagreement guarantees correction.

These studies help constrain claims about effortless belief choice and identify possible routes for influencing inquiry. They do not supply a scientific vote for one comprehensive theory of responsibility. A fair assessment asks what the task measured, whether a change persisted, and whether it reflected changed conviction rather than an answer given to satisfy the experimenter.

Gemini response · editorial edition

Cognitive science doesn’t offer a definitive answer on doxastic voluntarism, but it does provide some insights that influence the debate. Here’s a breakdown of how cognitive science approaches the issue:

Findings relevant to indirect influence:

  • Confirmation Bias: Selective searching can shape the evidence encountered. Deliberately adding a serious contrary source is one possible intervention. But biased searching may also happen without awareness, so the existence of confirmation bias is not itself proof of voluntary control.
  • Motivated Reasoning: Wanting a conclusion can influence which reasons seem persuasive. That influence may occur without a decision to believe the conclusion. Motivation, conscious strategy and actual belief change must therefore be distinguished.
  • Attention and Memory: Choosing to reread an argument, check a record or compare alternatives can alter later judgment. One cannot infer from these choices complete control over what is noticed, remembered or ultimately believed. They are candidate routes of indirect influence, with results to be checked.

Findings that limit claims of unrestricted direct control:

  • Heuristics and Biases: Shortcuts can affect judgment without being chosen. Their presence makes complete conscious control implausible, but neither every shortcut nor every resulting judgment is wrong. A specific task must establish which influence occurred.
  • Emotional Influences: An emotional reaction may change which risks feel salient without being summoned at will. It may also direct attention to a real concern. Showing an emotional influence therefore does not by itself prove either irrationality or the impossibility of all voluntary belief formation.

Overall View:

The available distinction is between influencing an inquiry and commanding its conclusion. Cognitive research can test particular routes of influence; it does not license the stronger claim that a person can make any proposition believable by deciding to do so.

A useful future experiment would compare belief reports before and after a chosen strategy, check incentives to merely report compliance, and test persistence beyond the immediate task. Its result would bear on a specified form of control, not settle the whole debate at once.

Doxastic Voluntarism Quiz – Answers

  1. Direct control over at least some beliefs in specified circumstances. Unrestricted versions claim more; direct voluntarism need not imply complete control over every belief.
  2. c) Reading and comparing a serious counterargument. This chooses an inquiry step rather than its conclusion.
  3. The Role of Evidence (Evidence can overwhelm our desires and lead us to believe things we’d rather not.)

Gemini response · editorial edition

Doxastic Voluntarism Discussion Questions:

  1. In your everyday life, can you think of examples where you felt you had a choice in what to believe? Conversely, can you think of situations where evidence forced you to accept something you didn’t want to believe?
  2. Do you think some beliefs are more susceptible to voluntary control than others? Why or why not?
  3. If we cannot directly choose a belief, when might neglect of inquiry still warrant criticism? How does justified belief differ from blameless or blameworthy belief?
  4. When an information feed limits exposure to contrary evidence, how much influence can a user exercise by choosing other sources? What limits should affect judgments of responsibility?
  5. Confirmation bias seems to contradict the idea of rational belief formation. How can we overcome these biases and seek out diverse viewpoints to challenge our existing beliefs?
  6. Can someone’s emotional state influence their ability to weigh evidence objectively? How might emotions like fear or anger impact doxastic voluntarism?
  7. How could we check whether a desired conclusion is shaping our treatment of evidence, without assuming that any procedure guarantees impartiality?
  8. Does the concept of indoctrination, where beliefs are instilled through force or manipulation, challenge the idea of doxastic voluntarism altogether?
  9. If our upbringing and cultural background significantly influence our beliefs, can we ever truly escape their influence and form entirely independent beliefs?
  10. With the rise of “fake news” and misinformation campaigns, how can we develop critical thinking skills to help us discern truth from falsehood, even when it goes against our initial beliefs?
  11. What experiment would help distinguish genuine voluntary belief change from deliberate reporting, pretense or compliance? What could brain measurements add?
  12. The concept of doxastic voluntarism seems to have implications for education. How can educators create learning environments that foster critical thinking and encourage students to question their own beliefs while being open to new evidence?
