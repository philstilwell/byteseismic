Prompt 1: What is a syllogism?

What is a syllogism?

ChatGPT response · editorial edition

A syllogism is an argument with two premises and a conclusion. In a standard categorical syllogism, three terms connect classes: the middle term appears in both premises and not in the conclusion. The major and minor terms are respectively the predicate and subject of the conclusion. Merely having this structure does not ensure validity.

Structure of a Syllogism

A syllogism consists of three parts:

  1. Major premise: Contains the predicate term of the conclusion. It need not be universal simply because it is called major.
  2. Minor premise: Contains the subject term of the conclusion. It need not describe a single individual.
  3. Conclusion: The claim the premises are offered to support; whether it follows is what the validity test determines.

Standard Form

One valid categorical form is:

  • Major Premise: All M are P.
  • Minor Premise: All S are M.
  • Conclusion: Therefore, all S are P.

Where:

  • M is the middle term.
  • P is the predicate of the conclusion.
  • S is the subject of the conclusion.

Examples of Syllogisms

Example 1
  • Major Premise: All mammals are warm-blooded.
  • Minor Premise: All whales are mammals.
  • Conclusion: Therefore, all whales are warm-blooded.
Example 2
  • Major Premise: All birds have feathers.
  • Minor Premise: All robins are birds.
  • Conclusion: Therefore, all robins have feathers.
Example 3
  • Major premise: No fish are mammals.
  • Minor Premise: All salmon are fish.
  • Conclusion: Therefore, no salmon are mammals.

Each of these examples demonstrates how the structure of a syllogism helps in deriving a logical conclusion based on the relationship shared between the major and minor premises. This form of reasoning is foundational in classical logic and is extensively used in various fields including education, mathematics, and philosophy to form arguments or to test the validity of argument structures.

Gemini response · editorial edition

The examples below use two premises to support a conclusion. Some apply a general claim to an individual; that is a common use, not the definition of every categorical syllogism.

  • Major premise: In categorical form, the premise containing the conclusion’s predicate term.
  • Minor premise: In categorical form, the premise containing the conclusion’s subject term.
  • Conclusion: This is a statement based on the two previous ones.

Here’s a classic example:

  • Major premise: All men are mortal. (This is a general statement about all men)
  • Minor premise: Socrates is a man. (This applies the category “men” to Socrates)
  • Conclusion: Therefore, Socrates is mortal. (This follows from the above statements)

Another example:

  • Major premise: All cats are felines.
  • Minor premise: Whiskers is a cat.
  • Conclusion: Therefore, Whiskers is a feline.

A valid inference can contain a false premise. It then lacks soundness, even if the conclusion happens to be true. For example:

  • Major premise: All birds can fly. This universal statement is false; exceptions defeat “all.”
  • Minor premise: Tweety is a bird. (This is true)
  • Conclusion: Therefore, Tweety can fly. The inference is valid, but the argument is unsound because its major premise is false. The premises do not establish whether this particular bird can actually fly.

Validity concerns what follows under the premises. Soundness requires that those premises also be true. A false premise does not force a false conclusion.

Prompt 2: What real-world value do syllogisms have?

What real-world value do syllogisms have?

ChatGPT response · editorial edition

Syllogisms hold substantial real-world value, particularly in enhancing logical thinking, improving argumentation skills, and facilitating decision-making across various domains. Here are three clear examples demonstrating the practical utility of syllogisms:

Example 1: Legal Reasoning

Situation: A simplified hypothetical legal rule can illustrate applying a rule to a case. The example is not a statement of any jurisdiction’s actual penalty law.

  • Assumed rule: Every person convicted under the specified rule must pay the stated penalty.
  • Case premise: The defendant was convicted under that rule.
  • Conclusion: Therefore, the defendant must pay a penalty.

Value: The conclusion follows if the rule and case premise apply. The syllogism exposes what must be checked; it does not establish the actual law, facts, or fairness of the outcome.

Example 2: Medical Diagnosis

Situation: A symptom pattern can prompt investigation, but reasoning from symptoms to a disease is usually uncertain inference rather than a conclusive categorical syllogism.

  • Proposed premise: A specified symptom pattern is compatible with tuberculosis. Compatibility is weaker than diagnosis or even a demonstrated high probability.
  • Case premise: The hypothetical patient has that pattern.
  • Limited conclusion: Tuberculosis is one possibility under the proposed premise. This does not establish that the patient has it.

Value: Formalization reveals the limited conclusion and missing diagnostic evidence. It does not ensure correct treatment; CDC diagnostic guidance describes evaluation beyond a symptom list.

Example 3: Business Strategy

Situation: Business leaders often utilize syllogistic reasoning to make strategic decisions that align with their overall business objectives and market conditions.

  • Major premise: Every product whose relevant revenue exceeds its total relevant costs is profitable over the specified period.
  • Minor premise: Product X meets that revenue-and-cost condition for the period.
  • Conclusion: Therefore, Product X is profitable.

Value: The conclusion follows from the stated accounting condition. The original combination of high satisfaction and low production costs was insufficient: it omitted revenue, volume, and other costs.

These examples make assumptions inspectable. A valid form cannot turn an uncertain medical inference, an unverified legal rule, or incomplete business data into an established fact.

Gemini response · editorial edition

Syllogisms, though seemingly simple, have real-world value in various situations. Here are 3 examples:

  1. Making Decisions:
  • Proposed premise: Peanut allergy can cause an adverse reaction after exposure. “Can” does not mean that everyone has the same reaction or that every reaction has this cause.
  • Minor premise: Sarah breaks out in hives and has difficulty breathing after eating a peanut butter sandwich.
  • Tentative interpretation: Allergy is a possibility, but no deductively certain diagnosis follows from the stated conditional and symptoms.

The example illustrates inference to a possible cause. A causal condition cannot simply be reversed, and further clinical assessment matters; see the NIAID-sponsored diagnostic guidelines.

  1. Identifying Faulty Arguments:
  • Encountered Argument: “This medicine cured my cold, so it will definitely cure yours too.”
  • Analysis: Improvement after taking something does not establish that it caused recovery; even an established effect in one case would not guarantee it in every other case.
  • What a reconstruction exposes: To infer a guaranteed result, the speaker would need a universal effectiveness premise and evidence that this case satisfies its conditions. Neither is supplied. Do not invent a medicine’s antiviral effect to repair the argument.

By dissecting arguments as syllogisms, we can expose weaknesses and ensure sound reasoning.

  1. Scientific Inquiry:
  • Proposed premise: Every planet with a strong magnetic field has a liquid metal core. This is an oversimplified scientific premise, not an established general rule.
  • Minor premise: Jupiter has a very strong magnetic field.
  • Conclusion under that premise: Jupiter has a liquid metal core. The form would be valid, but the premise is not warranted as stated. NASA describes a dynamo associated with liquid metallic hydrogen; a strong field alone does not establish a conventional molten-metal core.

Formalizing a scientific argument helps identify the model or empirical generalization that needs testing. Logical form alone does not validate that model.

These are just a few examples. Syllogisms provide a structured approach to reasoning, helping us make informed decisions, identify fallacies, and build a foundation for scientific inquiry in the real world.

Prompt 3: Demonstrate how an informally articulated argument can be reformulated as a syllogism so the logic can be tested.

Demonstrate how an informally articulated argument can be reformulated as a syllogism so the logic can be tested.

ChatGPT response · editorial edition

Turning informal arguments into syllogistic form is a valuable skill, particularly for testing the validity of the reasoning behind assertions commonly made in everyday life. Here are three examples that demonstrate this process:

Example 1: Environmental Policy

Informal Argument:
“If we do not reduce carbon emissions, global warming will continue to increase. We have to cut down on our carbon outputs to prevent this.”

Syllogistic Reformulation:

  • Conditional premise: If the specified emissions trajectory continues, warming will continue to increase. This retains the intended conditional instead of claiming that every individual action failing to reduce emissions causes warming.
  • Case premise: Continuing the specified practices maintains that trajectory.
  • Conclusion: Under these premises, continuing those practices means warming will continue to increase.

Logic test: This is modus ponens after the trajectory connection is made explicit. Its empirical premises need separate support. The policy claim “we must cut emissions” also needs an explicit goal or normative premise; it is not supplied by the prediction alone.

Example 2: Education Funding

Informal Argument:
“Schools with adequate funding have better educational outcomes. Our local school lacks sufficient funds and is performing poorly.”

Syllogistic Reformulation:

  • Major Premise: All schools with adequate funding have better educational outcomes.
  • Minor Premise: Our local school does not have adequate funding.
  • Conclusion: Therefore, our local school does not have better educational outcomes.

Logic test: Invalid: from adequate funding → better outcomes and not adequate funding, not better outcomes does not follow. That denies the antecedent. A counterexample could be an underfunded school with better outcomes for another reason. The original report of poor performance is a separate observation, not a deduction from lack of funding.

Example 3: Healthcare Access

Informal Argument:
“People without access to healthcare do not receive timely medical treatments. John does not have health insurance, so he’s not getting the medical help he needs.”

Syllogistic Reformulation:

  • Major Premise: All people without access to healthcare do not receive timely medical treatments.
  • Required case premise: John lacks access to the relevant care. The informal argument supplies only lack of insurance; the reconstruction therefore needs an additional connection rather than silently substituting access for insurance.
  • Conclusion: Therefore, John does not receive timely medical treatments.

Logic test: The displayed no-access inference is valid under its premises. Its universal premise and John’s actual access require evidence. The informal argument’s insurance-to-access step remains unestablished.

In each case, reformulating informal arguments into syllogisms not only tests the logic of the arguments but also clarifies the underlying assumptions that must be true for the conclusions to hold. This process is essential in disciplines such as law, policy-making, and academic debate, where logical consistency is crucial.

Gemini response · editorial edition

  1. Argument: “It’s always cold in Seattle, so I won’t need to pack any shorts for my visit.”
  • Reformulated Syllogism:
    • Implicit premise: If my visit will be cold throughout and my activities do not require shorts, I will not need to pack shorts. The original response accidentally wrote “require wearing shorts,” reversing the intended support.
    • Case premise: The visit will meet those conditions. “Seattle is always cold” is an unsupported blanket assertion, not a verified forecast for this trip.
    • Conclusion: Therefore, under those premises, I will not need shorts. The inference works; the weather and activity premises still need checking.
  1. Argument: “My friend went to that restaurant and got food poisoning, so it must be a bad place to eat.”
  • Reformulated Syllogism:
    • Major premise: Every restaurant causally responsible for one food-poisoning case is a bad place to eat. This sweeping evaluative premise is open to challenge.
    • Minor premise: The restaurant caused the friend’s illness. Becoming ill after a visit does not, by itself, establish this causal premise.
    • Conclusion: The restaurant is a bad place to eat. It follows if the stated premises are true, but neither causal attribution nor the sweeping evaluation is established merely by the anecdote.
  1. Argument: “Studying for the test is boring, so I’m not going to do it.”
  • Reformulated Syllogism:
    • Implicit decision rule: I will not undertake any activity I find boring. This makes the practical conclusion explicit without replacing “I will not do it” with “it is a waste of time.”
    • Case premise: I find studying for this test boring.
    • Conclusion: I will not study for the test. The inference follows from that decision rule; the rule’s wisdom is a different question.

By turning these informal arguments into syllogisms, we can expose the underlying assumptions and assess whether the logic is sound. This helps us make better decisions based on strong reasoning rather than emotional responses or faulty assumptions.

Prompt 4: Explain the difference between validity and soundness.

  2. An argument that is valid but not sound.
  3. An argument that is invalid, but sound.
  4. An argument that is both invalid and unsound.

ChatGPT response · editorial edition

Difference Between Validity and Soundness

Validity and soundness are two fundamental concepts used to evaluate the strength of syllogisms and arguments in logic:

  • Validity: An argument is valid if the conclusion logically follows from the premises. This means that it is impossible for the premises to be true and the conclusion false. Validity is concerned solely with the form of the argument and not the actual truthfulness of the premises.
  • Soundness: An argument is sound if it is both valid and all its premises are actually true. Soundness is a stronger condition than validity because it requires the argument to be valid and all the information it contains to be factually correct.

Examples Demonstrating Validity and Soundness

Example 1: Valid and Sound Argument
  • Major Premise: All mammals are warm-blooded animals.
  • Minor Premise: All dogs are mammals.
  • Conclusion: Therefore, all dogs are warm-blooded animals.

Analysis:

  • Validity: The argument is valid because if the premises are true, the conclusion must be true.
  • Soundness: The argument is sound because the premises are factually correct, and the argument form is valid.
Example 2: Valid but Not Sound Argument
  • Major premise: All fruits are animals.
  • Minor Premise: All apples are fruits.
  • Conclusion: Therefore, all apples are animals.

Analysis:

  • Validity: The argument is valid because the form of the argument is such that if the premises were true, the conclusion would necessarily follow.
  • Soundness: Unsound because “all fruits are animals” is false. The altered example avoids ambiguity between culinary and botanical uses of “vegetable.”
Example 3: Invalid but Sound Argument

No such example exists under the standard definition. Soundness includes validity, so “invalid but sound” is contradictory, not merely difficult to illustrate.

Example 4: Invalid and Unsound Argument
  • Major Premise: All birds are fish.
  • Minor Premise: All sparrows are birds.
  • Conclusion: Therefore, all sparrows are mammals.

Analysis:

  • Validity: The conclusion introduces mammals without a linking premise. A model with a sparrow that is both bird and fish but not mammal satisfies the premises and falsifies the conclusion. That countermodel establishes invalidity.
  • Soundness: The argument is unsound not only because it is invalid but also because the premises are factually incorrect.

Validity rules out true premises with a false conclusion. Soundness adds actual truth of the premises. Neither consistency alone nor a true conclusion establishes validity.

Gemini response · editorial edition

Validity and soundness are two concepts used to evaluate arguments. Here’s the key difference:

  • Validity: This refers to the structure or form of the argument. A valid argument is one where the conclusion necessarily follows from the premises, assuming the premises are true. It’s like a mathematical equation – if the formula is correct, and you plug in true values, you’ll get a true answer.
  • Soundness: This goes beyond just structure and considers the truth of the premises. A sound argument is not only valid (formally correct) but also has all true premises. So, a sound argument guarantees a true conclusion based on true starting points.

Here are some examples to illustrate the difference:

  • Valid and Sound:

Major premise: All cats are mammals. Minor premise: Whiskers is a cat. Conclusion: Therefore, Whiskers is a mammal.

This form is valid. It is sound if Whiskers really is a cat, together with the true classification premise. A fictional name in an example does not independently establish a real-world case premise.

  • Valid but not Sound:

Major premise: All birds can fly. Minor premise: Tweety is a bird. Conclusion: Therefore, Tweety can fly.

This argument is valid because the structure is correct: If all birds can fly and Tweety is a bird, then Tweety must also be able to fly. However, the argument is not sound because the major premise (“all birds can fly”) is false (ostriches, penguins, etc.).

  • Invalid but Sound: Impossible under the definition

An argument cannot be both invalid and sound. If an argument is invalid, the conclusion doesn’t necessarily follow from the premises, regardless of whether the premises are true or false. In such a case, even if the premises happen to be true by chance, the conclusion cannot be guaranteed to be true.

  • Invalid and Unsound:

Example: Everyone who eats chocolate has a cavity; I have a cavity; therefore I ate chocolate. This deliberately reverses the implication to supply an invalid form.

The revised example affirms the consequent: a cavity could occur without eating chocolate, even under the stated premise. The universal premise is also unwarranted. The original “ate chocolate, therefore will get a cavity” form would be valid if the times matched; a false causal premise would make it unsound, not invalid.
