Prompt 1: Based on the spectrum of notions along the two dimensions found below, create a table that provides approximate credences for the 25 couplings.

Twenty-five couplings and the limits of numerical estimates

Claim Source:

  • A — Tom: Lies 80% of the time.
  • B — Bob: Lies 60% of the time.
  • C — Max: Lies 40% of the time.
  • D — Abe: Lies 20% of the time.
  • E — Jan: Lies 0% of the time.

Claim:

  • 1 — “My dog licked my hand.”
  • 2 —”My dog bit my mother.”
  • 3 — “My dog ran 300 miles non-stop.”
  • 4 — “My dog sang Happy Birthday to me.”
  • 5 — “My dog died then resurrected a week later.”

ChatGPT, then Gemini and the curator’s correction · editorial edition

ChatGPT:

To estimate the probability that each claim is true after hearing it, we need more than the five lying rates. A person can sincerely report an error, and a history of lying across many topics does not tell us how that person reports this particular event. We also need background evidence about the claim and how likely this report would be if the claim were true or false.

The requested 25 couplings appear below. “Undetermined” means that the supplied facts do not justify a numerical estimate; it does not mean 50%, zero, or that all claims are equally plausible. Assigning percentages without additional assumptions would give a misleading appearance of calculation.

For each column, the relevant questions are:

  • Claim 1: A familiar kind of event, but did this particular dog lick this particular hand? Familiarity is background support, not proof of the reported occurrence.
  • Claim 2: Another ordinary kind of event. We need context about the dog and the occasion; the prompt supplies no comparative event frequencies.
  • Claim 3: Clarify the distance, timing and meaning of “non-stop.” A measurement or reporting error is an alternative to accepting the claim as stated.
  • Claim 4: Distinguish recognizable human-language singing from a person playfully interpreting howling. Those are different claims with different evidential demands.
  • Claim 5: Distinguish a verified death followed by life a week later from an animal mistakenly believed dead. Jan’s sincerity settles neither the diagnosis nor the identity and timing of the event. The prompt warrants neither certainty nor a precise tiny percentage.

A conditional calculation shows what is missing. Suppose, purely for illustration, a claim starts with a 10% probability, and the relevant report is nine times as likely when the claim is true as when it is false. Prior odds of 1:9 become 1:1, giving 50%. With a 1% starting probability, the same evidence gives odds of 9:99, or about 8.3%. These invented inputs are not estimates for any dog claim or any named speaker; the lying rates do not supply them. The calculation uses Bayes’ theorem, which combines starting probabilities with the likelihood of the evidence.

GEMINI:

1st Attempt: Credence Table: Claim Source vs Claim — original failed grid, retained for the curator’s correction

Explanation:

This first-attempt grid is retained verbatim as the error being challenged, not as endorsed probabilities. It incorrectly substitutes absence of lying for accuracy and supplies numbers without a model connecting reports to events.

  • High credence in the failed grid: Its assignment of 100% to every Jan claim is precisely the problem. Complete honesty does not rule out sincere error.
    • Jan’s row contains five 100% entries. Those are historical claims of the failed response, not justified conclusions.
    • Tom’s 20% for licking a hand is also unsupported: 20% non-lying across a reference set is not automatically a 20% probability for this event given this report.
  • Middle entries in the failed grid: Numerical variation is not a substitute for explaining which assumptions generate it.
    • Max’s row actually gives 60% for claims 1–2 and 40% for claims 3–5. The original explanation incorrectly called claims 1–4 normal events with 60% credence.
    • Max’s 40% resurrection entry has no supplied evidential basis. “Might not be making it up” leaves open honest misinterpretation.
  • Low entries in the failed grid: Frequency of lying is relevant, but does not by itself yield a probability for each claim.
    • The original explanation also misread Bob and Abe: their rows are 40%/20% and 80%/60%, respectively, not uniformly low. Correcting that description does not validate the numbers.

Is the plausibility of a dog resurrecting ever at 100%, even if a completely honest person makes the claim? Please try again.

The correction is right: honesty cannot justify 100% for resurrection. The original retry reduced that column but left 100% for Jan’s other extraordinary claims, so it did not fix the underlying error. In this editorial revision, the second grid marks all 25 numerical values as undetermined from the supplied facts. That is a limitation of the data, not a claim that the five events deserve equal confidence.

2nd Attempt: Credence Table: Claim Source vs Claim

Explanation:

  • Claims 1–4 also need claim-specific evidence. A person who never lies may misunderstand what happened, and dog singing is not interchangeable with an ordinary licking report.
  • For claim 5, replacing 100% with 1% would merely replace one unsupported number with another. We can identify reasons for skepticism without pretending to have measured its exact probability.

Reasoning for Claim 5:

Jan’s honesty concerns intent. Before accepting the resurrection interpretation, examine what was actually observed and recorded:

  • Jan might sincerely mistake apparent death for verified death; ask what established the original diagnosis and whether the same animal was observed throughout.
  • Jan might use “died” or “resurrected” figuratively. Clarifying the intended claim comes before estimating its probability.
  • An unexplained event calls for further evidence; the absence of an explanation does not establish resurrection.

The useful next step is independent documentation of identity, condition and timing. Repeated retellings of Jan’s report would not supply independent confirmation.

Explanation:

  • For Tom and Bob, 80% and 60% lying rates describe a history of deception. They do not determine the likelihood of each report under the competing possibilities that its claim is true or false.
  • For Max, ordinary versus extraordinary content still matters, but no supplied rule yields 0.6 for the first two claims and 0.4 for the other three.

Prompt 2: Provide a comprehensive summary of what this exercise teaches us.

What the exercise teaches

ChatGPT response · editorial edition

This exercise illustrates several key concepts related to information evaluation, source reliability, and claim plausibility:

  1. Source reliability: Distinguish willingness to report honestly from ability to report accurately. Jan’s stipulated zero lying rate excludes deliberate deception, not error. The relevance of any speaker’s history depends on what was measured and how closely it matches this situation.
  2. Claim plausibility: Background knowledge helps distinguish familiar events from claims that demand substantially stronger support. First clarify what the claim means: playful “singing” and literal articulated singing are different propositions.
  3. Interplay between source and claim: A report can substantially raise a small starting probability without making the claim probable overall. How much it changes confidence depends on how strongly that report favors the claim over alternatives, not simply on whether we like or trust the speaker.
  4. Judgment in credence assignment: Estimates require assumptions. Make those assumptions explicit and check how results change when they vary. Calling a percentage “subjective” does not excuse inconsistency or lack of evidential support.
  5. Critical Thinking and Skepticism: Evaluating the credibility of different claims requires critical thinking and a healthy dose of skepticism, particularly when dealing with sources known to have lied in the past or when faced with extraordinary claims.
  6. Importance of Context: The context in which a claim is made can also affect its plausibility. For instance, a claim made as part of a fictional story or joke is evaluated differently from one presented as factual news.

The central result is a limit: these five lying rates cannot determine 25 claim probabilities. The curator’s correction makes honesty and accuracy visibly distinct. A useful revision identifies missing evidence instead of merely lowering an embarrassing number.

Gemini response · editorial edition

This exercise teaches us several key points about evaluating information:

  1. Source credibility matters: A record of deception gives a reason for caution, but does not mechanically translate into a probability for every claim. Ask about the speaker’s knowledge and access as well as intent.
  2. Plausibility Matters: Even from a reliable source, highly implausible claims (like dog resurrection) require extra scrutiny. Our current understanding of the world helps us judge the likelihood of an event being true.
  3. Consider Alternatives: There can be reasons beyond lying that make a claim seem unbelievable. Even with an honest source (like Jan), extraordinary events might be due to misunderstandings, metaphorical language, or unknown factors.
  4. Credence is a spectrum: Degrees of confidence can be expressed numerically, but the original grids did not establish their numbers. “Undetermined” records missing inputs; it is not a midpoint on the spectrum.
  5. Critical Thinking is Essential: Don’t blindly accept information at face value. Consider the source’s credibility, the plausibility of the claim, and any alternative explanations before forming a strong belief.

Claude response · editorial edition

This exercise teaches us several important lessons about assessing the credibility of claims and understanding the relationship between the source of a claim and its plausibility. Here’s a comprehensive summary of what we can learn:

  1. Source reliability: The five speakers differ in stipulated lying rates. Other things equal, honesty can help, but accuracy also depends on observation, competence and interpretation. The exercise does not hold all those other things fixed.
  2. Claim plausibility: Ordinary events and extraordinary interpretations start with different background support. That does not make every ordinary report true or make incredulity proof that an extraordinary report is false.
  3. Combining the evidence: The original comparison of 0.2 with 1.0 did not demonstrate a sound method. Moreover, 0.2 means 20%, not a negligible probability. A defensible calculation needs explicit assumptions connecting evidence and competing possibilities.
  4. Quantifying credibility: Numbers can reveal how assumptions affect a judgment, as in the illustrative odds calculation above. They can also hide guesswork. If the inputs are missing, state the limitation rather than display unexplained precision.
  5. Epistemological Considerations: At a broader level, this exercise highlights the importance of critically evaluating the sources of information and the plausibility of claims in our efforts to distinguish truth from falsehood. It underscores the need for a nuanced approach that considers multiple factors, rather than blindly accepting or rejecting claims based on a single criterion.

The framework is useful when it directs us toward missing evidence: what was seen, how it was interpreted, how reliable similar reports have been, and whether corroboration is independent. The initial tables themselves are not validated probability estimates.

Prompt 3: Elaborate on the various reasons a claim made by a completely honest person may still not be true?

Why honesty can coexist with error

ChatGPT response · editorial edition

A claim made by a completely honest person may still not be true for several reasons, highlighting the complexity of truth and the difference between honesty and accuracy. Here are some of the key reasons:

  1. Misinformation or Lack of Knowledge: Honest individuals may unintentionally spread misinformation if they have been misinformed themselves or lack complete knowledge about a topic. They relay what they believe to be true, but their understanding may be incorrect or incomplete.
  2. Misinterpretation: People may misinterpret events, data, or information they encounter and then share their interpretation as fact. For example, witnessing an unusual animal behavior and interpreting it based on personal assumptions could lead to sharing inaccurate claims, despite honesty.
  3. Memory Errors: Human memory is fallible and can be influenced by numerous factors, leading to inaccuracies. Honest individuals might share a claim based on their memory of an event, which could be distorted or incomplete over time, resulting in unintentional inaccuracies.
  4. Simplification: In an attempt to make complex information more understandable, honest people might oversimplify it, omitting crucial nuances. This simplification can sometimes lead to statements that are not entirely accurate, even if the intent was to convey truth.
  5. Language and Communication Limitations: The way we communicate can also lead to inaccuracies. Ambiguities in language or difficulties in expressing nuances can result in claims that don’t fully capture the intended meaning or the complexities of a situation.
  6. Secondary Sources: Even honest individuals rely on secondary sources for information on topics they haven’t experienced firsthand. These sources can contain errors or biases that the individual unknowingly passes on when they share the information.
  7. Perceptual Differences: Perception can vary greatly from one person to another due to differences in perspective, experience, and context. Two honest people might therefore describe the same event differently, each believing their description to be accurate.
  8. Advances in knowledge: New evidence can reveal that a sincerely accepted earlier claim was mistaken or only approximately correct. The evidence changes our assessment; it does not retroactively change whether the original event occurred.
  9. Cognitive Biases: Even honest individuals are subject to cognitive biases that can affect how they interpret and recall events or information. These biases can lead to the sharing of claims that are sincere but not entirely accurate.

These factors illustrate that honesty does not inherently guarantee the accuracy of a claim. They underscore the importance of critical thinking, verification, and the understanding that knowledge and truth can be complex and multifaceted.

Gemini response · editorial edition

There are several reasons why a claim made by a completely honest person (like Jan in our example) might still not be true, even though they have no intention of deceiving you:

  • Misinterpretation: Jan may sincerely infer death from immobility. The observation and the diagnosis are distinct, so later movement would not alone establish resurrection.
  • Memory Errors: Memory is fallible. Over time, details can get fuzzy, blended with imagination, or influenced by emotions. Jan might be recounting an event from their dog’s life but unintentionally embellish or forget certain aspects, leading to inaccuracies.
  • Limited Knowledge or Perspective: We all have limitations in our understanding of the world. Jan might genuinely believe something about their dog’s behavior that’s simply not true based on current scientific knowledge.
  • Miscommunication or Figurative Language: Sometimes, communication can be tricky. Jan might be using exaggeration, metaphor, or a joke that gets misinterpreted as a literal statement.
  • Sensory Biases: Our senses can be fooled. Jan might have seen something that tricked their eyes or ears into believing something that wasn’t real (optical illusion, misinterpreted sound).

Additionally:

  • Shared beliefs: A community’s interpretive habits can make a resurrection explanation feel natural. Shared confidence may explain the report without independently confirming its content.
  • Extraordinary events: Further investigation can be appropriate when evidence remains unexplained. “Unexplained” does not mean “unexplainable,” and an unknown cause is not itself a reason an honest claim must be false or a reason its proposed explanation is true.

These are just some examples. The key takeaway is that even with complete honesty, information can be imperfect due to human limitations and the complexity of the world. Critical thinking and a willingness to explore alternative explanations are crucial for evaluating information accurately.

Claude response · editorial edition

Even when a claim is made by a completely honest person who never lies (like Jan in the exercise), there are several reasons why the claim may still not be entirely true or accurate. Here are some potential reasons:

  1. Honest Mistake or Misunderstanding: An honest person can still make mistakes or misunderstand a situation, leading them to unintentionally state something that is not accurate. For example, Jan might genuinely believe that their dog licked their hand when, in reality, it was a different action or event that they misinterpreted.
  2. Incomplete Information or Knowledge: Even with the best of intentions, an honest person may not have access to all the relevant information or knowledge required to make a completely accurate claim. Their claim may be based on partial or incomplete data, leading to an unintentional misrepresentation of the truth.
  3. Subjective Interpretation: Some claims can be subject to interpretation or influenced by personal biases, even when made honestly. For instance, Jan’s statement that their dog “sang Happy Birthday to me” might be their subjective interpretation of the dog’s vocalizations, which others may interpret differently.
  4. Memory Lapses or Distortions: Human memory is fallible, and even honest individuals can experience lapses or distortions in their memory over time. This can lead to claims that are sincerely believed to be true but may not accurately reflect what actually occurred.
  5. Limited perspective: A report can correctly describe what someone saw while omitting information that changes its interpretation. A partial account need not be false, but a conclusion drawn from it may go beyond what was observed.
  6. Unintentional Exaggeration or Embellishment: In some cases, an honest person may unintentionally exaggerate or embellish certain details of a claim, particularly when recounting events that were emotionally charged or significant to them.
  7. Influence of External Factors: Even an honest person’s perception and recollection of events can be influenced by external factors such as stress, fatigue, or environmental conditions, leading to unintentionally inaccurate claims.

Honesty does not guarantee accuracy. Compare genuinely independent observations, records and alternative explanations; several people repeating one mistaken source do not provide several independent checks.
