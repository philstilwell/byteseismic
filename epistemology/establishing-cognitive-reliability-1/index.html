As a philosophical thought experiment, imagine you wake up alone in a room. The last thing you remember is that you were in a car accident. You have no confidence in the reliability of your mind. How might you go about establishing the degree of reliability of your mind?

ChatGPT response · editorial edition

In the room experiment, the remembered accident is itself uncertain evidence. Begin with small comparisons among memory, perception, and reasoning. The aim is a revisable assessment of particular abilities, not an assumption that the whole mind is reliable.

  1. Self-reflection and observation: Record a remembered fact or calculation, then compare it with a separate check if one becomes available. Repeatedly recalling the same answer demonstrates stability, not necessarily accuracy.
  2. Physical Checks: Inspect the environment for clues about your situation. Look for any documents, signs, or personal belongings that might provide context to your current situation. This can include looking for a hospital band, any medications, notes, or instructions left in the room. These might offer clues about your condition and the accuracy of your sensory perceptions.
  3. Consistency testing: Observe the same feature more than once and compare sight with touch where appropriate. Agreement can support limited confidence; correlated errors or a coherent illusion can also produce agreement.
  4. Cognitive exercises: Begin with simple tasks whose results can be checked by another method. Difficulty with an unfamiliar puzzle alone says little about impairment; task familiarity and fatigue affect performance.
  5. Interaction with Others: As soon as you can, interact with others, whether it’s calling out for help or speaking with individuals who might enter the room. Other people can provide feedback and information that can either confirm or question the accuracy of your own thoughts and perceptions.
  6. Seeking External Validation: Once you have access to external sources like a phone or computer, try to verify the information you remember or believe. For instance, you can check factual information online or call someone you trust to confirm your memories and current perceptions.
  7. Professional evaluation: If this were a real accident, seek medical attention rather than attempting to diagnose yourself through a philosophical exercise. Here, professional assessment is a possible later source of comparison, not something available merely because the prompt mentions an accident.

In this thought experiment, these steps can help you piece together a more accurate understanding of your cognitive reliability and grasp the reality of your situation, given the limitations and potential impairments following the accident.

Gemini response · editorial edition

Here’s how you, in this thought experiment, could attempt to assess the reliability of your mind:

Internal Consistency:

  1. Self-Examination: Start by examining your thoughts and memories. Look for inconsistencies. Are your memories fragmented or contradictory? Do your thoughts follow a logical flow, or are they jumping erratically?
  2. Sensory perception: Compare observations across occasions and senses. Disagreement calls for investigation; agreement does not prove that the observations correspond to an independent world.
  3. Reasoning and Problem-solving: Try basic logic puzzles or math problems. If your mind struggles with tasks it should be able to handle, it raises a red flag.

External Validation:

  1. Object interaction: Form a simple prediction about an apparent object, then compare what happens with the prediction. A surprising result can reflect a mistaken expectation or observation; it does not by itself establish a defective mind.
  2. Looking for Clues: Search the room for clues about your situation. Look for a calendar, newspaper, or any object that can provide a date and location. This can help establish if your memories are grounded in reality.
  3. Access to further checks: If communication or safe assistance becomes available, it can provide additional comparisons. The prompt does not say the door is locked, and escaping is not a test of overall cognitive reliability.

Limitations:

  • This is a limited test. Without human interaction, it’s difficult to fully assess your social reasoning and emotional responses.
  • Memories can be faulty even in healthy minds. So, complete reliance on fragmented memories isn’t ideal.

Conclusion:

By combining internal consistency checks with attempts to find external validation, you can get a better sense of your mind’s reliability. Inconsistencies within and a lack of correspondence with the external world suggest a need for caution.

Additional Thoughts:

  • If the room has any technology, like a working computer, you could try to access information and confirm details about the world.
  • Consider the possibility that the car accident might have caused some memory loss.

Remember, this is a thought experiment. In a real situation, seeking medical attention would be the most important step.

ChatGPT response · editorial edition

In the scenario you’ve described, where you’re trying to establish the reliability of your mind following an uncertain event like a car accident, you indeed face foundational philosophical questions about how to establish any knowledge or certainty. The idea of whether to adopt foundationalist presuppositions or to provisionally introduce cognitive functions for testing hinges on two philosophical approaches to knowledge:

  1. Foundationalism: This theory gives some beliefs justification that does not depend on other beliefs; it does not universally require infallible beliefs or an assumed external world. Using perception in an investigation is therefore not, by itself, adopting foundationalism. See the Stanford discussion of foundational justification.
  2. Coherentism: Justification depends on relations within a system of beliefs. Comparing outputs can fit this approach, but provisional testing does not uniquely entail coherentism. Mere absence of contradiction is weaker than evidence-sensitive mutual support.

Practical Application:

  • A foundationalist might treat some perceptual beliefs as noninferentially justified and use them in further checks. This is a claim about their justification, not necessarily a blanket assumption that memory and perception are reliable.
  • Under a coherentist interpretation, compare how observations, memories, and predictions support or challenge one another. A coherent story alone can still be mistaken. Prefer checks with different likely failure modes, and preserve unresolved discrepancies.

You can introduce a faculty for testing without first asserting that it is reliable. The remaining difficulty is that interpreting a test still uses cognition. This dependence does not establish that a particular foundationalist doctrine is mandatory, nor that provisional inquiry defeats every skeptical challenge.

Gemini response · editorial edition

The question leaves open whether foundationalist presuppositions are necessary; it does not affirm that they are. Distinguish what a test uses from what its investigator must believe before testing.

  • External-world hypothesis: One can compare apparent experiences while withholding judgment about their ultimate source. Calling them evidence of an independent world requires an additional inference; interaction alone does not settle it.
  • Logic and reasoning: Comparing results exercises reasoning. This procedural dependence does not require declaring all reasoning reliable. It does limit any claim to have validated reasoning from a wholly independent standpoint.

However, there are ways to mitigate these presuppositions to some extent:

  • Provisional Acceptance: Acknowledge that you can’t be 100% sure about the external world or your own logic. Instead, provisionally accept these functions as working for the purposes of the test. Focus on identifying gross inconsistencies rather than absolute truths.
  • Focus on Comparative Results: Look for discrepancies within your own system. Are some memories clearer than others? Can you solve some problems but not others? These comparisons can still be valuable even with foundational uncertainties.
  • Additional comparisons: Look for records or repeatable interactions that could challenge an expectation. These remain perceived checks, not automatically independent proof of a world beyond experience.

The useful goal is conditional, task-specific confidence. Withholding belief in an external world differs from asserting solipsism. Neither skeptical doubt nor the practical need to act, by itself, establishes the truth of a foundational belief.

Since coherentism works to calculate the degree of cognitive functionality you possess, why do some resort to assuming without evidence elements within this calculus?

Your challenge distinguishes evidence-earned confidence from assumptions inserted merely to make inquiry convenient. Coherence can help assess outputs, but it does not by itself calculate a numerical reliability. The following motives explain assumptions without automatically justifying them.

  1. Practical necessity: Urgency can require choosing an action with incomplete information. A working assumption may help plan that action; the need to act does not establish that the assumption is true.
  2. Epistemic Limitations: Human knowledge is inherently limited, and our cognitive capacities are not equipped to verify every belief or piece of information through direct evidence.   3. Basic beliefs: Treating a belief as justified independently of the belief network would depart from a pure coherentist account. A central belief can instead remain revisable and supported by its relations to others; centrality is not exemption from evidence.
  4. Conservatism in Beliefs: There’s a tendency in human cognition to conserve existing beliefs that have worked well in the past. This conservatism can lead to the acceptance of some assumptions without evidence because they cohere with our pre-existing belief system and have not yet been contradicted by our experiences.
  5. Responding to skepticism: An investigator must use cognitive processes to compare results. It does not follow that they must first believe those processes reliable, or assume their reliability as a premise. Whether such inquiry ultimately provides adequate justification remains disputed.
  6. Simplicity and Economy: The principle of simplicity, or Occam’s Razor, often leads to favoring simpler explanations and assumptions over more complex ones. Assumptions without direct evidence might be adopted because they offer a straightforward way to integrate new and old information into a coherent whole.

Practical convenience explains why assumptions are attractive. It does not show that unsupported assumptions are epistemically required. Your proposal instead permits testing a candidate method before endorsing it, with conclusions limited by the quality and independence of the checks.
