Tom and Randy: timing, credit and causal evidence

Claude response · editorial edition

Tom: Did you see the latest unemployment numbers? They’re way down!

Randy: A lower rate could be good news. Let’s check whether more people found work or whether some stopped looking; those changes have different implications.

Tom: You know why, don’t you? It’s all thanks to the new president’s policies. Follow the leadership!

Randy: The policies might matter, but the rate alone does not tell us whether they caused the whole drop. I’d keep other plausible contributors open until we compare the evidence.

Tom: Oh come on, Randy. Don’t overthink it. The real reason is obvious – the president’s pro-business agenda is paying off.

Randy: Some policies could have an impact. We should also examine overall demand, changes within industries and the size of the labour force, and ask which changes preceded the drop.

Tom: It boils down to the president’s tax cuts and deregulation. Businesses are hiring more because they have extra cash.

Randy: That gives us a mechanism to test. Did firms affected by those changes hire more than otherwise similar firms, after accounting for earlier trends and other differences? A simple correlation would not settle the causal question.

Tom: You always have to make things so complicated. Just look at the timing – unemployment started dropping after the new policies went into effect. The fingerprints are all over this.

Randy: The timing is relevant, but it is not sufficient. Another change might explain both the timing and the hiring. We need a credible comparison with what would probably have happened without these policies.

Tom: If you want to know why unemployment dropped, the smoking gun is the president’s leadership. It’s that simple. The genesis of this job growth is his economic plan.

Randy: A policy could be the main cause of a particular change. What we have not established is your stronger claim that it explains all of this one. Naming possible alternatives does not prove they mattered either.

Tom: Whatever, man. I’m just calling it like I see it – this upswing is a direct result of the president getting government off businesses’ backs. End of story.

Randy: Labour economists can help test the proposed mechanisms and comparisons. I’d want to see their assumptions and uncertainty, not accept a conclusion just because an expert or a politician endorses it.

Tom: You always have to suck the fun out of everything with your “data” and “analysis”. Sometimes the obvious explanation is the right one.

Randy: Sometimes it is. If the evidence singled out this policy, I’d update toward it. My objection is to treating an appealing explanation as settled before doing that work.

Tom: Ugh, here we go again – you and your skepticism of clear-as-day cause and effect. Why is it so hard to give credit where it’s due?

Randy: I’m willing to give credit. Let’s separate whether a policy helped, how much it helped, and whether it explains the whole fall. Those are three different claims.

Tom: Typical scientist response – refusing to see the forest for the trees. When are you going to learn to follow the money and see how the world really works?

Randy: Then let’s follow the evidence as well as the money: name the policy, the affected firms, the period and a useful comparison. We can state in advance what result would make either of us change our mind.

Prompt 2: What might be an optimal way for Randy to lay out all of the potential casual factors and the reasonable credences he assigns to each factor in this scenario?

Defining what each credence is confidence about

ChatGPT response · editorial edition

Randy first needs a defined outcome: the unemployment rate in a specified country and period, with comparable measurement. The US Bureau of Labor Statistics, for example, measures unemployed people as a share of the labour force. A falling rate need not mean rising employment if people leave that labour force. He can then organize candidate explanations and confidence as follows:

  1. List of Potential Causal Factors: Begin with a revisable shortlist, including mechanisms that could move the rate and a check for omitted explanations. “All possible causes” is not an achievable guarantee. Retain the following candidates for investigation:
    • Economic Policies of the Current Administration: Name the particular policy, implementation date and affected groups. “Pro-business” is a political description, not an estimate of its employment effect.
    • Global Economic Conditions: The state of the global economy can significantly affect local employment rates.
    • Technological Advancements: Automation and digital transformation might be creating new jobs or rendering others obsolete.
    • Population Dynamics: Changes in the working-age population, migration patterns, and demographics.
    • Industry Trends: Shifts within key industries, such as a rise in renewable energy or a decline in manufacturing, can impact job availability.
    • Education and Skills Training: The availability of education and vocational training programs can affect the employability of the workforce.
    • Health and Pandemic Factors: Health crises can have immediate and profound effects on employment rates.
  2. Assigning Credences: Define a proposition for each factor, such as whether it lowered the rate by at least 0.1 percentage point over the chosen period under a specified comparison. Credence is confidence that this proposition is true, not the factor’s share of the total change. Several propositions can be true together, so their probabilities need not sum to 100%. These bands are reporting conventions:
    • High Credence (70–100%): A chosen reporting band for a clearly specified proposition, justified by evidence and assumptions. The label alone supplies neither; reserve certainty for cases that actually warrant it.
    • Medium Credence (40–69%): Another reporting band, not a natural boundary or automatic consequence of disagreement among experts. State why the evidence supports this judgment and how sensitive it is to the model.
    • Low Credence (0–39%): Low probability of the specified contribution is different from having little information. Weak information alone does not tell us which numerical probability to assign.
  3. Evidence and Reasoning: For each candidate, record a mechanism, timing, evidence, a comparison group and an update condition. For example: “Hiring rose more in eligible firms than comparable ineligible firms after the tax change; confidence falls if the groups already had different trends.” A comparison only helps to the extent its assumptions are credible.
  4. Interconnectedness of Factors: A policy may influence investment, demand and industry hiring in one causal chain. Those labels need not represent separate additive contributions. A diagram of the proposed links can prevent counting one pathway several times.
  5. Conclusion and Recommendations: Summarize which claims are supported, which remain unresolved and which observations would best discriminate between them. Report confidence and estimated effect size separately. Do not infer that the factor with the highest probability of any contribution necessarily had the largest effect.

This method replaces an argument over a single headline with answerable questions. It can support a finding that one cause dominated, several interacted, or the available evidence is insufficient. The rational constraint is responsiveness to evidence, not a requirement to multiply causes.

Gemini response · editorial edition

Randy can make a short comparison without pretending to list every possible cause. He should define the outcome, separate confidence from effect size, and explain what evidence would change his judgment.

1. Acknowledge the Complexity:

Start by acknowledging the complexity of the situation:

“Tom, the lower unemployment rate is worth investigating. First let’s check employment and labour-force participation, then ask what changed and when.”

2. List Potential Factors:

Then, list some potential causal factors and briefly explain them:

“For instance, the president’s policies might have played a role, but so could other things like:”

  • Overall Economic Conditions: Stronger demand could support hiring, but “the economy improved” is partly a description of the outcome. Specify the underlying change and collect evidence before assigning high confidence.
  • Job Market Trends: An expanding industry could contribute. Check its size, hiring and losses elsewhere rather than assigning a medium rating from the possibility alone.
  • Population Changes: Entries to and exits from the labour force can change the rate. Track both the numerator and denominator before interpreting it as job creation.

3. Emphasize Importance of Data:

Emphasize the need for data to assess the true impact:

“For each claim, we need dates, affected groups and a comparison that addresses other differences. Then I can say how confident I am that it contributed and, separately, estimate how large the effect was.”

4. Remain Open-Minded:

Finally, express openness to the possibility of the president’s policies playing a role, but within a broader context:

“I’m open to the president’s policies explaining much of the change. I’m also open to little or no effect. We should say what result would move us toward either conclusion.”

Benefits of this approach:

  • Fairness: Use the same evidential standards for favoured and disfavoured explanations. A list by itself does not remove bias or guarantee completeness.
  • Nuance: It acknowledges the complexity of economic trends.
  • Openness: Permit evidence to identify a dominant cause as well as multiple contributors; neither answer is built into the method.

Randy now has a plan for improving the explanation. Merely replacing “the president did it” with “it is complicated” would leave the causal question unanswered.

Prompt 3: Create a hypothetical table of the potential causes Tom and Randy might consider to rationally evaluate the issue they are discussing.

Two hypothetical cause tables with explicit limits

ChatGPT response · editorial edition

Invented teaching example, not an estimate of a real economy. Suppose we study a one-year fall in a country’s unemployment rate. For every named row, the proposition is: “Under the specified causal model, this factor’s realized change made the final rate at least 0.1 percentage point lower than it would have been without that change.” The percentages below are stipulated confidence judgments under the imagined evidence described in the notes. They preserve the source’s numerical exercise without pretending publicity or generic plausibility warrants a number. They are not shares of the observed fall, so they need not total 100%. Overlapping causal pathways and interactions require additional modelling before any attribution of shares.

Imagined evidence and limitations behind the exercise:

  • Economic Policies of the Current Administration — 70%: Suppose the policy preceded a relative hiring increase in eligible firms, with reasonably similar earlier trends but some remaining differences. That hypothetical comparison, not publicity, motivates the stipulated confidence. Other models or failed comparison assumptions could lower it.
  • Global Economic Conditions — 50%: Suppose trading partners’ demand improved, but exposure and timing provide only mixed evidence of a contribution large enough to meet the threshold.
  • Technological Advancements — 50%: Suppose firms report both automation-related losses and new work. The net contribution to the defined fall is unresolved; this is not a 50% estimate that technology affected any job anywhere.
  • Population Dynamics — 30%: Suppose measured labour-force composition changed little, leaving a comparatively weak case for a threshold-sized contribution. If many job seekers instead left the labour force, this judgment would need revision.
  • Industry Trends — 50%: Suppose gains in one industry coexist with losses elsewhere. Industry change may also transmit the policy or global-demand effect, so it cannot simply be added as an independent share.
  • Education and Skills Training — 40%: Suppose programme completion increased, but placement gains are small or delayed and participant selection remains a concern.
  • Health and Pandemic Factors — 75%: Suppose this invented year follows a health disruption and affected sectors show a sizeable reopening rebound. This supplies a reason for the highest stipulated confidence in this particular scenario, not a general ranking for all unemployment changes.
  • Market and Business Confidence — 60%: Suppose improved expectations preceded investment and hiring, but confidence may itself reflect anticipated policy or demand changes. Treat it as a possible pathway, not automatically an independent cause.
  • External Investments and Foreign Trade Policies — 25%: Suppose the measured changes are modest and concentrated in small sectors. A larger exposure than assumed would call for updating.
  • Environmental Policies and Sustainability Initiatives — 20%: Suppose few projects had begun during the specified year and sectoral losses partly offset gains. Later effects remain outside this one-year claim.
  • Legislative Changes Affecting Labor Markets — 45%: Suppose the changes affected different groups in conflicting ways and comparison evidence remains inconclusive. Do not count a law again if it is already included in the administration-policy row.
  • Unknown Causes: This is a reminder that the shortlist may miss something, not a measurable residual with an automatic probability. Define a proposition about omitted influences and a model for it before assigning a number.

The precise values are teaching stipulations, not calculated posteriors. A real inquiry would need evidence sufficient to defend or replace them. Confidence of 75% rather than 70% neither proves a larger effect nor licenses a claim that 75% of the unemployment fall came from that factor.

Prompt 4: Are there salient analogies to show that jumping to a single cause is often misguided?

Analogies for alternatives, interactions and decisive causes

ChatGPT response · editorial edition

These analogies expose two different mistakes: choosing one explanation before comparing alternatives, and ignoring contributors that act together. Neither mistake implies that every outcome must have many equally important causes.

  1. The Doctor’s Diagnosis Analogy: A symptom may fit several competing explanations. Selecting one without the relevant checks resembles crediting a policy from a headline alone. The logical lesson is to compare alternatives; it does not establish that every patient has several simultaneous illnesses.
  2. Cooking a Gourmet Meal Analogy: Ingredients, technique and timing jointly affect a meal. Yet the question “why was this batch different?” may have a narrower answer: perhaps the oven failed while everything else stayed much the same. Likewise, distinguish the background conditions for employment from the particular cause of a change in its rate.
  3. Orchestra Performance Analogy: Players, conductor and acoustics jointly shape a performance. A good result does not give each an equal share of credit; nor can removing one essential part reveal a unique percentage attributable to it. Interaction makes a simple pie chart misleading.
  4. Ecosystem Balance Analogy: An ecosystem’s health is influenced by a balance of factors, including predator-prey relationships, plant diversity, climate conditions, and human activity. Attributing a change in the ecosystem, such as a decrease in a particular species, to one factor without considering others can lead to misdirected conservation efforts. This complexity mirrors the interplay of variables affecting economic trends.
  5. Sports Team Success Analogy: The success of a sports team depends on the players’ skills, the coach’s strategy, the team’s morale, and even external factors like weather or referee decisions. Crediting a win solely to a star player ignores the contributions of the team, the coaching staff, and situational factors, just as attributing economic improvements to a single policy overlooks other significant influences.

Use each analogy to ask a concrete question: which alternatives remain, which factors interact, and what changed relative to a comparison? Analogies suggest ways to investigate the economy; they do not provide evidence for the causal weights in either table.

Gemini response · editorial edition

Absolutely! Here are a few salient analogies to show that jumping to a single cause is often misguided:

1. The Garden Analogy:

Imagine a beautiful garden with vibrant flowers. To understand why the flowers are flourishing, you wouldn’t just look at the sunshine (one factor). You’d consider the quality of soil, watering schedule, pest control, and overall climate (multiple factors). Similarly, complex outcomes like unemployment rates are influenced by a variety of factors, not just one policy.

2. The Car Analogy:

If a car will not start, battery, fuel and electrical connections are possible explanations. Checking alternatives need not end in a many-cause diagnosis: a failed battery might be sufficient to explain this episode. The mistake is deciding before checking, not finding a single fault after a good test.

3. The Recipe Analogy:

A delicious cake doesn’t come from just one ingredient like sugar (one factor). It requires a balanced combination of flour, eggs, butter, and other components (multiple factors). Similarly, economic trends are a result of a mix of policies, consumer behaviour, and global influences, not just one government action.

4. The Medical Diagnosis Analogy:

A diagnostic judgment distinguishes the evidence observed from the explanation proposed. Several symptoms can support one diagnosis, while one symptom can fit several diagnoses. Likewise, several economic indicators are evidence to assess; they are not automatically several causes of unemployment.

The garden and recipe emphasize joint contributions; the car and diagnostic examples emphasize competing explanations. Both help Randy investigate without making complexity itself into a dogma. A decisive single cause remains possible when evidence supports it.

Discussion Questions: Beyond a Single Cause

  1. Beyond the President: In the conversation, what other potential factors influencing unemployment rates were mentioned besides the president’s policies?
  2. Data-Driven Decisions: Why is it important to analyze data when evaluating the impact of policies on complex issues like unemployment?
  3. The Nuance of Causation: How can someone distinguish between correlation and causation when analyzing trends like unemployment rates?
  4. Economic Complexity: How would you distinguish background conditions, interacting contributors and a dominant cause of a particular change?
  5. Open-mindedness in Analysis: How can we approach discussions about economic trends with a balanced and open-minded perspective?
  6. The Role of Evidence: How can evidence be used to support or refute claims about the causes of economic phenomena?
  7. The Limits of Prediction: How can a useful probabilistic prediction differ from certainty about a policy’s effect?
  8. Global Influences: How do international factors, like trade agreements or global economic conditions, influence a nation’s unemployment rate?
  9. Technological Disruption: In what ways can technological advancements create or eliminate jobs, and how can we prepare for these changes?
  10. Policy Mix: How might different government policies, beyond just economic ones, indirectly affect the job market? (e.g., education, infrastructure)
  11. The Skeptic’s Role: Why might some people, like Randy, be skeptical of simplistic explanations for economic trends?
  12. Beyond Politics: What shared evidence standard and advance update conditions could reduce political bias without pretending it disappears automatically?
  13. Long-Term vs. Short-Term: How can policies be designed to address both short-term unemployment concerns and long-term economic growth?
  14. Individual vs. Systemic Issues: To what extent do individual factors like job skills or work ethic contribute to unemployment rates?
  15. The Power of Collaboration: How can economists, politicians, and the public work together to develop effective strategies for managing unemployment?
