Prompt 1: Is the notion that extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence flawed in any way?

The maxim and its limits

ChatGPT response · editorial edition

The maxim “extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence,” associated with Carl Sagan, is a useful shorthand rather than a complete rule. One precise interpretation is that a claim with low prior probability needs a large likelihood ratio to become highly credible. Mere unfamiliarity does not justify assigning a tiny prior, and evidence must be evaluated against plausible alternatives.

  1. Definition of Extraordinary: One of the primary challenges lies in defining what constitutes an “extraordinary” claim. The perception of what is extraordinary can vary widely among different individuals, cultures, or scientific disciplines, making it a subjective criterion.
  2. Evidence Evaluation: The notion presupposes an ability to evaluate the quality and sufficiency of evidence objectively. However, biases, limitations in current scientific knowledge, and the availability of evidence can all influence this evaluation, potentially leading to disagreements on what constitutes “extraordinary evidence.”
  3. Scientific Paradigms: Scientific understanding evolves over time, and what is considered extraordinary in one era may become commonplace in another as evidence accumulates and paradigms shift. This dynamic nature of science means that the application of this principle may change over time.
  4. Inquiry versus acceptance: Requiring strong evidence before accepting a claim need not prohibit investigating it. Misuse would involve refusing to consider relevant evidence or setting a threshold no possible observation could meet.
  5. Burden of proof: Difficulty obtaining evidence may justify further research or suspension of judgment; it does not by itself justify accepting a claim on weaker support.
  6. Common standards: All claims can be evaluated by the same probability rules while requiring different evidential strength to reach the same confidence. Posterior odds equal prior odds multiplied by the evidence’s likelihood ratio.

In conclusion, while the principle that extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence serves as a valuable guideline in scientific inquiry and critical thinking, its application is not without challenges. It requires careful consideration of what is deemed extraordinary, the nature and quality of evidence, and the ever-evolving landscape of scientific knowledge. This highlights the importance of maintaining an open, yet critically evaluative stance towards new claims and evidence.

Gemini response · editorial edition

The idea that “extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence,” often referred to as the Sagan Standard, is a valuable principle for critical thinking, but it does have some limitations and potential for misuse. Here’s a breakdown:

Strengths:

  • Promotes healthy skepticism: It encourages caution when evaluating claims that go against established knowledge or seem highly unlikely. This is crucial in preventing acceptance of misinformation and pseudoscience.
  • Highlights burden of proof: It reminds us that the responsibility to provide convincing evidence lies with the person making the claim, especially if it’s unusual or unexpected.
  • Recognizes different levels of evidence: It suggests that the strength and quality of evidence needed to believe a claim should be proportional to how extraordinary it is.

Weaknesses:

  • Subjectivity: Defining “extraordinary” can be subjective and context-dependent. What seems impossible today might become accepted science tomorrow.
  • Potential for misuse: It can be used to dismiss potentially legitimate but unconventional ideas just because they seem strange.
  • Quality, not just quantity: Evidence should discriminate between the claim and serious alternatives. Independent checks may help, but ruling out every imaginable alternative is an impossible standard.

Overall:

The Sagan Standard offers a valuable starting point for evaluating claims, but it shouldn’t be applied dogmatically. A nuanced approach that considers the specific claim, the quality of evidence, and the wider context is necessary for truly critical thinking.

Here are some additional points to consider:

  • Who is making the claim? Consider their expertise, track record, and potential biases.
  • What is the evidence based on? Is it peer-reviewed research, personal anecdotes, or something else?
  • Are there alternative explanations? Have these been adequately addressed?
  • How does this claim fit into our existing understanding of the world?

By examining claims on a case-by-case basis and using multiple tools for critical thinking, we can avoid falling prey to misinformation and make informed judgments about the world around us.

Yes. I did not show that those historical examples contradicted or misused the standard. They support neither a blanket condemnation nor a blanket endorsement of every skeptic.

However, the point I was trying to make is that misuse can occur around how the principle is applied, not inherent flaws within the principle itself. Here are some clarifications and alternative examples:

Misuse around application:

  • Setting an unreasonably high bar for “extraordinary”: What counts as “extraordinary” is often subjective and can change over time. Applying rigid standards for “extraordinary” might unintentionally stifle new ideas.
  • Ignoring alternative explanations: Sometimes, “extraordinary” claims can be explained by mundane factors not considered initially. Failing to explore all reasonable explanations before demanding “extraordinary evidence” can lead to biases.

Alternative examples of misuse:

  • Hypothetical misuse: Refusing to inspect a report solely because it concerns an unidentified aerial observation. An unexplained sighting and a claim of extraterrestrial origin are different claims requiring different evidence.
  • Rejecting new medical treatments out of hand because they seem too effective to be true, without giving them a fair chance through proper trials and rigorous evaluation.
  • Accepting anecdotal evidence as sufficient for claims with significant implications, ignoring the need for more robust and replicable data.

The essence of the Sagan Standard is valuable, but we need to apply it cautiously, considering the specific context, quality of evidence, and potential for bias. We should avoid using it as a blunt tool to simply dismiss anything unfamiliar or seemingly unlikely. A nuanced approach to evaluating claims, considering their merits rather than just their perceived “extraordinariness,” is crucial for fostering true critical thinking and scientific progress.

The correction is to distinguish a legitimate evidential demand from refusing to update when adequate evidence is presented.

The principle that extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence serves as a cornerstone of scientific skepticism and method, ensuring that claims, especially those that challenge established knowledge, are rigorously tested and substantiated before being accepted. The critical point is that while the demand for extraordinary evidence is both legitimate and necessary, it must be applied judiciously to avoid the unwarranted dismissal of claims that may, in fact, be supported by substantial evidence. This entails several key considerations:

  1. Open-mindedness: While skepticism is a healthy and necessary part of the scientific process, it should not lead to outright dismissal without fair evaluation. Scientists and scholars must remain open to examining the evidence for extraordinary claims with an unbiased perspective, acknowledging that scientific understanding evolves and that today’s extraordinary claims may become tomorrow’s accepted facts.
  2. Evaluation of Evidence: The evidence presented in support of extraordinary claims must be evaluated on its own merits, considering the quality, reproducibility, and coherence of the data. This means not only looking for flaws or weaknesses but also recognizing the potential for new insights or breakthroughs that the evidence may offer.
  3. Avoiding Cynicism: There’s a fine line between skepticism and cynicism. While skepticism encourages questioning and evidence-based examination, cynicism may lead to automatic rejection of new ideas without due consideration. The application of the Sagan Standard should be guided by a genuine desire to understand and test, rather than a reflex to dismiss.
  4. Scientific Progress: History has shown that scientific breakthroughs often occur when evidence challenges and eventually overturns existing paradigms. The demand for extraordinary evidence should not become a barrier to innovation but rather a mechanism to ensure that new theories are thoroughly vetted and supported by robust evidence.
  5. Communication: Clear and open communication about what constitutes extraordinary evidence and why it is required can help mitigate misunderstandings and promote a more productive dialogue between proponents of new ideas and the broader scientific community.

In essence, the legitimate demand for extraordinary evidence for extraordinary claims must be balanced with a commitment to fair and open evaluation of the evidence. This approach ensures that the scientific process remains dynamic and adaptable, capable of incorporating new discoveries while maintaining rigorous standards of evidence and reasoning.

Gemini response · editorial edition

Yes, you’re absolutely right. The key takeaway shouldn’t be to throw out the notion of “extraordinary claims requiring extraordinary evidence” entirely. It’s a valuable principle for critical thinking, helping us avoid readily accepting sensational claims or unsubstantiated beliefs.

The caveat about potential abuse is indeed a reminder to avoid two extremes:

  1. Blindly rejecting any seemingly “extraordinary” claim without examining the evidence: This can impede scientific progress and stifle exploration of new ideas. As you rightly pointed out, when compelling evidence for an extraordinary claim exists, it should be taken seriously and objectively evaluated.
  2. Applying an unreasonably high bar for “extraordinary” evidence: This risks creating biases and overlooking potentially legitimate but novel discoveries.

The key lies in applying the principle with flexibility and critical thinking:

  • Evaluate the evidence objectively: The quality and quantity of evidence offered should be scrutinized, regardless of the claim’s perceived “extraordinariness.”
  • Consider the context: What constitutes “extraordinary” can evolve over time, influenced by existing knowledge and the specific field of inquiry.
  • State the standard clearly: The confidence warranted by evidence depends on prior support and the evidence’s diagnostic strength. The consequences of error affect what action to take, not the probability the same evidence warrants.
  • Be open to alternative explanations: Even seemingly strange claims might have mundane explanations that should be explored before jumping to conclusions.

By adopting this more nuanced approach, we can utilize the “extraordinary claims requiring extraordinary evidence” principle effectively as a tool for critical thinking while simultaneously remaining open to new discoveries and avoiding unnecessary dismissal of potentially valid ideas.

Together, these principles facilitate a balanced and dynamic scientific process. That’s a much more precise and nuanced way of phrasing it, and it gets closer to the ideal approach. Here are some additional points to consider for a truly fair representation:
