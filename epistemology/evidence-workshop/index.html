Prompt 1: Tom claims he has a spherical cube of gold in his pocket. He produces flakes of gold as evidence for his claim. What strength of evidence do those flakes of gold constitute, given Tom’s claim?

Tom claims he has a spherical cube of gold in his pocket. He produces flakes of gold as evidence for his claim. What strength of evidence do those flakes of gold constitute, given Tom’s claim?

ChatGPT response · editorial edition

Under the ordinary meanings of sphere and cube, Tom’s description is contradictory. Gold flakes cannot support the existence of that exact object. They may establish a weaker claim about access to gold, provided their composition is confirmed; that is a different proposition.

  1. Composition: Confirmed gold flakes show that gold is present. Without a demonstrated connection to a larger object, they do not establish the composition or existence of the object Tom describes.
  2. Irrelevance to Geometry: The gold flakes provide no evidence concerning the geometric nature of the object. They do not support the claim that the object is a cube, let alone a “spherical cube,” which is a geometric impossibility given standard definitions.
  3. Strength: Evidence for a component of a claim is not automatically evidence for the entire conjunction. A contradictory conjunction has no possible instance under the fixed meanings being used.
  4. Conclusion: The flakes provide no support for a literal spherical cube. Ask whether Tom means a rounded cube, a sphere inside a cube, or some other coherent shape; only after clarification can an object-specific empirical test begin.

Gemini response · editorial edition

The flakes provide no support for the exact contradictory description “spherical cube.” They can bear on a weaker claim about gold, but calling that weak evidence for the whole claim obscures the contradiction.

Supporting the Claim:

  • Partial Match: The flakes are indeed made of gold, which aligns with the material of the claimed object.
  • Multiple sources: Genuine gold flakes could come from jewelry or other gold objects unrelated to the claim. If their composition has not been checked, imitation flakes are another possibility; the prompt itself calls them gold.

Contradicting the Claim:

  • Shape Mismatch: The claim specifies a “spherical cube,” a geometrically impossible shape. Finding gold flakes doesn’t address this impossibility.
  • Quantity and Origin: The number and origin of the flakes are unknown. A few flakes could easily be obtained from sources unrelated to a solid cube.

Additional Considerations:

  • No Direct Observation: We haven’t directly observed the object Tom claims to have, raising doubts about its existence and characteristics.
  • Alternative Explanations: The presence of gold flakes doesn’t rule out other explanations for their origin.

Photographs or inspection could reveal a real object and clarify the intended description. They cannot turn a contradiction under unchanged definitions into a possible shape. Keep uncertainty about wording separate from evidence for the literal claim.

Remember, critical thinking involves evaluating evidence in its entirety, considering both supporting and contradicting factors, and seeking alternative explanations before reaching conclusions.

2Original prompt · edited responses

Prompt 2: Ron claims that the following two claims have equal evidentiary strength if the number and quality of eyewitnesses for both claims are equal.  1: A dog swam across the lake.  2: A dog flew across the lake.How might we respond to Ron?

Ron claims that the following two claims have equal evidentiary strength if the number and quality of eyewitnesses for both claims are equal.  1: A dog swam across the lake.  2: A dog flew across the lake.How might we respond to Ron?

ChatGPT response · editorial edition

First clarify “flew”: transport by aircraft differs from unaided biological flight. Next distinguish the amount testimony changes the odds from the probability of the claim after all evidence is considered. Equal witness number and general credibility do not settle either quantity.

  1. Plausibility and Prior Knowledge:
  • Swimming is compatible with ordinary canine abilities, though the particular distance and conditions matter. Its higher background plausibility does not automatically make any given witness report strong evidence.
  • Unaided dog flight conflicts with established canine anatomy. A report therefore requires careful comparison with transport, misidentification, deception, or mistaken wording. It is an empirical anomaly, unlike the logical contradiction in the spherical-cube exercise.
  1. Extraordinary Claims Require Extraordinary Evidence:
  • For a low-prior claim to become highly credible, the evidence must strongly favor it over relevant alternatives. The slogan about extraordinary evidence expresses this demand; it does not license rejecting every unfamiliar report regardless of its content.
  1. Evaluation of Evidence:
  • Ask how likely the reports would be if the event occurred and if it did not. Witnesses may share a source or the same misleading view, so multiple accounts need not provide independent checks.
  1. Consistency with Known Physical Laws:
  • Background knowledge affects prior plausibility. The likelihood ratio measures how strongly the report favors the claim over its alternative. These are distinct parts of a Bayesian assessment; an unlikely claim can receive strong evidence while remaining unlikely overall.

If “equal quality” means equal likelihood ratios, the two testimony sets can have equal incremental evidential force while yielding different final probabilities because the priors differ. If it merely means equally sincere and observant witnesses, even equal evidential force has not been established. See Bayes’ theorem and measures of evidential support.

Gemini response · editorial edition

Ron’s conclusion depends on what “quality” and “evidentiary strength” mean. Equal numbers and reputations do not ensure equal diagnostic value; equal diagnostic value would still not ensure equal final credibility.

Prior Probability:

  • Claim 1: Swimming is compatible with the animal’s ordinary capacities. Background plausibility and the effect of the actual witness evidence must nevertheless be assessed separately.
  • Claim 2: Unaided flight has very low plausibility given canine anatomy. This is not a logical impossibility, and transport would change the interpretation of the claim.

Nature of the Claim:

  • Claim 1: Falls within the realm of established natural phenomena, making it inherently more believable within our current understanding of the world.
  • Claim 2: An unusual biological event requires checking alternatives carefully. Do not replace that work with the unsupported assertion that every interpretation violates physical law.

Consider Alternative Explanations:

  • Misidentification: For either report, ask what observers could have mistaken for the described event. The relevant alternatives differ between swimming and apparent flight.
  • Hoax or illusion: Consider these alternatives where the circumstances support them. An unusual claim alone does not establish that a hoax occurred or that witnesses are dishonest.

Therefore:

  • A report’s evidential force depends on its relative likelihood under competing explanations. Final confidence also depends on prior plausibility; keeping these separate answers Ron more precisely.
  • With equal likelihood ratios and a higher prior for swimming, the swimming claim has the higher posterior probability. For illustration only, prior odds of 1:1 and 1:999 multiplied by the same factor of 9 become 9:1 and 9:999—probabilities 90% and about 0.89%. These invented numbers illustrate the distinction, not measured dog-event frequencies.

Responding to Ron:

  • You could explain the concept of prior probability and how it influences the believability of a claim based on existing knowledge.
  • Highlight the importance of considering alternative explanations, especially for extraordinary claims like a dog flying.
  • Emphasize the need to evaluate evidence not just in quantity but also in context and its alignment with established knowledge.

Good inquiry checks whether evidence challenges background expectations as well as whether it agrees with them. Consistency with accepted knowledge is relevant context, not a rule requiring all evidence to conform.

Prompt 3: Mary claims that while the evidence for telepathy is inconclusive, her feelings that telepathy must be true are evidence for its truth. How might you respond to Mary?

Mary claims that while the evidence for telepathy is inconclusive, her feelings that telepathy must be true are evidence for its truth. How might you respond to Mary?

ChatGPT response · editorial edition

Responding to Mary’s claim involves addressing the distinction between subjective feelings and objective evidence, as well as the standards for evidence in scientific inquiry. Here’s a structured response:

  1. Differentiating Between Subjective Feelings and Objective Evidence:
  • Mary’s feeling is evidence that she feels convinced, assuming an accurate report. Whether it supports telepathy depends on whether that feeling is more likely if telepathy exists than if it does not. Strong conviction alone supplies no such comparison.
  1. The Role of Anecdotal Evidence:
  • Personal feelings or experiences, often categorized as anecdotal evidence, can serve as a starting point for scientific investigation but cannot alone validate a phenomenon. Anecdotal evidence is limited by its susceptibility to cognitive biases, such as confirmation bias, where individuals may give more weight to experiences that support their beliefs and disregard those that do not.
  1. Scientific Standards for Evidence:
  • A useful test specifies predictions before the outcome, prevents ordinary communication or cueing, records failures as well as successes, and checks replication. Statistical significance alone does not exclude chance, bias, error, or fraud; the whole design and record matter.
  1. The Place of Subjective Experience in Scientific Inquiry:
  • Reported experiences can themselves be data. The inference from an experience to its proposed cause needs separate evaluation; treating all subjective reports as non-evidence would be too broad.
  1. The Importance of Open-minded Skepticism:
  • An open-minded yet skeptical approach is crucial in science. This means being open to exploring new ideas and phenomena, like telepathy, while also maintaining a rigorous standard for what is considered evidence. This approach ensures that scientific knowledge advances in a way that is both innovative and grounded in empirical evidence.
  1. Encouraging Further Research:
  • If Mary is passionate about her beliefs in telepathy, one might encourage her to explore the existing scientific literature on the subject and to support further research. This research should aim to meet the scientific standards for evidence, thereby contributing to a better understanding of whether telepathy exists as a real phenomenon.

Respect Mary’s experience while distinguishing confidence from its object. Ask what observations would favor telepathy over ordinary alternatives and what would reduce her confidence. The prompt supplies no reason to regard intensity of belief alone as diagnostic.

Gemini response · editorial edition

Mary’s claim raises interesting points about the role of personal belief and feelings in evaluating evidence. Here are some ways you could respond:

Emphasize the Difference Between Feelings and Evidence:

  • Distinguish evidence about the feeling from evidence about telepathy. Subjective reports can be investigated, but Mary must explain why her conviction favors telepathy over alternative causes of conviction.
  • Highlight that many people hold strong beliefs about various things, but not all beliefs translate to factual realities.

Explain the Challenges of Subjectivity:

  • Feelings may reflect experience, expectations, cues, or wishful thinking. This does not make every feeling evidentially worthless; it means its accuracy and diagnostic value cannot be assumed.
  • Mention that relying solely on subjective experiences can lead to confirmation bias, where we tend to interpret information in a way that confirms our existing beliefs, ignoring contradictory evidence.

Propose Alternative Explanations:

  • Suggest that Mary’s feelings could be explained by other factors, such as intuition, coincidence, or misinterpreted social cues. This doesn’t negate her experiences, but encourages exploring alternative interpretations.
  • Offer to research the topic together using objective and verifiable evidence, like scientific studies or experiments exploring telepathy. This can foster a more informed discussion based on shared resources.

Seek Common Ground:

  • Acknowledge that the concept of telepathy is fascinating and has captured human imagination for centuries. This shows respect for Mary’s interest and opens a space for further discussion.
  • Emphasize the importance of open-mindedness and critical thinking when exploring unusual claims. Encourage a willingness to weigh evidence objectively and revise beliefs based on new information.

Remember:

  • The goal is not to invalidate Mary’s feelings or beliefs, but to encourage critical thinking and understanding the distinction between personal experiences and objectively verifiable evidence.
  • Be respectful and open to her perspective while offering alternative explanations and resources for further exploration.
  • The most important aspect is to engage in a constructive and informative dialogue that encourages learning and critical thinking about evidence and claims.

Ultimately, the decision to accept or reject claims like telepathy lies with each individual. However, fostering a culture of critical thinking and evidence-based evaluation can lead to more informed and reasoned conclusions.

Prompt 4: Which of the following statements is most likely true for a 30-year-old man in Tokyo who just bought a fishing pole? 1: His name is Koji and he enjoys fishing. 2: His name is Koji.

Which of the following statements is most likely true for a 30-year-old man in Tokyo who just bought a fishing pole? 1: His name is Koji and he enjoys fishing. 2: His name is Koji.

ChatGPT response · editorial edition

Statement 2 is at least as probable as statement 1; it need not be strictly more probable. The governing principle is the conjunction rule, not a general preference for shorter descriptions.

Here’s the reasoning:

  • The first statement makes two specific claims: that his name is Koji and that he enjoys fishing. While the purchase of a fishing pole might suggest an interest in fishing, it does not guarantee that the man’s name is Koji or that he enjoys fishing. The assumption about enjoyment is based on the action of buying a fishing pole, which could be for various reasons other than personal enjoyment (e.g., a gift, a one-time event, or curiosity).
  • Let K mean “his name is Koji,” F mean “he enjoys fishing,” and E be all the supplied information. Every case in K and F is also in K, so P(K and F | E) ≤ P(K | E). The frequency of the name is not needed for this comparison.

If P(K | E)>0, the product rule gives P(K and F | E)=P(K | E)P(F | K,E). Equality is possible if F is certain conditional on K and E; both sides can also be zero. No numerical probabilities follow from the prompt. The fishing-pole purchase does not reverse the subset relation.

Prompt 5: Read the following statements and rank the quality of evidence provided from strongest to weakest. Justify your rankings.

Read the following statements and rank the quality of evidence provided from strongest to weakest. Justify your rankings.

  1. A peer-reviewed study showing a statistical correlation between exercise and improved mental health.
  2. A personal anecdote about feeling happier after starting to exercise.
  3. A news article discussing a single study on exercise and mental health.
  4. A testimonial from a celebrity endorsing a specific type of exercise for mental health.

ChatGPT response · editorial edition

For a general claim about exercise and mental health, a provisional ranking is study first, accurate report of that study second, then the two individual endorsements. Their last-place ordering is underdetermined without details. The following preserves the original sequence while making that limitation explicit.

1: A peer-reviewed study showing a statistical correlation between exercise and improved mental health.

  • Justification: A well-conducted study can offer systematic observations and inspectable methods. Peer review does not guarantee soundness, generalizability, or causation; correlation alone does not establish that exercise caused the outcome.

2: A news article discussing a single study on exercise and mental health.

  • Justification: An accurate report can convey the study’s findings, but is a secondary route to the same underlying evidence, not independent confirmation. Check the original methods and whether the article fairly describes uncertainty.

3 (provisional, tied with 4 unless further details distinguish them): A testimonial from a celebrity endorsing a specific type of exercise for mental health.

  • Justification: Fame adds no evidential force to an endorsement. A documented observation may matter, while an unexplained or paid recommendation may tell us little. Nothing in the prompt establishes that this testimonial outranks the personal anecdote.

4 (provisional, tied with 3 unless further details distinguish them): A personal anecdote about feeling happier after starting to exercise.

  • Justification: The anecdote describes a before-and-after experience. It can motivate investigation, but cannot isolate exercise from other changes or establish a population effect. Its comparative value depends on the detail and reliability of the report.

This ranking is conditional on study and reporting quality. Source labels alone do not establish a universal hierarchy. A flawed study can be less informative than a carefully documented observation, and two reports of one study must not be double-counted.

Gemini response · editorial edition

Ranking the Quality of Evidence:

A defensible provisional order is the study, an accurate article about it, then the two individual reports. The last two cannot be strictly ranked from their descriptions alone; the listed order is illustrative rather than established.

1. Peer-reviewed study showing a statistical correlation:

  • Strength: A study may offer systematic comparison and methods that readers can inspect. Its design, measurements, uncertainty, and analysis determine how much the reported association supports the claim; peer review is no guarantee.
  • Weakness: We need to further consider the study’s design, sample size, and potential limitations to fully assess its strength.

2. News article discussing a single study:

  • Strength: Accurate reporting can communicate the study and its limitations. It is not a second independent study, and credibility does not follow merely from appearing in a news article.
  • Weakness: News articles may omit details, potentially misinterpreting or sensationalizing the study’s findings. It’s crucial to evaluate the original study if possible.

3. Personal anecdote:

  • Strength: A detailed report can describe one person’s experience and suggest a hypothesis. It cannot by itself support a population-wide causal conclusion.
  • Weakness: An individual’s experience might not reflect a broader cause-and-effect relationship due to potential confounding factors and biases.

4. Celebrity testimonial:

  • Strength: A celebrity may supply an observation like anyone else. Audience size is a measure of reach, not evidential strength.
  • Weakness: Check relevant expertise, documentation, and possible sponsorship rather than inferring competence or dishonesty from celebrity status alone. The prompt does not establish whether this report is weaker than the personal anecdote.

Additional Points:

  • It’s important to consider the source of the information and their potential biases.
  • Sample size and study design significantly impact the reliability of conclusions.
  • Be cautious about generalizations from individual experiences or anecdotal evidence.

Remember: This is just an example, and the strength of evidence can vary depending on specific details and context. Always strive to critically evaluate the source, methodology, and limitations of any evidence you encounter.
