1Original prompt · edited responses

Prompt 1: Imagine a new ideology proposes an entity that conveys testable truths about the universe. The entity constantly outputs predictions that turn out to be true. However, the inner workings of that entity are inscrutable. Are we rational in placing as much confidence in the future predictions of that entity as its track record of predictive successes warrants. Or does ignorance of its inner workings prevent this?

Predictive trust without access to mechanism

ChatGPT response · editorial edition

Your question touches on a profound issue at the intersection of epistemology (the study of knowledge) and philosophy of science, particularly around the themes of reliability, understanding, and trust in epistemic authorities or mechanisms.

Yes: independently verified predictive performance can warrant confidence without an account of the mechanism. That confidence should match the tested task and conditions, with uncertainty about sampling and future change. Understanding can add evidence, but it is not a prerequisite for learning that a source predicts well.

Mechanistic understanding can help identify when performance will transfer to new conditions. Its absence does not cancel evidence from controlled prospective tests. Several mechanisms may explain the same success; uncertainty about which mechanism operates is different from uncertainty about whether predictions have been successful.

The balance between these perspectives might depend on several factors:

  1. Nature of Predictions: Are the predictions about well-bounded and well-understood phenomena, or do they extend into areas where our current theories and models are inadequate?
  2. Consequences of Errors: What are the potential consequences of acting on a false prediction? In high-stakes situations, we might demand a higher standard of understanding.
  3. Alternative Sources of Knowledge: Are there alternative, perhaps more understandable, sources that make similar predictions? If so, how do their track records compare?
  4. Opportunity for Validation and Falsification: Is there an opportunity to test and potentially falsify the entity’s predictions in a controlled manner, thereby gaining more insight into its reliability?

In summary, whether it is rational to place confidence in the future predictions of the entity, as much as its past success warrants, can depend on a nuanced assessment of these factors. While predictive success is a powerful indicator of reliability, a deeper understanding of the underlying mechanisms can significantly enhance trust and confidence, particularly in changing or unprecedented circumstances.

Gemini response · editorial edition

The question asks for confidence warranted by performance, not complete trust. Opacity alone does not prevent that proportional confidence:

Reasons to be cautious:

  • Black-Box Limitation: We may not know why the system succeeds or when it will fail outside its tested domain. But a sufficiently large, independently recorded test can distinguish sustained success from chance without opening the box.
  • Potential for Manipulation: Without understanding the mechanics, the entity could be feeding us tailored information to influence our behavior for its own unknown purposes.
  • Misinterpreting the Predictions: There’s a risk of misinterpreting the predictions. Are they absolute or probabilities? Do they have unintended consequences?

Reasons to be trusting:

  • Track Record: Prospective, specific predictions scored against all outcomes can support reliability. Variety helps test scope, but selective reporting, dependence and vague scoring can exaggerate apparent success.
  • No Alternative: If the entity provides crucial information about the future that we can’t get elsewhere, then trusting it might be the best option, even with reservations.

So, what’s the answer?

Here’s a balanced approach:

  • Conditional Trust: Trust the entity’s predictions provisionally, based on their past success.
  • Seek Understanding: Continue to research the entity’s workings. Perhaps with time, we can unravel its secrets and solidify our trust.
  • Verification and Backup Plans: Look for ways to verify the predictions independently. Also, have backup plans in case the predictions are wrong.

Separate Belief from Action: Risk tolerance affects how much to stake or whether to act, not what probability the evidence supports. The same probability can rationally lead different people to choose different bets.

This scenario is similar to the debate surrounding powerful artificial intelligence. We might get amazing results, but trusting something we don’t fully understand requires careful consideration.

Prompt 2: Let me make the scenario more precise. The black box entity has had 90% accurate on the correct throw of a fair die in a casino for 5 years. It’s your turn to bet. Do you place a 90% confidence in the black box entity based on its track record, or do you fall back on the transparent probability of 16.7% you’ll throw a 6 on your next roll? What is rational?

A fair die and an informative prediction

ChatGPT response · editorial edition

A fair die can have an unconditional probability of 1/6 for each face while an informative signal changes the probability conditional on that signal. Fairness does not imply independence from every possible source of information. We must also distinguish overall predictive accuracy from accuracy specifically when the box predicts a six.

From a purely rational standpoint, considering both the empirical track record and the theoretical basis for the probabilities involved, the decision could be approached from a few angles:

  1. Empirical Reliability: If the record is verified, sufficiently large and relevant to the next throw, it warrants substantial confidence in the predicted outcome. Under stable, symmetric performance across faces, 90% accuracy supports about 90% confidence in the indicated face; without that calibration, overall accuracy alone does not determine the exact number.
  2. Understanding the Basis of Prediction: Without understanding how the black box makes its predictions, there’s an element of risk, especially since its performance defies conventional understanding of randomness and fairness in dice rolls. The concern is whether the conditions under which the black box achieved its past accuracy would remain unchanged for the next roll. If its success is contingent on factors that are not stable or predictable, then its past performance may not be a reliable indicator of future success.
  3. Betting: Whether to bet depends on payouts, losses and resources. Those stakes affect the action threshold, not the evidential probability. A one-off bet does not make weaker evidence stronger.
  4. Independence: Rolls can be independent of one another while each roll remains correlated with the box’s signal. The evidence need not show a biased die; it may show that the signal contains information omitted from the unconditional model.

It is not equally rational to ignore applicable evidence merely because one prefers theory. Update the conditional forecast while keeping 1/6 as the unconditional probability if fairness remains supported. Investigate the mechanism without making ignorance of it a veto on the track record.

Gemini response · editorial edition

Under the intended assumptions of verified, stable and relevant performance, use the box’s information rather than defaulting to 1/6 after hearing its forecast. An exact 90% estimate additionally requires suitable calibration for that prediction.

  • Proven Track Record: The black box has consistently exceeded the expected outcome (16.7%) over a significant period (5 years), demonstrating an ability to predict beyond randomness.
  • Limited Scope: Testing the same well-defined task supports transfer to the next trial. A clear task does not guarantee absence of hidden variables; those could be precisely what makes prediction possible.

While the inner workings remain a mystery, the consistent and significant outperformance over a long period suggests a genuine capability.

Coin Analogy: Ninety correct advance forecasts out of one hundred fair-coin trials would support predictive information if the trials and scoring were sound. This is an illustrative dataset, not a simulation performed here, and finite observations leave uncertainty about future accuracy.

Of course, in more complex situations, trusting a black box without understanding its mechanisms might be riskier. But for a well-defined task with a proven track record of exceeding chance, trusting the black box is the rational choice in this scenario.

Prompt 3: For the last, more rigorous scenario, ChatGPT claims that for individuals who “prioritize theoretical foundations“, the stance that there is a 1/6 chance of throwing a 6 remains rational. Do you agree, and what is your rationale? Would the same rate of 90% success over 1,000 years make a difference?

The curator challenges theoretical immunity

ChatGPT response · editorial edition

The claim that for individuals who prioritize theoretical foundations, maintaining a 1/6 chance of throwing a 6 remains rational, indeed warrants further scrutiny, especially in light of the more extreme and precise scenario you’ve provided. Let’s dissect this from a philosophical and probabilistic standpoint.

Prioritizing Theoretical Foundations

  • Rationality and Evidence: The curator is right to challenge the earlier escape clause. Preference for theoretical foundations is not an evidential reason to ignore a verified signal. Bayesian reasoning conditions on all relevant information; it does not require abandoning the unconditional fairness model.
  • Independence: P(roll = 6) = 1/6 and P(roll = 6 | signal = 6) near 0.9 can both hold. The first averages across signals; the second uses additional information. Independent rolls need not be independent of their corresponding signals.

Impact of Extended Time Frame

  • A Longer Record: More relevant, independently informative trials can tighten estimates and strengthen evidence against chance prediction. Years alone supply no sample size, and dependence or changes in conditions matter. A thousand years does not prove that future conditions cannot change.

Conclusion

The correction is to update the forecast conditional on the signal, not necessarily to declare the die unfair. Mechanistic ignorance can limit extrapolation without erasing observed predictive information.

Understanding remains a worthwhile aim. It should guide further investigation while confidence follows the tested performance; a reliable opaque signal is not disqualified merely because an elegant but incomplete model omits it.

Gemini response · editorial edition

I should not defend the earlier answer by treating epistemic standards as a matter of personal preference. Under the stated evidential conditions, the signal must be taken into account.

The unconditional 1/6 remains appropriate before the signal, or if there is evidence that the signal is irrelevant here. It is not justified after an informative signal merely by valuing theoretical understanding.

What theoretical understanding can legitimately contribute:

  • Scope: A physical model may help explain whether performance will transfer to a changed throwing process. That is additional evidence about applicability, not a general exemption from updating.
  • Avoiding Bias: Relying solely on past data can lead to biases if the data isn’t perfectly representative. The black box might have exploited some unforeseen pattern in past throws, which wouldn’t necessarily hold true in the future.

The case for using the signal is epistemic as well as practical:

  • Exploiting an Advantage: If the goal is simply to win the bet, the black box offers a significant advantage over pure chance. Ignoring its success would be neglecting valuable information.
  • The data estimate a conditional relationship. There is no need to present practical prediction as a deviation from probability theory; conditioning is part of that theory.

Regarding the 1,000 year timeframe:

  • More informative trials strengthen evidence for reliability, provided recording, independence and relevance are adequate. Duration is not a substitute for those conditions.
  • Diminishing Returns: Further data can tighten uncertainty around an accuracy rate near 90%; they do not make each future prediction approach certainty.

The evidential judgment should therefore update. Personal priorities can change whether to bet or how much to invest in explanation, but they do not make ignoring the same reliable evidence equally well supported.

Bayesian calculations and the amount of evidence

ChatGPT response · editorial edition

The calculation requires the number of scored predictions, successes and a model of their dependence—not just years. Two calculations answer different questions: whether prediction beats chance, and what accuracy to expect next.

Bayesian Updating in Brief

Bayes’ theorem is P(H | E) = P(E | H)P(H)/P(E), for P(E) greater than zero. The original equation image is retained below.

where:

  • (P(H|E)) is the posterior probability of the hypothesis (H) given the evidence (E).
  • (P(E|H)) is the likelihood of observing the evidence (E) if the hypothesis (H) is true.
  • (P(H)) is the prior probability of the hypothesis (H) before observing the evidence.
  • (P(E)) is the probability of observing the evidence under all possible hypotheses.

Applying Bayesian Reasoning to the Black Box Scenario

  1. Prior: Let H1 be a stable predictor with accuracy 0.9 and H0 be guessing with accuracy 1/6. Assign prior odds between these hypotheses; 1/6 itself is not the prior probability that H1 is true.
  2. Data: Let n be the number of prospectively scored trials and k the correct predictions. Retain failures as well as successes; five years without a trial count is insufficient for a numerical update.
  3. Likelihood Ratio: Under independent trials with constant accuracy, BF = (0.9/(1/6))^k × (0.1/(5/6))^(n−k). Multiply prior odds by this Bayes factor to get posterior odds. This compares two specified models, not every possible explanation.
  4. Accuracy Estimate: To estimate an unknown stable accuracy p, an illustrative uniform Beta(1,1) prior gives Beta(k+1,n−k+1) after the data. The next-trial predictive probability of a correct forecast is (k+1)/(n+2). For 90 successes in 100 trials, it is 91/102, about 89.2%. The prior is illustrative, not uniquely rational.

Impact of a 1,000-Year Track Record

Longer Records: If k/n stays at 0.9 as independent n grows, evidence against chance prediction accumulates and uncertainty about p narrows near 0.9. That is increased confidence in the estimated reliability, not a prediction that the next forecast is almost certain to succeed.

Calculus Offered by Bayesian Probability

Bayesian probability calculus allows us to quantify the shift in belief about the black box’s predictive ability. It formalizes the intuition that as we accumulate more evidence (in this case, the extended track record of accuracy), our confidence in the hypothesis that the black box has predictive power should increase. This increased confidence is not merely a factor of time but of the compounded improbability of the evidence occurring under the null hypothesis of random chance.

For the next forecast of six specifically, use accuracy conditional on that forecast or justify a symmetric error model. Overall 90% accuracy can conceal different performance by predicted face. No single posterior number is fixed by the prompt’s duration and aggregate success rate alone.

Gemini response · editorial edition

You’re absolutely right. Probability theory coupled with Bayesian statistics provides a powerful framework to analyze this scenario. Here’s how it works:

1. Initial Belief (Prior Probability):

  • Separate Two Priors: The die’s unconditional 1/6 probability is not a prior probability that the predictor is reliable. We need a model relating the signal to the result, informed by the track record.

2. New Evidence (Likelihood):

  • The evidence is the recorded sequence of successes and failures; a likelihood is the probability of that evidence under a specified model. Neither selectively counting successes nor calling the evidence itself a likelihood is sufficient.

3. Posterior Probability:

  • A Concrete Symmetric Model: Suppose each face is equally likely, the box names the actual face with probability 0.9, and each wrong face with probability 0.02. Then P(signal = 6) = 1/6 and P(roll = 6 | signal = 6) = (0.9 × 1/6)/(1/6) = 0.9. This is fully compatible with a fair die.

The Strength of Evidence (Inductive Density):

As you mentioned, the more successful predictions (higher inductive density), the stronger the influence of the likelihood on the posterior probability. Here’s how this plays out in our scenario:

  • Five Years: Evaluate the trial count and the relevance of the testing conditions. A short period with many independent trials can contain more information than a long period with a few correlated observations.
  • One Thousand Years: More relevant observations may tighten the reliability estimate, but changes in conditions require modeling. Even perfect knowledge that accuracy is 0.9 leaves a 10% error probability for an exchangeable next prediction.

The Takeaway:

Bayesian updating formalizes the curator’s demand to let evidence affect confidence. It distinguishes confidence in a reliability estimate from the probability that a particular prediction is correct.

Additional Points:

  • This is a simplified example, and real-world scenarios might involve more complex calculations.
  • Some might argue that even with a very high posterior probability, there’s still a small chance the black box is wrong. This highlights the inherent uncertainty in some situations.

Overall, Bayesian statistics provides a powerful tool to analyze situations like this and make informed decisions based on evidence and prior beliefs.

Updating confidence versus choosing a bet

ChatGPT response · editorial edition

Yes, on the intended reading that the record establishes applicable reliability for this forecast, retaining 1/6 solely because the mechanism is opaque ignores relevant evidence. The correction is epistemic, not merely a strategy for winning bets.

Bayesian reasoning essentially tells us that rationality involves integrating new evidence with prior beliefs to form updated beliefs. If the evidence strongly contradicts prior expectations (in this case, the expectation based on the classical probability of dice rolls), then not updating those beliefs in light of the new evidence would conflict with the Bayesian conception of rationality.

To insist on the 16.7% chance without incorporating the empirical evidence of the black box’s predictive success would mean ignoring the significant epistemic weight of that evidence. Such a stance would be to disregard the principle that beliefs should be adjusted to reflect new information, especially when the information is robust and contradicts prior assumptions.

The qualification is about the evidence supplied: aggregate accuracy over a duration does not uniquely determine accuracy conditional on predicting six. If relevant calibration is established, use it. That qualification should not be used to resurrect the claim that a personal preference for theory makes disregarding reliable evidence rational.

Gemini response · editorial edition

From a purely Bayesian perspective, yes, sticking to the 16.7% chance in the face of overwhelming evidence from the black box would be considered irrational.

Here’s why:

  • Update on Relevant Evidence: A reliable signal changes the probability conditional on that signal. It does not contradict the unconditional probability of a fair die.
  • Long Records: Additional informative trials can make a reliability estimate more secure. A stable 90% accurate predictor still makes errors; reliability near 90% is not near-certainty on each prediction.

However, there are nuances to consider:

  • Understanding: Wanting an explanation is reasonable, but it does not supply a reason to assign 1/6 after receiving a signal known to be informative.

The earlier appeal to a principled preference for first principles was misplaced. Probability theory itself requires appropriate conditioning, and its assumptions must be assessed against evidence.

The key takeaway is:

  • The evidential case is to condition on the reliable signal. The practical decision then combines that probability with costs, benefits and alternatives.
  • Nuance: Investigate calibration, data quality and whether conditions have changed. These are evidential questions, not a license to ignore evidence because its source is mysterious.

Risk tolerance can determine the size of a bet; it does not determine the probability warranted by the same information.
