Prompt 1: Describe in detail the notion of properly basic beliefs.

Proper basicality

ChatGPT response · editorial edition

In foundationalist theories, a properly basic belief has appropriate non-inferential justification or warrant: it is not justified by inference from other beliefs. “Basic” describes how it is held; “properly” marks its positive epistemic status. It does not mean arbitrary, ungrounded, universally accepted, or necessarily infallible.

Characteristics of Properly Basic Beliefs

  1. Self-evidence: Some classical accounts admit self-evident beliefs as basic. External-world and other-minds beliefs are not straightforward examples of self-evident propositions; broader accounts may ground them non-inferentially in experience.
  2. Incorrigibility: Some classical accounts include beliefs thought immune to correction, especially restricted reports of present experience. Proper basicality in general does not require incorrigibility. Whether “I am happy” is immune to error is disputed.
  3. Sensory experience: A perceptual belief such as “there is a tree” can be non-inferentially grounded in experience on some theories. It remains open to defeat by evidence of illusion or unsuitable conditions.

The Role in Epistemology

The concept of properly basic beliefs plays a critical role in the structure of knowledge and justification. In foundationalist epistemology, they serve as the non-inferential foundation upon which all other knowledge is built. Without such a foundation, it is argued, knowledge would be impossible because it would lead to an infinite regress of justifications, where every belief must be justified by another, ad infinitum.

Criticisms and Discussions

Critics of the notion of properly basic beliefs argue that this concept allows for arbitrary beliefs to be considered foundational, leading to subjective and possibly conflicting foundations of knowledge. Moreover, what is considered “properly basic” can vary significantly between different epistemological traditions or even individuals.

Defenders distinguish a non-inferential ground from no ground at all. Plantinga’s account of warrant invokes proper cognitive function, a suitable environment, and a truth-directed design plan with appropriate reliability; merely feeling certain does not satisfy those conditions. See Warranted Christian Belief.

Conclusion

Properly basic beliefs are foundational to our epistemological frameworks, allowing for the construction of knowledge without the need for infinite justification. While the concept is not without its criticisms, it remains a central topic in the philosophy of knowledge, challenging and refining our understanding of what it means to know something.

Gemini response · editorial edition

In philosophy, especially epistemology (the study of knowledge), properly basic beliefs are the fundamental building blocks of our belief system. They are considered the starting point for justifying other beliefs. Here’s a breakdown of the key aspects:

What are they?

  • Foundational: These beliefs do not depend on inference from other beliefs for their positive epistemic standing. Mathematical axioms provide only a limited analogy, since axioms need not be self-evident or asserted as infallible truths.
  • Justified: These beliefs are not just random hunches. They are considered reasonable to hold based on some kind of justification.

Types of Justification (There’s some debate here):

  • Self-evident: Some philosophers believe certain propositions are so clear and obvious they don’t need proof. Like “I think, therefore I am” (René Descartes’ famous quote).
  • Sensory experience: Our basic sensory experiences (sight, touch, hearing) are another contender for proper justification. Seeing a red apple in front of you is a strong reason to believe there’s a red apple there. However, skeptics like Descartes point out our senses can be fooled (think mirages or dreams).
  • Non-doxastic justification: This means the justification comes from outside the realm of belief itself. For example, memory can play a role. If I vividly remember eating cake yesterday, that’s a good reason to believe I did.

Important points to consider:

  • Not infallible: Even properly basic beliefs can be mistaken. A memory could be false, or our senses deceived. The key is that they are justified at the time of belief.
  • Basic versus properly basic: A belief can be psychologically fundamental yet lack appropriate warrant, or face undefeated contrary evidence. Centrality to a person’s worldview is not sufficient.

Further Exploration:

  • Foundationalism is a philosophical theory that emphasizes properly basic beliefs as the foundation of knowledge https://1000wordphilosophy.com/2023/07/20/properly-basic-belief/.
  • There are debates about what qualifies as a properly basic belief, especially regarding the reliability of senses. Rene Descartes’ ideas on skepticism are a good starting point https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basic_belief.

Prompt 2: While ChatGPT claims properly basic beliefs are “incorrigible”, GEMINI claims they are “not infallible”. Attempt to reconcile these two claims.

Incorrigibility and fallibility

ChatGPT response · editorial edition

The original universal claim that properly basic beliefs are incorrigible was an overstatement. Different foundationalist theories impose different conditions; fallibilist versions allow properly basic beliefs that can be mistaken.

Incorrigibility

Incorrigibility concerns immunity to correction under a specified account; infallibility concerns impossibility of error. Strong first-person access does not by itself establish either property for every report about emotion.

Not Infallible

GEMINI’s assertion that properly basic beliefs are “not infallible” acknowledges that, despite being foundational and not requiring external justification, these beliefs can still be mistaken. This is especially relevant when considering beliefs that are evident to the senses or based on memory. While these beliefs are justified in being considered basic or foundational due to their immediate and direct nature, the possibility of error remains. Sensory deception or false memories are examples where our cognitive faculties might lead us astray.

Reconciliation

  1. Scope: Some theories reserve a privileged status for narrow present-experience reports, while allowing fallible perceptual or memory beliefs. That theoretical distinction cannot turn every self-report into an unquestionable truth.
  2. Proper basicality describes non-inferential epistemic standing, not a uniform degree of certainty. Whether a particular belief is incorrigible or infallible is a further question.
  3. Philosophical Rigor: This reconciliation underscores a critical philosophical practice: applying rigorous scrutiny to our beliefs. While some beliefs are foundational and form the bedrock of our worldview, acknowledging their potential fallibility invites continuous reflection and adaptation of our knowledge framework.

Thus the replies should be reconciled by correcting the first overgeneralization, not by saying that all properly basic beliefs are both uncorrectable and potentially corrected.

Gemini response · editorial edition

The disagreement is substantive: the first reply overgeneralized incorrigibility. Proper basicality does not generally imply infallibility or immunity to defeat.

  • Incorrigibility vs. Not Infallible:
    • Incorrigible: Immune to correction in the relevant sense. A person’s privileged access to pain is a proposed example in some accounts, not a proof that every introspective description is beyond error.
    • Not Infallible: This means a belief, even a properly basic one, can still be wrong. I highlight this aspect. Our senses can be fooled, memories can be faulty, and even seemingly self-evident truths might be challenged with new information.

Finding Common Ground:

Both claims acknowledge that properly basic beliefs hold a special place in our knowledge system. They are:

  • Foundational: They serve as the starting point for justifying other beliefs.
  • Justified at the time of belief: There’s a reason we hold them, even if it might not be perfect.

The Debate:

The key difference lies in how strongly we view their resistance to correction.

  • A belief that was justified but later corrected illustrates defeasible justification, not incorrigibility. The earlier reply conflated these concepts.
  • Not infallibility proponents emphasize the possibility of error, even with properly basic beliefs.

Conclusion:

Both replies concern non-inferential belief, but the corrected account must distinguish basicality, justification, incorrigibility and infallibility.

Prompt 4: How can a belief in a God be directly accessible in the way an emotion is directly accessible? Provide a clear argument including its syllogistic form if possible.

Direct access and argument forms

ChatGPT response · editorial edition

The argument that a belief in God can be directly accessible, similar to the direct accessibility of an emotion, stems from certain strands of Reformed epistemology, particularly the work of philosopher Alvin Plantinga. Plantinga suggests that belief in God can be “properly basic,” meaning it does not necessarily require evidential support from other beliefs to be rational or warranted. Instead, it can be directly instilled or triggered by experiences, practices, or the workings of what he terms the “sensus divinitatis” (a sense of the divine), which he argues is a cognitive faculty given by God for perceiving God’s presence.

Argument in Conceptual Form

  1. Premise 1: If a belief is produced non-inferentially by a suitably reliable, properly functioning faculty under the relevant conditions, it can have non-inferential warrant within this theory.
  2. Premise 2: Suppose there is a sensus divinitatis satisfying those conditions and producing belief in God. This is the disputed substantive premise, not an established empirical fact.
  3. Premise 3: Suppose the particular belief is produced in those conditions and is not defeated by relevant contrary considerations.
  4. Conclusion: Under those assumptions, the belief can have non-inferential warrant. The conditional model does not independently prove that God or the faculty exists.

Syllogistic Form

To put the argument into a syllogistic form:

  1. Major premise: If the relevant warrant-producing conditions obtain for belief B, then B can be warranted without inference from other beliefs.
  2. Minor premise: The relevant conditions obtain for this belief in God. This premise requires defense; it is not established by the believer’s feeling of direct access.
  3. Conclusion: This belief in God can be warranted non-inferentially. The form is modus ponens; its soundness depends on the premises.

Explanation

The argument hinges on the notion that just as we have direct access to our emotions or sensory experiences without the need for inferential reasoning, so too can we have direct access to a belief in God through an innate cognitive faculty. This faculty, when triggered by certain experiences, conditions, or practices (such as prayer, natural beauty, moral experience, etc.), allows an individual to “sense” or “perceive” God’s presence or reality in a direct and non-inferential manner.

The proposal concerns non-inferential warrant, not incorrigibility. It is also different from an argument that a feeling of contact deductively proves a divine cause.

This approach has sparked considerable debate within epistemology and philosophy of religion, particularly around the existence and nature of the sensus divinitatis, the conditions under which it operates, and the implications for religious pluralism and disagreement.

Gemini response · editorial edition

Here’s the challenge with directly equating a belief in God with an emotion like happiness:

  • Emotions are experienced internally. Knowing what an experience feels like and accurately classifying its character are related but distinguishable, and not every emotional self-description is infallible.
  • A belief is itself a mental state, while its content can concern an external or metaphysical reality. Feeling that God exists and God’s actually existing are different propositions.

Arguments for Direct Access (Analogy):

While a belief in God might not be directly accessible like an emotion, some argue for a kind of direct experience:

  • Religious experiences: Some people report profound religious experiences that feel like a direct encounter with the divine. These experiences can be very convincing and lead to a strong belief in God.

Syllogistic Form (Possible, but Weak):

Valid conditional reconstruction: If an experience reliably presents God under warrant-conferring conditions, it can provide non-inferential warrant; this experience satisfies those conditions; therefore it can provide such warrant. The second premise is disputed. The original inference from “feels like an encounter” and “real encounters cause belief” was invalid: it did not establish a real encounter.

Why it’s Weak:

  • Subjectivity: Religious experiences are subjective and vary greatly. What one person interprets as divine, another might explain differently.
  • Alternative explanations: These experiences could be caused by psychological factors or powerful emotions, not necessarily God.

Better Analogy:

A more common way to view strong religious beliefs is similar to how we hold deep moral convictions. We might not be able to objectively prove them, but they feel true and guide our actions.

The takeaway:

Belief in God can be a powerful and central aspect of a person’s life, but it’s different from directly experiencing an emotion. The justification for such beliefs comes from a combination of factors, including reason, tradition, and personal experiences.

Prompt 5: But the feeling that someone has direct access to God logically substantiates only the feeling that they have direct access to God, and does not substantiate direct access to God, right?

Feeling versus external reality

ChatGPT response · editorial edition

Yes, you’ve highlighted a critical distinction in the discussion about the direct accessibility of belief in God compared to the direct experience of one’s emotions. When someone claims to have direct access to God or feels that they are directly aware of God’s presence, this indeed substantiates the feeling itself but does not necessarily substantiate the objective truth of having direct access to God.

The distinction lies between the subjective experience and the objective reality it purports to represent:

  1. Subjective Experience: The feeling or belief that one has direct access to God is a subjective experience. It’s real to the person having the experience and can have profound implications for their faith, behavior, and worldview. This subjective aspect is analogous to experiencing an emotion; just as feeling happy is a subjective state known directly to the person, so too is the feeling of being connected to or aware of God.
  2. The experience alone does not deductively entail its proposed divine source. Whether it provides defeasible evidence depends on its reliability, background information, and competing explanations; lack of entailment is not the same as zero evidential value.

Philosophical Implications

The distinction you’ve drawn is essential in epistemology and the philosophy of religion because it touches on the nature of justification and evidence. While subjective experiences can be compelling to the individual, they do not automatically serve as universal proof or justification for a claim in the eyes of others. For example:

  • Personal Assurance: A person’s direct feeling of access to God can be personally assuring and foundational for their faith.
  • Epistemic Status: The subjective experience of feeling that one has direct access to God has an epistemic status that is internal to the individual’s cognitive framework. It might justify belief for the individual, but it doesn’t necessarily extend beyond the individual’s subjective experience to serve as an objective justification for the belief in God.

Conclusion

The feeling supports the claim that the person has that experience, but does not logically guarantee divine contact. Its possible evidential significance is a further, disputed question.

Gemini response · editorial edition

Correct about entailment: the existence of the feeling does not by itself logically guarantee the existence of its proposed divine object.

  • Subjectivity of Feelings: Feelings are subjective and personal. Just because something feels real to you doesn’t necessarily mean it reflects objective reality. For example, someone might feel invincible but still get hurt.
  • Alternative Explanations: There could be other explanations for the feeling of direct access. Powerful emotions, meditation, or even certain neurological states can create intense experiences that feel like a connection to something beyond ourselves.

Logical Argument:

We can express this point in a syllogism:

  1. The feeling of direct access to God is a subjective experience.
  2. Subjective experiences alone cannot definitively prove the existence of an external reality.
  3. Therefore, the feeling alone does not deductively prove direct access to God. This conclusion leaves open the separate question of defeasible evidence.

However, it’s Important to Note:

  • The feeling can be a powerful motivator for faith and religious practice.
  • It can be a meaningful experience that strengthens a person’s belief system.
  • Different religions may have different interpretations of such feelings.

In Conclusion:

The feeling of direct access to God is a complex phenomenon. While it doesn’t logically prove God’s existence, it can be a significant aspect of a person’s spiritual life.

Prompt 6: To confirm, it is epistemologically illegitimate to jump from “I have a feeling God exists” to “God exists”, correct?

The inferential gap

ChatGPT response · editorial edition

As a deduction from that feeling alone, yes: the conclusion does not follow. As a claim about every possible non-inferential justification, the answer is disputed; that broader question cannot be settled simply by identifying the gap in entailment.

Understanding the Epistemological Gap

  1. Subjective Justification: The statement “I have a feeling God exists” is grounded in personal, subjective experience. It’s a reflection of an individual’s internal state or perception, which can be deeply convincing and meaningful to that person. This subjective experience may serve as a personal justification for their belief in God.
  2. “God exists” concerns reality beyond the feeling. Publicly establishing it requires reasons an audience can assess. Whether an individual can have warranted belief without possessing a public argument is precisely what non-inferential theories dispute.

Epistemological Standards

For a public argument, relevant support might include the following. These are possible forms of support, not a universally accepted requirement that every warranted belief be publicly demonstrated:

  • Empirical Evidence: Observable, measurable evidence that can be independently verified.
  • Logical Reasoning: Arguments that use logic to connect premises to a conclusion, where the premises provide strong support for the conclusion.
  • Consensus or Corroboration: In many cases, especially in scientific inquiry, consensus among experts or corroboration by independent observations or experiments strengthens the epistemological standing of a claim.

Philosophical Considerations

It’s important to note that while the leap from personal feeling to objective claim is epistemologically problematic without additional justification, philosophical and theological contexts often engage with these questions in more nuanced ways. For instance, discussions about the nature of faith, religious experience, and the role of personal revelation or experience in religious belief acknowledge the complexity and personal dimension of these experiences, while also grappling with the challenge of communicating and justifying these beliefs in a broader epistemological context.

Conclusion

In summary, while personal feelings or experiences can be profound and formative for individual belief systems, making a direct leap from a personal feeling to an assertion of objective truth requires additional steps of justification to meet epistemological standards. This distinction is crucial in the rigorous examination of beliefs and claims about reality.

Gemini response · editorial edition

The inference is invalid if it treats a feeling as logically guaranteeing God’s existence. That does not establish a consensus that every experiential or non-inferential route to warrant is illegitimate.

  • Feelings are not guarantees of external truth. Some experiences can nevertheless be evidence, so the blanket statement “feelings are not evidence” is too strong.
  • A claim about warrant needs an account of why the experience is truth-conducive and how possible defeaters are handled. Merely describing the feeling as intense does not supply that account.

There are, however, some nuances to consider:

  • Religious experiences: Some argue that powerful religious experiences can be a source of justification for belief in God. However, these experiences are subjective and open to alternative explanations.
  • Faith: In some religious traditions, faith is seen as a virtue, and belief in God doesn’t necessarily require justification through reason or evidence.

Conclusion:

The secure conclusion is the gap in logical entailment. The epistemic force of an experience remains a separate question that competing theories answer differently.

Reasons and public defense

ChatGPT response · editorial edition

The relationship between properly basic beliefs and the obligation to provide evidence and argumentation is nuanced within the framework of epistemology, particularly concerning beliefs about God.

Properly Basic Beliefs

The concept of a belief being “properly basic” is primarily discussed within foundationalist epistemology, which suggests that certain beliefs serve as the foundation for other beliefs and do not themselves require justification in the way that non-basic beliefs do. The idea, especially as developed by philosophers like Alvin Plantinga, is that certain beliefs (including belief in God, for some) can be rational and justified even without being supported by argumentation or evidence in the traditional sense.

Evidence and Argumentation

  1. Context-Specific Rationality: Within the framework that accepts properly basic beliefs, the need for external evidence or argumentation to support these beliefs might not be seen as necessary for their rationality or justification. For instance, Plantinga argues that belief in God can be considered rational without inferential evidence if it arises from a properly functioning cognitive faculty in the appropriate circumstances.
  2. Dialogue and Persuasion: However, when engaging in dialogue with others who do not share the same foundational beliefs, or in a broader intellectual context that demands justification for claims, proponents of properly basic beliefs might still find it necessary or beneficial to provide evidence and argumentation. This is not because their belief lacks rationality in their own framework, but because argumentation and evidence are part of the shared language of public discourse and critical dialogue.
  3. The Role of Evidence and Argument in Faith: Many religious traditions value the role of evidence and reasoned argument in supporting faith. Even if a belief is held to be properly basic for the believer, engaging with evidence and arguments can strengthen faith, address doubts, and facilitate meaningful conversations with others.

Philosophical Debate

The question of whether properly basic beliefs, including belief in God, require external justification is a matter of ongoing philosophical debate. Critics argue that without such justification, there’s a risk of arbitrarily designating certain beliefs as basic, which could lead to epistemological relativism or subjectivism. Proponents, however, maintain that this approach is necessary to avoid an infinite regress of justifications and to acknowledge the foundational role of certain experiences or cognitive faculties in forming our belief systems.

Conclusion

In summary, while the notion of properly basic beliefs might provide a framework within which certain beliefs (like belief in God) can be considered rational without traditional forms of evidence or argumentation, the engagement with evidence and reasoned argument remains valuable and often necessary for dialogue, personal reflection, and the broader intellectual defense of one’s beliefs.

Traditional View of Properly Basic Beliefs:

Prompt 8: How can a belief in God avoid an epistemic dependency on evidence and argumentation outside one’s mind to substantiate that God?

Non-inferential warrant

ChatGPT response · editorial edition

The position that a belief in God can avoid epistemic dependency on external evidence and argumentation while remaining substantiated is central to certain strands of Reformed epistemology, particularly the views proposed by philosopher Alvin Plantinga. Plantinga argues that belief in God can be “properly basic” and does not necessarily require inferential justification from evidence or argumentation. Here’s an outline of how this can be philosophically defended:

Properly Basic Beliefs and the Sensus Divinitatis

  1. Cognitive Faculty for Perception of God: Plantinga introduces the concept of the “sensus divinitatis,” a natural cognitive faculty that, when functioning properly, provides individuals with a direct awareness of God’s existence. This faculty enables a person to perceive God in various contexts (e.g., in response to the beauty of nature, moral experiences, or the vastness of the universe), leading to a belief in God that is immediate and non-inferential.
  2. Circularity: Positing a faculty is not by itself a proof of its existence or reliability. A conditional account of how belief could be warranted avoids requiring an inference by the believer; it does not automatically settle a critic’s request for independent support.

Epistemological Framework

  1. Foundationalism: In a foundationalist epistemology, not all beliefs require evidence from other beliefs to be rational. Some beliefs (properly basic beliefs) serve as the foundation for further beliefs. The belief in God, according to Plantinga, falls into this category because it arises from a fundamental aspect of human cognitive structure—the sensus divinitatis—and hence does not depend on external argumentation or evidence for its justification.
  2. The Role of External Evidence and Argumentation: While the initial belief in God may not require external evidence, engagement with evidence and arguments can still play a role in the reflective life of a believer. This engagement can provide further support for the belief, address doubts, and contribute to a deeper understanding of one’s faith.

Challenges and Criticisms

  1. Subjectivity and Variability: Critics argue that the sensus divinitatis concept allows for a high degree of subjectivity and variability among individuals’ experiences. What one person attributes to the sensus divinitatis, another might attribute to psychological or cultural factors.
  2. Pluralism and Conflicting Beliefs: The existence of diverse and often conflicting religious beliefs raises questions about the reliability of the sensus divinitatis. Critics question whether this faculty can lead to true beliefs if different individuals, presumably with the same cognitive faculty, arrive at different conceptions of God or the divine.

Conclusion

The proposal separates having non-inferential warrant from being able to demonstrate that warrant to others. It is a conditional account, not a proof of God obtained by declaring a belief self-evident.

  1. Internal Religious Experiences:
  • Some argue that powerful religious experiences can be a source of justification for belief in God. These experiences can be very personal and convincing, leading individuals to feel a direct connection with the divine.
  • Challenges: These experiences are subjective and vary greatly. What one person interprets as divine, another might explain differently. Additionally, alternative explanations like psychological factors or intense emotions can exist.
