Feasibility of changing the vocabulary

ChatGPT response · editorial edition

Feasibility of Challenging the Notion of a Moral Realm with Moral Anti-Realism

Challenging Social Inertia: The proposal is feasible as a deliberate way of speaking in a community of inquiry; eliminating “moral” from public discourse is a much larger and uncertain aim. The word compresses praise, blame, identity, personal guidance and institutional expectations. Some users assert objective authority; others do not. Resistance may therefore reflect different meanings and practical habits as well as commitment to moral realism.

Promoting Moral Anti-Realism:

  1. Educational Initiatives: Teach the distinctions the proposal needs: what a person feels, what they value, what a group accepts and what supports a recommendation. Ask students to translate a disputed judgment into those terms and identify anything they think is lost. This tests the usefulness of the vocabulary without presenting anti-realism as a conclusion already established by the exercise.
  2. Public Discourse: Demonstrate the method on specific disagreements. Replace “That is immoral” with a statement of the harm, the priorities involved and the proposed change. Then answer objections about facts and values. Clear examples are more informative than a campaign that merely bans a familiar word.
  3. Scientific and Philosophical Research: Research into the causes of intuitions can inform an account of how judgments arise and change. It does not, by itself, decide whether any moral claims are true: causal explanations and philosophical justification answer different questions. The anti-realist still needs arguments against the proposed grounding, just as the realist cannot establish that grounding merely by pointing to a strong feeling.

Replacing “Moral” with “Emotional” or “Socially Acceptable”: Use the replacements precisely. “Emotional” describes a response; “value” names a priority; “socially acceptable” describes approval within an identified group. None alone expresses every recommendation. For advocacy, state the values and consequences that support it. This retains the curator’s rejection of an added realm without turning social acceptance into a hidden substitute for justification.

Potential Challenges:

  1. Resistance to Change: People may rely on familiar language for personal guidance as well as influence over others. The curator’s closing note on the linked essay explicitly softened the earlier emphasis on social control by recognizing this roadmap role. A persuasive reformulation should explain how people can retain guidance and commitments while examining their grounding.
  2. Misunderstanding and Misrepresentation: Listeners may hear “no moral facts” as “nothing matters” or “anything is permitted.” Show why those inferences do not follow from the curator’s position: emotions, values, harms, agreements and opposition remain. Conversely, the replacement should not be advertised as neutral if particular values are being advocated.
  3. Integration with Existing Norms: Examine practical functions one at a time—stating rules, giving reasons, coordinating conduct and criticizing abuse. Ask whether the new wording communicates each function clearly and what commitments or institutions sustain it. Requiring it to retain an unexplained “normative force” would simply reinstate the notion the curator has challenged.

Conclusion: A limited, demonstrable change in discussion is more plausible than predicting wholesale replacement. Try the vocabulary, check whether participants can identify the actual disagreements, and revise it where it blurs distinctions. Its success would show that some discussions can proceed more clearly without moral terminology; it would not alone prove anti-realism or guarantee wider adoption.

 #1: What is the central question addressed in Yudkowsky’s essay “The Meaning of Right”?

Answer: Yudkowsky addresses the question of what morality is and what the word “should” means.

 #2: How does Yudkowsky propose to investigate how the brain labels things as “right”?

Answer: By starting out wearing morality-goggles and considering morality-as-morality, then transitioning to reduction-goggles and discussing cognitive algorithms.

 #3: What analogy does Yudkowsky use to explain the backward flow of should-ness?

Answer:He traces the reason to act backward from saving the child through the actions needed to achieve it. This is reasoning about consequences, not a physical influence traveling backward in time.

 #4: What is Stilwell’s main argument against the concept of morality?

Answer: Stilwell argues that moral intuitions are actually emotional intuitions and their emergent values, and that the use of the term moral is superfluous.

 #5: According to Stilwell, why is the term “immoral” powerful in social dialogue?

Answer: Because it can psychologically control others and establish the speaker as “moral” or “good”.

#6: How does the revised fictional exchange address Stilwell’s objection to normative force?

Answer:It separates psychological influence, social enforcement and recommendations based on stated commitments. It rejects the shortcut of equating pressure with justification; the fictional accommodation is not a report of Yudkowsky conceding anti-realism.

 #7: What term does Stilwell suggest using instead of “moral landscape”?

Answer: Stilwell suggests using the term “landscape of socially acceptable behaviors”.

#8: Why does the fictional interlocutor initially resist simply dropping the word “moral”?

Answer:He asks whether replacement language preserves distinctions among impulses, lasting commitments, shared rules and criticism. Stilwell replies by making those elements explicit without treating the label as an argument.

 #9: How does Stilwell view the relationship between moral systems and human emotions?

Answer: Stilwell views moral systems as mappings of human emotions that use terminology to give common emotions greater obligatory force.

 #10: What is Yudkowsky’s stance on the objectivity of morality?

Answer:His essay holds the evaluative standard fixed across imagined changes in a person’s desires. It does not equate rightness with whatever a person or society currently approves.

 #11: According to Yudkowsky, what is the role of cognitive algorithms in moral decision-making?

Answer: Cognitive algorithms guide human behavior and decision-making by labeling certain actions as “right” or “should”.

 #12: What does Stilwell suggest about the legitimacy of moral systems?

Answer:Stilwell rejects the claimed grounding of moral systems and challenges the authority their language appears to add to emotions and values. This does not mean that rules, practical guidance or opposition to harm cease to exist.

#13: What disagreement remains after the fictional speakers adopt more explicit terminology?

Answer:Whether the account of human evaluation justifies retaining claims of moral truth or authority. Agreement to discuss emotions, values and social practices does not settle that question.

#14: What role does the idea of a “gift” play in the passage discussed from “The Meaning of Right”?

Answer:It expresses the possibility of continuing to cherish human concerns while revising beliefs about their origin. It is not a rule to approve everything produced by evolution.

#15: What limit does the revised discussion place on using coherence to resolve a conflict between values?

Answer:Showing that two commitments conflict does not alone determine which to abandon. Further deliberation must state the concerns and reasons doing the work.
