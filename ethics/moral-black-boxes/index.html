1Original prompt · edited responses

Prompt 1: Rigorously assess the following argument.

The original argument: validity and contested premises

P1: If you cannot question the moral goodness of a deity, you cannot rationally claim that deity is the legitimate arbiter of morality.
P2: If there is no imaginable action of Deity X that would constitute evidence against the moral goodness of Deity X, you then cannot question the moral goodness of Deity X.
P3: There is no imaginable action of Deity X that would constitute evidence against the moral goodness of Deity X.
Conclusion: You cannot rational claim that Deity X is the legitimate arbiter of morality.

ChatGPT response · editorial edition

The inference is valid on a consistent reading of its terms: the conclusion must follow if the three premises are true. That does not yet make the argument sound, which would require true premises as well. The main work is to explain what “cannot question” means and defend the links supplied by P1 and P2.

Premise 1 (P1): If you cannot question the moral goodness of a deity, you cannot rationally claim that deity is the legitimate arbiter of morality.

  • Assessment: P1 states a standard for justified moral authority: a claim to such authority must be open to meaningful assessment. This expresses the curator’s concern about immunity from scrutiny. It needs defense, however. Being forbidden to ask questions, being unable to understand every reason, and lacking any way to assess a claim are different conditions. A prohibition imposed by a community does not itself erase evidence someone already has.

Premise 2 (P2): If there is no imaginable action of Deity X that would constitute evidence against the moral goodness of Deity X, you then cannot question the moral goodness of Deity X.

  • Assessment: P2 supplies a further premise; it does not follow from P1. It is plausible if “question” means testing goodness against possible action-based counterevidence. It is too broad if it means any rational examination at all: one could still challenge a definition, an inference, or the reliability of a purported revelation. The argument must explain why those routes fail or restrict the conclusion to the action-based route.

Premise 3 (P3): There is no imaginable action of Deity X that would constitute evidence against the moral goodness of Deity X.

  • Assessment: P3 can describe a believer’s rule for handling evidence, a definition of “Deity X,” or a claim that a necessarily good being cannot act badly. Those readings are not equivalent. Someone may say a cruel being would not qualify as God while still allowing cruelty to count against identifying a particular alleged speaker or actor as God. The curator’s target is clearest when every possible action of the very same alleged authority is protected from adverse assessment.

Conclusion: You cannot rationally claim that Deity X is the legitimate arbiter of morality.

  • Assessment: The stated conclusion follows once the premises are accepted with those meanings held fixed. It concerns whether a person can rationally claim moral authority for X. It neither establishes that X is evil nor proves that X does not exist.

Logical Structure:

  1. Let Q mean “we can meaningfully question X’s goodness,” A mean “we can rationally claim X is the legitimate moral arbiter,” and E mean “some imaginable action could count against X’s goodness.” P1 is ¬Q → ¬A.
  2. P2 is ¬E → ¬Q, and P3 is ¬E. Applying P2 to P3 gives ¬Q.
  3. Applying P1 to ¬Q gives ¬A. Each step uses the ordinary rule that if “if p then q” and p hold, q follows. The conclusion therefore follows without assuming that X actually performed a bad act.

Conclusion of Assessment: The argument validly exposes the cost of accepting all three premises. Its soundness requires defending P1 and P2 as well as establishing the relevant reading of P3. A useful test is whether the alleged evidence can distinguish a good authority from an authority whose conduct is excused regardless of what it does. Hidden motives alone do not settle that test.

Claude response · editorial edition

The argument is valid if “cannot question” has one meaning throughout. Its soundness remains open because all three premises, not just P3, carry substantive commitments.

P1: If you cannot question the moral goodness of a deity, you cannot rationally claim that deity is the legitimate arbiter of morality.

P1 connects rational authority with evaluability. An authority that forbids criticism may still be assessable from evidence; an authority whose goodness has been defined beyond all assessment presents a deeper problem. Use the second meaning if the argument is to address the curator’s moral black box.

P2: If there is no imaginable action of Deity X that would constitute evidence against the moral goodness of Deity X, you then cannot question the moral goodness of Deity X.

P2 is contestable. A mathematical truth need not have an observational counterexample to be rationally examined; similarly, a defender could offer conceptual grounds for necessary goodness. Whether those grounds succeed and identify the relevant deity is a further question. Excluding action-based counterevidence therefore needs an additional argument before it excludes every form of inquiry.

P3: There is no imaginable action of Deity X that would constitute evidence against the moral goodness of Deity X.

P3 is best treated here as the immunity claim under examination, not an independently established fact about a deity. Ask its proponent whether apparently cruel conduct could undermine a claimed revelation, an interpretation, or the identity of the speaker. If so, some relevant assessment remains possible.

Conclusion: You cannot rationally claim that Deity X is the legitimate arbiter of morality.

If premises P1, P2, and P3 are true, then the conclusion logically follows. If we cannot question the moral goodness of Deity X (P2 and P3), then by P1, we cannot rationally claim that Deity X is the legitimate arbiter of morality.

The deductive steps work: P3 together with P2 yields inability to question, and P1 yields inability rationally to claim authority. A critic can accept that structure while disputing a premise. Describing a premise as “logically sound” would obscure this difference between an inference’s form and a premise’s truth.

A defender might offer independent reasons for trusting X: an argument about necessary goodness, testimony, or a purported revelation. Those reasons must themselves be assessed. If their reliability is justified only by the goodness they are meant to establish, the defense risks circularity; if they are independent, the critic must address them rather than rule them out by definition.

The strongest application is conditional: if the defender excludes every adverse moral assessment while relying on comparable favorable assessments, ask what justifies the asymmetry. This preserves the force of the curator’s argument without declaring every belief in divine goodness irrational solely because it lacks a simple empirical falsifier.

Prompt 2: Therefore, anyone claiming that there can be no evidence submitted against the moral goodness of Deity X must also conclude they have no access to the moral status of Deity X. Correct?

What follows about access to moral status?

ChatGPT response · editorial edition

Correct on a stronger interpretation of “no access,” but not as an unrestricted deduction from the original conclusion. The original argument denies a rational claim to X’s moral authority. To conclude that the person has no justified access to any aspect of X’s moral status requires further assumptions. The three premises give the following route:

  1. Questioning the moral goodness of a deity is necessary to rationally claim that the deity is a legitimate arbiter of morality (as suggested in P1).
  2. The ability to question the moral goodness of a deity is contingent upon the possibility of imagining actions that would count as evidence against their moral goodness (as suggested in P2).
  3. No imaginable action of Deity X can be considered as evidence against the moral goodness of Deity X (as stated in P3).

Together, these premises yield the denial of a rational claim to X’s authority. If “no access” means no action-based procedure for distinguishing goodness from its absence, P2 and P3 support that narrower diagnosis. If it means no possible moral knowledge of X by any route, an additional premise must exclude independent grounds. The curator’s challenge remains: someone who rejects every adverse judgment as beyond human competence owes an explanation of how favorable judgments escape the same restriction.

Claude response · editorial edition

The extension needs a qualification. Accepting P1–P3 commits someone to the stated conclusion about rationally claiming moral authority. It does not, by formal logic alone, yield the broader claim that they have no access whatsoever to X’s moral status.

The crux of the argument rests on premises P2 and P3:

P2: If there is no imaginable action of Deity X that would constitute evidence against the moral goodness of Deity X, you then cannot question the moral goodness of Deity X.

P3: There is no imaginable action of Deity X that would constitute evidence against the moral goodness of Deity X.

On the action-based reading of P2, P3 blocks that way of testing goodness. It does not automatically rule out questioning the source’s identity, the concept of goodness, or an argument offered independently of actions.

And by P1: If you cannot question the moral goodness of a deity, you cannot rationally claim that deity is the legitimate arbiter of morality.

The conclusion is therefore conditional on P1 and P2 as well as P3. A person who accepts only P3 can dispute the connecting premises; calling them logically forced to accept the conclusion without those premises would overstate the argument.

Nevertheless, “we cannot judge” cannot do selective work without explanation. If the same human capacities identify kindness as evidence for goodness but are disqualified whenever cruelty appears, the policy protects the conclusion rather than fairly assesses it. To claim broader moral access, the defender must specify a route that remains credible under their own restrictions.

3Original prompt · edited responses

Prompt 3: I have seen theists of different sorts attempt to make their God off-limits to questions of moral goodness by claiming their God cannot be morally judged by humans. Comment on any logical flaws in this in light of our discussion above.

When moral immunity undermines moral praise

ChatGPT response · editorial edition

The claim deserves scrutiny, especially when it is used to admit moral praise while excluding criticism. Its exact force depends on whether it expresses limited understanding or complete inability to assess divine goodness. Those positions have different consequences:

  1. Implications for Rational Inquiry: Not understanding every reason for an action does not entail having no relevant evidence. A person may rationally remain uncertain. The stronger claim that no possible conduct could reduce confidence needs separate support; it should not be smuggled in through the uncontroversial fact that human knowledge is limited.
  2. Tension with Premise 1: If the defender accepts P1 while denying every form of moral evaluation, the claim of rationally established authority conflicts with that premise. A defender who rejects P1 has a philosophical disagreement to answer, not an immediate formal contradiction. The issue is whether their alternative standard justifies authority rather than merely asserts it.
  3. Issue of Moral Relevance: Even a clear command leaves two questions: did X issue it, and why should X’s instruction have moral authority? Complete opacity about goodness makes the second difficult to answer. Mere power or authorship does not, without another normative premise, establish a duty to obey.
  4. Autonomy and Moral Agency: A person can delegate decisions for reasons they understand without grasping every detail. The problem is unconditional delegation that also disables checks on deception, misinterpretation, or harm. Ask what could count against the alleged authority or its spokesperson before surrendering those checks.
  5. Logical Coherence: “We cannot fully understand X” and “we have some reason to trust X” can coexist. “We have no basis for any favorable or unfavorable assessment” conflicts with claiming an established favorable assessment on that same basis. The selective use of mystery must be exposed at that specific point.

The most precise criticism is thus of an evidential double standard. If suffering can never count against goodness because purposes are hidden, why does apparent beneficence count for it without equivalent scrutiny? A defender may propose an asymmetry, but must justify it. The mere possibility of a hidden good reason does not establish that such a reason exists in this case.

Claude response · editorial edition

There is a serious tension when “God cannot be judged by humans” is used to block every unfavorable verdict while “God is morally good” remains a humanly justified assertion. The tension needs to be stated carefully rather than declared an inherent flaw in every theistic position.

A total ban on relevant adverse evidence resembles P3. A narrower admission that humans lack complete information does not. Treating these as interchangeable would make the criticism easier but less accurate.

If the defender accepts P1 and P2 as well as that total ban, the original conclusion follows. If they reject a connecting premise, the discussion must assess their reasons for doing so.

An illustrative comparison is a ruler whose kind acts count as evidence of goodness while every cruel act is excused by unknowable reasons. The comparison targets a rule for using evidence; it does not assume that a human ruler and a deity have identical knowledge, powers, or duties.

The double standard appears when the same kind of moral judgment is trusted in one direction and dismissed in the other without an independent reason. Calling that “having it both ways” is justified only after the difference in treatment has been shown, not merely because the defender mentions mystery.

Defining “good” as whatever X commands may avoid a contradiction, but it changes the question. It does not by itself explain why X’s commands deserve allegiance, why X exists, or why a particular message comes from X. Those claims still need reasons.

A successful defense must make its claimed grounds of moral trust intelligible enough to assess. The curator’s challenge is not a demand for omniscience; it is a demand that a declaration of goodness do more work than protecting an authority from every possible objection.

Prompt 4: Create an interesting and clear hypothetical dialogue between a theist and a non-theist that saliently highlights the argument above.

Two dialogues testing the asymmetry

ChatGPT response · editorial edition

Here’s a hypothetical dialogue that highlights the argument discussed above. The conversation occurs between Theo, a theist who believes that God’s actions and nature are beyond human moral judgment, and Alex, a non-theist who questions this view:

Alex: Theo, you’ve mentioned before that you believe God’s moral goodness can’t be judged by humans. Isn’t that a bit limiting for meaningful discussion about morality?

Theo: It might seem that way, but it’s actually very straightforward. God is perfect and His ways are beyond our understanding. We cannot apply our human standards of morality to the divine.

Alex: But doesn’t that make it impossible to question God’s moral goodness? How do we then affirm God as a legitimate moral arbiter if we cannot even question His actions?

Theo: We trust that God is good and His judgment is always right. His moral goodness is a given.

Alex: Suppose a leader’s kindness counts for him, but every cruel act is excused by secret reasons. What makes that assessment reliable? I am comparing the rule for evidence, not saying a deity has a leader’s limitations.

Theo: I would say my trust has another basis: revelation and an argument that God’s nature is necessarily good. It need not come only from judging individual actions.

Alex: Then those grounds matter. How do you identify a revelation as trustworthy without already assuming the speaker’s goodness? And what establishes that this particular claimed speaker has the nature your argument describes?

Theo: His commands are inherently good because they come from Him.

Alex: That states your proposed source of goodness. It does not yet justify our trust in this speaker. If the revelation is reliable because God is good, and we know God is good only because the revelation says so, the support runs in a circle.

Theo: It might appear circular from a human perspective, but divine logic is different from human logic.

Alex: If “divine logic” permits that circle to establish its own conclusion, how could we distinguish your speaker from a deceptive one making the same claim? Limited understanding can warrant caution, but it does not itself select the favorable interpretation.

Theo: It’s challenging, but faith often requires us to accept things beyond our understanding.

Alex: Yes, but trust and a justification offered to someone else are different tasks. If there is independent support, we should examine it. If every adverse sign is disqualified and the only favorable support assumes the conclusion, the claim to moral authority has not been earned.

This dialogue preserves the curator’s challenge while allowing an independent-ground reply. The unresolved question is whether Theo can supply and defend those grounds without circularity or selectively disabling the standards used to assess them.

Claude response · editorial edition

Here is a hypothetical dialogue between a theist (Tara) and a non-theist (Nate) that highlights the argument we discussed:

Nate: So Tara, you believe your God is the ultimate source of morality, correct?

Tara: Absolutely. God is perfectly good and righteous, and God’s commands define what is ethical.

Nate: But how can you be sure God is truly good and the legitimate moral authority if God’s goodness cannot be questioned or evaluated?

Tara: God is beyond human judgment. We cannot apply our limited moral reasoning to an all-knowing, all-powerful deity.

Nate: Does limited knowledge make every possible adverse judgment irrelevant, or does it make some judgments uncertain? Your conclusion needs the stronger claim, not merely the admission that we lack complete information.

Tara: No, you’re mistaken. God’s moral perfection is an unquestionable fact. Any evil or suffering allowed by God must be for a greater good we cannot comprehend.

Nate: A hidden justification may be possible, but that alone does not show it exists. If an apparent kindness supports goodness, why can apparent cruelty never count against it? What supports that difference?

Tara: That doesn’t follow. God’s nature as the source of morality is self-evident.

Nate: Our argument says: if no action can count against goodness, and that prevents meaningful moral questioning, and such questioning is required to justify moral authority, then your claim to justified authority fails. You can dispute either connecting premise, but you need reasons beyond repeating the conclusion.

Tara: I accept that conditional argument, but I dispute the claim that action-based criticism is the only route to justification. I think God’s necessarily good nature provides another route. I would also question whether an apparently cruel command really came from God.

Nate: Then you have left the total black box: you allow assessment of the alleged source. We still need to examine your argument about divine nature and your reasons for identifying a message as divine. That is a live disagreement, not a reason to announce either that your case is proved or that all faith is irrational.
