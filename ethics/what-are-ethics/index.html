1Original prompt · edited responses

Prompt 1: Describe the philosophical field of ethics or moral philosophy.

The scope of moral philosophy

ChatGPT response · editorial edition

Ethics, also known as moral philosophy, is a branch of philosophy that involves systematizing, defending, and recommending concepts of right and wrong behavior. It seeks to address questions about morality and examines how individuals ought to act, exploring the nature of moral judgments and moral reasoning. The field is divided into several sub-disciplines, including:

  1. Meta-ethics: Investigates the origin and meaning of ethical principles, questioning the nature of ethical statements, judgments, and values. It deals with questions such as “What does it mean for an action to be right?” and “Is morality objective or subjective?”
  2. Normative ethics: Examines standards for right action and good character. Utilitarianism assesses overall welfare; deontology emphasizes duties or constraints; virtue ethics examines character and practical judgment. These are proposals to defend, not established answers merely because they have names.
  3. Applied ethics: Involves examining specific controversial issues by applying normative ethical theories. It includes areas such as medical ethics, environmental ethics, and business ethics, addressing questions like the ethical implications of abortion, euthanasia, and the moral considerations of climate change.
  4. Descriptive ethics: Empirical study of people’s beliefs about morality. It seeks to understand how people actually behave and think concerning moral issues, drawing on research from psychology, sociology, and anthropology.

Ethics asks about duty, virtue and a good life, while also examining whether moral judgments have the authority they claim. For example, describing widespread disapproval of lying is a descriptive task; defending a prohibition is normative; asking what could make the prohibition objectively binding is meta-ethical.

Gemini response · editorial edition

Ethics, also known as moral philosophy, is a branch of philosophy that explores fundamental questions about morality and right and wrong behavior. Here’s a breakdown of its key aspects:

  • Focus on Moral Principles: Ethics delves into the core principles that guide our moral judgments. It asks questions like “What is good?” and “What is the right thing to do?”
  • Normative vs. descriptive: Descriptive inquiry studies existing moral beliefs and practices. Normative inquiry asks which standards should guide action. It need not assume at the outset that those standards are objective moral facts.
  • Subfields: There are three main subfields within ethics:
    • Normative Ethics: Focuses on developing moral theories that prescribe what is right and wrong. (e.g., utilitarianism, deontology)
    • Applied Ethics: Examines how these theories play out in specific real-world situations. (e.g., medical ethics, business ethics)
    • Metaethics: Analyzes the nature of morality itself. It questions the basis of our moral judgments and how we decide what is good or bad.
  • Moral vs. legal: A law can be valid within a legal system yet morally disputed. A moral demand can lack legal recognition. Neither legality nor widespread approval alone settles whether a demand is justified.

Ethics provides a framework for us to analyze complex situations, make moral judgments, and ultimately, act in a way we believe to be right.

Prompt 2: Any moral system needs grounding. What are the various ways thinkers have attempted to establish a moral realm and the existence of moral facts?

Proposed foundations and their unanswered questions

ChatGPT response · editorial edition

The grounding question asks what, if anything, makes moral claims true or authoritative. The original list mixes proposed foundations, theories of right action and positions about moral truth. Keeping those roles distinct lets us evaluate each proposal without assuming that naming a theory establishes a moral realm.

  1. Divine command theory: Grounds moral obligation in divine commands. It must explain why those commands bind, how they are identified, and whether goodness depends on command or supplies a reason for it. The existence of a powerful commander alone does not establish moral authority.
  2. Natural law: Grounds practical principles in an account of human nature and goods. Aquinas, Summa Theologiae I–II, question 94, explains natural-law precepts through practical reason and human inclinations. A critic can ask how identifying an inclination establishes what ought to be pursued; the theory needs its account of goods, not merely observations of common behavior.
  3. Deontology: Duty-based theories identify obligations or constraints. Kant connects obligation to rational agency and treats humanity as an end, never merely a means. Distinguish an action’s conformity to duty from its moral worth when done from duty. The further grounding question is why rational agency carries this authority.
  4. Consequentialism: Evaluates actions through consequences. Utilitarianism takes overall welfare as the relevant standard; Mill’s Utilitarianism, chapter II, explains the happiness criterion. Measuring welfare does not itself establish that maximizing it is obligatory. That normative premise needs a defense.
  5. Virtue ethics: Examines good character and practical wisdom, often through an account of human flourishing associated with Aristotle. It must explain which traits count as virtues and why its account of flourishing provides a standard for us, rather than simply reflecting admired habits.
  6. Moral relativism: Makes the truth or justification of moral judgments relative to a framework, rather than establishing framework-independent moral facts. Mere cultural disagreement does not establish relativism, and relativism does not imply that every act is acceptable within every framework.
  7. Moral realism: Affirms moral truths; stronger formulations emphasize their independence from our attitudes. This states a position to defend, not a complete grounding argument. Realists disagree about what moral facts are and how reason, experience or intuition could give us access to them.
  8. Constructivism: Explains justified principles through a specified procedure of practical reasoning. Rawls’s construction of principles of justice is one example, not automatically a foundation for every moral claim. The procedure’s starting assumptions and authority require justification; actual agreement alone is insufficient.

For each proposal ask three questions: What is supposed to ground the claim? How could we know that it does? Why would it bind someone who rejects it? A theory can offer useful guidance while its stronger claim to objective authority remains disputed.

Gemini response · editorial edition

Attempts to ground morality appeal to divinity, nature, rational insight, agreement or flourishing. Some defend objective facts; others explain moral practice without that commitment. Here are the original approaches, with their different roles made explicit.

  • Moral realism affirms moral truths; stronger versions emphasize independence from our attitudes. The following three approaches can enter realist accounts, but they are not three equivalent definitions of realism:
    • Divine command theory: Makes divine commands constitutive of obligation. The source’s example of Aquinas is better placed under natural law below; a theistic account need not reduce morality to commands.
    • Natural law: Appeals to human nature, practical reason and goods. Aquinas is the clearer example here. The challenge is to justify the connection between an account of human nature and binding practical principles.
    • Utilitarianism: Uses overall happiness or welfare as a criterion, as in Mill. Its criterion can be combined with different views about moral truth; the label alone does not prove realism.
  • Moral intuitionism: Holds that at least some moral principles can be known without inference from other beliefs. This is not simply an innate feeling or permission to trust any impulse. Sidgwick’s examination of intuitive methods distinguishes judgments about particular cases from general principles. His work is a more suitable example here than the original attribution to Hume.
  • Social contract approaches: Seek justification through actual or hypothetical agreement under specified conditions. Locke and Rousseau are important political-contract thinkers, but explaining political legitimacy does not automatically ground all moral obligations. Why the proposed agreement should bind remains a central question.
  • Virtue Ethics: This approach focuses on developing good character traits, or virtues, like honesty, courage, and compassion. These virtues guide us towards moral action. (Thinkers like Aristotle)

Objectivity does not mean that a rule admits no exceptions. Likewise, intuitionism does not mean that any personal feeling is authoritative. These approaches need arguments about evidence, scope and justification; describing their appeal does not settle those arguments.

The debate about grounding morality continues, with no single universally accepted answer.

Prompt 3: Define moral skepticism or moral nihilism and provide basic arguments for this position.

Skepticism, nihilism and the arguments for them

ChatGPT response · editorial edition

Moral skepticism questions moral knowledge or justification; some skeptical positions also challenge moral truth. Moral nihilism commonly denies objective moral values. These are distinguishable claims: doubting our access to moral facts is not yet proving that there are none.

Moral Skepticism

A skeptic may ask what justifies a moral belief and whether the proposed method reliably distinguishes truth from approval. The challenge need not demand certainty: ordinary knowledge is often understood as compatible with possible error. Nor does the absence of direct observation alone refute a moral claim; the issue is whether another adequate justification exists.

Moral Nihilism

Moral error theory holds that ordinary positive moral judgments purport to attribute properties that nothing possesses. In Mackie’s influential version, the rejected properties are objectively prescriptive values. This does not make every sentence containing moral vocabulary false: a denial of such values can be true on the theory, and reports of people’s beliefs remain possible.

Arguments for Moral Skepticism and Nihilism

  1. Relativity Argument: Observations of moral diversity across cultures and times can lead to skepticism about the existence of universal moral truths. If moral values vary so widely, the argument goes, perhaps they are not grounded in objective facts but in cultural practices or individual preferences.
  2. Evolutionary explanation: If selection shaped a judgment because it aided reproduction rather than because it tracked moral truth, that can challenge our confidence in the judgment. Evolution has no conscious aim. The argument needs to show a relevant disconnect from truth and address possible independent justification; an evolutionary origin alone does not establish falsity.
  3. Disagreement: Persistent disagreement among informed, reflective people can challenge claims of reliable access to moral truth. A defender can cite mistaken background facts or biased reasoning. The skeptic’s stronger question is whether disagreement persists after such explanations are addressed, not whether disagreement by itself proves there is no truth.
  4. Queerness: Mackie challenges the idea of values that would inherently prescribe action, and asks how we could know such properties. The objection concerns their claimed authority and our access to them, not merely their invisibility or unfamiliarity. A realist must explain why the proposed properties and way of knowing are credible.
  5. Subjectivity of experience: Moral reactions often involve emotion and personal perspective. This can support an explanation in terms of attitudes, but does not alone establish that no moral judgment tracks facts. The argument must compare the competing explanations rather than assume its conclusion.

While moral skepticism and nihilism present challenging views, they have also provoked significant debate and discussion, leading to the development of various counterarguments and defenses of moral realism. The dialogue between these perspectives continues to be a central part of ethical theory, pushing philosophers to clarify and defend their positions on the nature of morality.

Gemini response · editorial edition

Moral skepticism and moral nihilism, though sometimes used interchangeably, have distinct nuances. Here’s a breakdown of both:

Moral Skepticism:

  • Core claim: We may lack adequate justification for moral beliefs. Skepticism about knowledge is stronger than the ordinary admission that we can make mistakes, but it need not deny that moral facts exist.
  • Arguments:
    • Disagreement: Divergent judgments raise a question about reliability. They support skepticism only with an argument explaining why error, differing information or bias cannot adequately account for the divergence.
    • Emotivism: Offers a different account of moral language: moral utterances express attitudes rather than simply report facts. Expressing disapproval is not the same as reporting “I dislike stealing,” which can itself be true or false. Emotivism is an alternative theory, not by itself a proof of skepticism.

Moral Nihilism:

  • Core claim: There are no objective moral facts of the proposed kind. This does not deny that pain, preferences, care or social practices are real. Their existence and their alleged objective moral authority are separate questions.
  • Arguments:
    • Moral disagreements: Diversity may support an explanation of moral belief through culture and attitudes. To reach nihilism, one needs a further argument against objective moral facts; disagreement alone is insufficient.
    • Evolutionary byproduct: Some moral responses may be explained by selection and social learning without appealing to moral facts. Explaining a belief’s origin can undermine its justification if that origin is insensitive to truth; it does not automatically show that the belief is false.

It’s important to note that moral skepticism and nihilism don’t necessarily lead to moral inaction. Some skeptics might advocate for following social conventions for a functioning society, while others might focus on building a moral framework based on reason or compassion.

Prompt 4: Differentiate between “ethics” and “morality” as best you can.

Two overlapping words and useful distinctions

ChatGPT response · editorial edition

The terms “ethics” and “morality” are often used interchangeably, but they can be differentiated in philosophical discussions and various contexts. The distinction, though subtle, helps to clarify discussions in moral philosophy, professional conduct, and personal behavior.

Ethics

Ethics is a branch of philosophy that deals with the systematic study of what is morally right and wrong, good and bad. It involves critical analysis of how individuals ought to act and the reasons behind moral principles. Ethics is concerned with the development, defense, and recommendation of concepts of right and wrong behavior. It seeks to answer questions such as “What actions are morally permissible?” and “Why should we act morally?” Ethics can be divided into various subfields, including meta-ethics, normative ethics, and applied ethics, each examining different aspects of moral thought and practice.

Ethics also refers to a set of standards or codes of conduct that guide individuals or groups, such as professional ethics, medical ethics, or business ethics. These are often formalized principles that dictate how members of a profession or organization should behave.

Morality

Morality refers to the actual content of right and wrong actions and the moral principles held by individuals or societies. It encompasses the beliefs, standards, and practices concerning what is right or wrong, guiding individuals’ behavior in various situations. Morality is often derived from cultural, religious, or personal convictions and can vary significantly across different societies and communities.

In this descriptive use, morality concerns the standards people actually accept and enact. The word also has a normative use: the standards that would be justified, whether or not a community accepts them. State which meaning is intended.

Key Differences

  • Origin and Scope: Ethics is the philosophical study of morality, providing a structured framework for understanding moral theories and principles. Morality, on the other hand, is concerned with the specific norms, values, and practices that guide individuals’ actions in everyday life.
  • Universality and context: Neither word guarantees a universal or context-dependent standard. Philosophical ethics includes relativist arguments, while ordinary moral claims can aspire to universal authority. The distinction between the words does not decide that dispute.
  • Theory and practice: Calling ethics the study and morality its subject is useful in this discussion. It is a working convention, not a rigid division: applied ethics is practical, and moral reasoning can be highly theoretical.

Despite these distinctions, the overlap between ethics and morality is significant, and the terms are often used synonymously in many contexts. Both are concerned with questions of right and wrong, and how to live a good life.

Gemini response · editorial edition

The words overlap. One useful contrast is between a person’s convictions and a professional code, but it should not become a definition that makes all morality private and all ethics external.

Morality:

  • Internal compass: Personal morality can refer to an individual’s convictions. Morality can also refer to shared standards or to proposed standards that hold regardless of anyone’s convictions.
  • Source: Morality can be shaped by cultural upbringing, religious beliefs, personal experiences, and values.
  • Examples: You might believe lying is wrong because you were raised to value honesty, or you might think helping others in need is morally right.

Ethics:

  • External system: Professional ethics often means a formal code. Ethics also names philosophical inquiry, which can question that code instead of merely enforcing it.
  • Focus on action: Applied ethics examines particular practices and cases. The wider field also investigates meaning, justification and character, so it is not confined to applying agreed rules.
  • Examples: A doctor follows a code of medical ethics that outlines professional obligations and patient care. A business might have an ethics code that emphasizes fair treatment of employees and environmental responsibility.

As an analogy, personal convictions resemble a navigator’s habitual route, while a professional code resembles published directions. Philosophical ethics also asks whether either route is defensible. The analogy illustrates two uses; it does not establish that a personal conviction is subjective or a published code authoritative.

Key Differences:

  • Level: Both words can concern individuals or groups. Specify “personal morality” or “professional ethics” when that contrast is intended.
  • Source: Both personal convictions and public codes can arise from custom, religion or reflection. Their origin does not by itself justify them.
  • Focus: A useful convention distinguishes the systematic inquiry, ethics, from the standards examined, morality. Ordinary usage often crosses this boundary.

Overlaps:

It’s important to remember that ethics and morality are interconnected.

  • A person’s ethical behavior is often influenced by their morals.
  • Ethical codes often reflect widely held moral beliefs within a society.

In Conclusion:

Use the distinction that serves the conversation, and state it explicitly. Whether a standard is called ethical or moral tells us little about whether it is justified.

Two sets of fifteen discussion questions

ChatGPT response · editorial edition

  1. How does the distinction between ethics and morality influence our understanding of cultural differences in moral values?
  2. Can ethical theories provide a universal framework for morality, or are they limited by cultural relativism?
  3. How do personal experiences and cultural backgrounds shape an individual’s moral judgments?
  4. In what ways do meta-ethics, normative ethics, and applied ethics complement each other in the study of moral philosophy?
  5. Can moral skepticism or moral nihilism be reconciled with the need for ethical standards in society?
  6. How do professional ethics differ from personal morality in guiding behavior within specific fields like medicine, law, or business?
  7. Can morality be universal while allowing judgments to depend on circumstances? How does that differ from relativism?
  8. How does the concept of moral relativism challenge the pursuit of universal ethical principles?
  9. In what ways can understanding the philosophical underpinnings of ethics enhance ethical decision-making in real-world situations?
  10. How do ethical theories address the challenge of moral dilemmas where competing values are in conflict?
  11. Can the study of ethics help resolve disagreements about contentious moral issues, such as euthanasia, abortion, or animal rights?
  12. How does the evolutionary explanation of moral beliefs impact the validity of moral reasoning?
  13. What role do emotions play in moral reasoning, and how do they interact with ethical principles?
  14. How can individuals and societies balance the need for ethical guidelines with respect for diverse moral perspectives?
  15. In what ways do discussions about ethics and morality contribute to personal growth and the development of a just society?

Gemini response · editorial edition

Discussion Questions on Ethics and Morality:

  1. Can there be a universal moral code that applies to all cultures? Why or why not?
  2. Is morality primarily shaped by upbringing and cultural background, or are there innate moral instincts?
  3. How do religious beliefs influence a person’s moral compass?
  4. In a situation where following a moral principle might have negative consequences, should the principle still be upheld?
  5. Utilitarianism emphasizes maximizing overall happiness. Can this principle be applied fairly in all situations?
  6. Is moral intuitionism a reliable guide for moral decision-making? How can we ensure our intuitions are well-informed?
  7. Social Contract Theory suggests we agree to follow moral rules for societal benefit. What happens when there’s disagreement about the rules?
  8. How can virtue ethics be used to cultivate a more ethical society?
  9. Does moral skepticism imply relativism? Would either view imply that every action is acceptable? Explain the additional premises those inferences would require.
  10. If moral nihilism is true, does it mean life is meaningless? How can we find purpose without objective morality?
  11. Can advancements in science, like brain scans, help us understand the basis of morality?
  12. How does technology influence our moral decision-making? (e.g., self-driving cars, social media algorithms)
  13. What role do emotions play in moral reasoning? Should emotions be completely removed from moral judgments?
  14. Is there a difference between personal ethics and professional ethics? If so, how should they interact?
  15. How can we encourage more open and productive discussions about ethics in our society?
