Prompt 1: Many moralists invoke the “self-evident” nature of morality to substantiate their diverse notions of morality. Rigorously critique the intrinsic weaknesses in this argument.

Many moralists invoke the “ self-evident ” nature of morality to substantiate their diverse notions of morality. Rigorously critique the intrinsic weaknesses in this argument.

Example: And so I came to the conclusion that there is a reasonable case for saying that there are objective moral truths and this is not just a matter of our attitudes or of our preferences universalized, but there’s something stronger going on and it’s, in some ways, more like the objectivity of mathematical truths or perhaps of logical truths. It’s not an empirical thing. This is not something you can describe that comes in the world, the natural world of our sense that you can find or prove empirically. It’s rather something that is rationally self-evident, I guess, to people who reflect on it properly and think about it carefully. So that’s how I gradually made the move towards objectivist metaethic. — Peter Singer

ChatGPT response · editorial edition

Calling a moral claim self-evident does not settle its truth. The strongest critique asks how alleged direct justification can be distinguished from conditioning, error and prejudice. It should not assume that self-evidence means universal actual agreement. The arguments below retain the original structure while marking premises and inferences that require correction.

Subjectivity of Self-Evidence

What is deemed self-evident is often subject to personal intuition, cultural background, or societal norms. This subjectivity raises the question of whether self-evidence can serve as an objective foundation for morality.

Syllogism:

  1. Premise 1 (disputed): if a proposition is self-evident, everyone recognizes it as true. This is stronger than the claim that adequate understanding can justify belief without inference.
  2. Premise 2: at least some moral propositions lack universal recognition.
  3. Conditional conclusion: if the disputed first premise holds, at least some moral propositions are not self-evident. The existential premise does not establish that none are.

Symbolic Logic:

Let:

  • S(p) = “Proposition p is self-evident”
  • U(p) = “Proposition p is universally recognized as true”

We have:

  1. \forall p \ (S(p) \rightarrow U(p))
  2. \exists p \ (M(p) \land \neg U(p))
  3. Therefore, \exists p \ (M(p) \land \neg S(p))

Where M(p) denotes that p is a moral proposition.

Circular Reasoning

A claim of direct, non-inferential justification is not automatically circular. Circularity arises when the only support for self-evidence is the proposition’s assumed truth and the only support for its truth is that alleged self-evidence.

Syllogism:

  1. Premise: Moral proposition p is true because it is self-evident.
  2. But why is p self-evident? Because it is true.
  3. Conclusion: Therefore, p is true because it is true.

Symbolic Logic:

Let:

  • T(p) = “Proposition p is true”
  • S(p) = “Proposition p is self-evident”

We observe:

  1. T(p) \leftrightarrow S(p)
  2. S(p) \leftrightarrow T(p)
  3. These two biconditionals express the same equivalence; they are not tautologies. Without an independently warranted assertion of either side, they establish neither truth nor self-evidence.

Disagreement in Moral Intuitions

Disagreement challenges an unqualified claim of effortless recognition. A defender must explain which disagreements reflect inadequate understanding without simply defining all dissenters as defective.

Syllogism:

  1. Premise 1 (disputed): every rational agent knows every self-evident proposition. Rationality alone does not guarantee attention, comprehension or freedom from error.
  2. Premise 2: Rational agents often disagree on moral issues.
  3. Conditional conclusion: the specified proposition is not self-evident if that strong recognition premise is accepted. This is not a proof about every moral proposition.

Symbolic Logic:

Let:

  • R(a) = “Agent a is rational”
  • S(p) = “Proposition p is self-evident”
  • K(a, p) = “Agent a knows proposition p“

We have:

  1. \forall a \ (R(a) \rightarrow (S(p) \rightarrow K(a, p)))
  2. \exists a, b \ (R(a) \land R(b) \land \neg (K(a, p) \leftrightarrow K(b, p)))
  3. Therefore, \neg \forall a \ (R(a) \rightarrow K(a, p))
  4. Hence, \neg S(p)

Inapplicability of Mathematical Objectivity

Mathematics provides useful comparisons, but an analogy cannot establish a moral claim’s objectivity. Proof from axioms, self-evidence of premises and mind-independent truth are separate questions.

Syllogism:

  1. Original premise to reject: mathematical truths are self-evident because they are provable. A difficult theorem can be proved without being self-evident; not every truth is provable in a specified formal system.
  2. More defensible observation: competing moral systems often dispute their starting premises. Valid deductions from moral premises remain possible.
  3. Repaired conclusion: a comparison with mathematics needs an account of why the proposed moral premises are justified. Absence of an agreed proof does not entail absence of self-evidence.

Symbolic Logic:

Let:

  • P(p) = “Proposition p can be logically proven”
  • S(p) = “Proposition p is self-evident”
  • M(p) = “Proposition p is a moral proposition”
  • T(p) = “Proposition p is a mathematical truth”

The original formulas below are retained as a failed argument to diagnose: neither premise supplies the needed implication S(p) → P(p).

  1. \forall p \ (T(p) \rightarrow (P(p) \land S(p)))
  2. \forall p \ (M(p) \rightarrow \neg P(p))
  3. The proposed conclusion ∀p(M(p) → ¬S(p)) does not follow. A counterexample sets T(p) false, M(p) true, P(p) false and S(p) true: both displayed premises hold while this conclusion fails.

Contradictory Self-Evident Claims

Different moral systems often present contradictory propositions as self-evident, leading to logical inconsistencies.

Syllogism:

  1. Premise 1: p says action A is obligatory in specified circumstances and is claimed to be self-evident.
  2. Premise 2: q denies that same obligation in the same circumstances and is also claimed to be self-evident.
  3. Conclusion: Contradiction arises, so both propositions cannot be self-evident truths.

Symbolic Logic:

Let:

  • S(p) = “Proposition p is self-evident”
  • S(q) = “Proposition q is self-evident”
  • p: O(A) = “Action A is obligatory”
  • q: \neg O(A) = “Action A is not obligatory”

We have:

  1. S(p) \land p
  2. S(q) \land q
  3. Since p and q are contradictory: p \leftrightarrow \neg q
  4. If self-evidence entails truth, the two contradictory propositions cannot both be self-evident. It follows that at least one claim of self-evidence is mistaken, not that all such claims are.

Conclusion

An unsupported appeal to self-evidence is a weak public justification when competent disagreement persists. The critic can demand an account of comprehension, reliability and error correction. That challenge preserves the skeptical force of the original without treating disagreement, lack of proof or conflicting assertions as a demonstration that no moral truth exists.

The Subjectivity of Self-Evidence in Moral Arguments

Introduction

This essay examines variation in what people experience as obvious. Its target is the unsupported transition from felt certainty to warranted moral belief. The formal arguments are conditional challenges to strong recognition claims; they do not establish moral non-realism merely from disagreement.

The Nature of Self-Evidence

Self-evidence is commonly understood as justification available through adequate understanding rather than inference from other propositions. It need not mean immediate recognition by everybody. Axioms can also be adopted as starting assumptions without being self-evident. See Intuitionism in Ethics for the philosophical distinction.

The Problem of Subjectivity

Differences in upbringing, experience and social learning can affect apparent obviousness. These influences raise a reliability question, but a belief’s causal history alone does not determine its truth.

Analysis through Syllogisms and Symbolic Logic

To rigorously critique the subjectivity of self-evidence in moral arguments, we will employ syllogistic reasoning and symbolic logic.

Syllogism
  1. Premise 1 (a strong, contested interpretation): self-evidence entails universal actual recognition.
  2. Premise 2: some moral propositions are not universally recognized.
  3. Conditional conclusion: at least some moral propositions fail that strong interpretation of self-evidence.

This argument is valid only with a matched quantifier and the stated recognition premise. The premise is precisely what a defender of a more qualified account can reject.

Symbolic Logic

Let us define the following:

  • S(p): “Proposition p is self-evident.”
  • U(p): “Proposition p is universally recognized as true.”
  • M(p): “Proposition p is a moral proposition.”

We can formalize the argument as:

  1. \forall p \ (S(p) \rightarrow U(p))
    • This universal recognition condition is an assumption for the conditional argument, not an agreed definition.
  2. \exists p \ (M(p) \land \neg U(p))
    • There exists a proposition p such that p is a moral proposition and p is not universally recognized as true.
  3. Therefore, \exists p \ (M(p) \land \neg S(p))
    • Under that assumption, there exists at least one moral proposition that is not self-evident.

The existential conclusion concerns some propositions, not all. To challenge a particular claim, identify disagreement about that same proposition and defend the relevance of the dissenters’ understanding.

Cultural and Individual Variability
Influence of Culture

Cultural diversity leads to different moral codes and values. For instance, practices considered morally acceptable in one culture may be deemed reprehensible in another. This cultural variability undermines the claim of self-evidence in moral propositions.

Hypothetical example: people may disagree about a family’s role in choosing a spouse. Distinguish consensual arranged marriage from forced marriage; disagreement about different practices is not disagreement about one identical proposition.

Influence of Personal Intuition

Individual experiences and psychological factors shape personal moral intuitions. Two rational individuals may arrive at opposing moral conclusions based on their unique perspectives, further illustrating the subjectivity involved.

Example: people may differ about whether capital punishment is justified. Whether it deters crime is an empirical question, distinct from the moral judgment about punishment; neither is settled by calling it obvious.

The Illusion of Objectivity

Mathematical axioms are explicit starting points within specified systems. Alternative geometries illustrate why universal acceptance should not define their status. The moral analogy therefore needs more than a claim that mathematics is culturally uniform.

Comparing Mathematical and Moral Self-Evidence

In mathematics:

  • Axioms are adopted premises; some seem evident, while others are justified by their role in a theory.
  • A proof’s validity within a stated system differs from how people acquire, understand or accept that system.

In morality:

  • Moral propositions lack such universal acceptance.
  • Apparent self-evidence may reflect conditioning. Establishing that a particular belief is unreliable requires an argument, not merely the observation that it has social influences.
The Challenge of Moral Disagreements

Persistent informed disagreement can weaken confidence in easy access to moral truth. It does not entail that every disputed proposition lacks direct justification.

Symbolic Logic of Disagreement

Let:

  • R(a): “Agent a is rational.”
  • K(a, p): “Agent a knows proposition p.”

We can express the issue as:

  1. \forall a \ (R(a) \rightarrow (S(p) \rightarrow K(a, p)))
    • This assumes rationality alone guarantees recognition; a more qualified account would require adequate understanding and relevant conditions.
  2. \exists a, b \ (R(a) \land R(b) \land \neg (K(a, p) \leftrightarrow K(b, p)))
    • The formula assumes one agent knows p and another does not. Observed disagreement alone does not establish which participant has knowledge, since knowledge entails truth.
  3. Therefore, \neg \forall a \ (R(a) \rightarrow K(a, p))
    • Not all rational agents know p.
  4. Hence, \neg S(p)
    • The conclusion follows only under the strong recognition assumption for this specified p.

The structure is a conditional test, not an unconditional refutation of moral self-evidence.

Implications for Moral Philosophy
The Need for External Justification

Where an appeal fails to persuade, participants can make their assumptions and reasons available for criticism. This involves:

  • Providing empirical evidence where possible.
  • Employing coherent ethical theories that can be critically examined.
  • Engaging in cross-cultural dialogues to understand diverse moral perspectives.
Avoiding Dogmatism

Relying on self-evidence can lead to dogmatism, where individuals or groups refuse to consider alternative viewpoints due to the belief that their moral stance is unquestionably true. Recognizing the subjectivity involved encourages openness and critical examination of one’s moral beliefs.

Conclusion

Variation in perceived obviousness supplies a serious challenge to unqualified appeals to self-evidence. The challenge concerns access and justification; it does not by itself decide whether objective moral truths exist. A successful defense must explain error and disagreement without assuming the very claim in dispute.

References
  • Kant, Immanuel. Critique of Pure Reason. An exploration of the limits of self-evidence and reason.
  • Hume, David. A Treatise of Human Nature. Discusses the role of sentiment in moral judgments.
  • Rawls, John. A Theory of Justice. Introduces the idea of the “veil of ignorance” to derive principles of justice.
  • Singer, Peter. Practical Ethics. Examines ethical issues through a utilitarian lens, challenging self-evident moral intuitions.

The Circular Reasoning in Appeals to Self-Evident Morality

Introduction

This essay targets a specific circular defense: p is true because self-evident, while its self-evidence is defended only by assuming p true. It distinguishes that failure of support from the separate proposal that some beliefs are justified without inference.

Understanding Circular Reasoning

Begging the question occurs when the disputed conclusion supplies its own essential support. Such an argument can be deductively valid—p therefore p is valid—while providing no new reason for someone who doubts p.

Example of Circular Reasoning in Everyday Context

A classic example of circular reasoning is:

  • “I am trustworthy because I say I am trustworthy.”

Here, the speaker’s trustworthiness is assumed based on their own assertion, offering no independent verification.

Circular Reasoning in Moral Arguments

An appeal becomes circular when the speaker defends it in the following way:

  1. Premise: Moral proposition p is true because it is self-evident.
  2. Question: Why is p self-evident?
  3. Response: Because it is true.
  4. Conclusion: Therefore, p is true because it is true.

The exchange fails to provide independent support; its defect is explanatory and justificatory, not necessarily invalid deduction.

Formalizing the Circular Reasoning

To critically analyze this circularity, we can employ syllogisms and symbolic logic.

Syllogism
  1. Premise 1: Moral proposition p is self-evident.
  2. Premise 2: If a proposition is self-evident, then it is true.
  3. Conclusion: p is true. This is a valid inference from the two premises; the issue is whether the first premise has adequate support.

If the only defense of the first premise assumes p true, support is circular. A non-inferential account must instead explain the claimed direct warrant.

Symbolic Logic

Let us define:

  • T(p): “Proposition p is true.”
  • S(p): “Proposition p is self-evident.”

We can express the argument as:

  1. S(p) \rightarrow T(p)
    • If p is self-evident, then p is true.
  2. T(p) \rightarrow S(p)
    • The reverse conditional is an additional claim, not something that follows from the first. Many true propositions are not self-evident.

This forms a biconditional relationship:

  1. T(p) \leftrightarrow S(p)

The equivalence alone establishes neither side. Circular support arises only when each side is used as the sole warrant for the other; a biconditional is not inherently a fallacy.

The Logical Flaws of Circular Reasoning
Lack of Independent Support

A circular defense offers no new support to someone who doubts its premise. Merely asserting self-evidence can also be unsupported without literally being an argument in a circle.

Example
  • Assertion: “Honesty is morally obligatory because it is self-evident.”
  • Challenge: “Why is it self-evident?”
  • Response: “Because honesty is morally obligatory.”

This exchange highlights the lack of independent justification for why honesty should be considered morally obligatory.

Failure to Persuade Skeptics

Circular arguments are ineffective in persuading those who do not already accept the proposition. Since the argument assumes agreement on the very point in question, it cannot bridge differences in moral perspectives.

Illustration
  • A person skeptical about the moral obligation of honesty will not be convinced by an argument that presupposes the obligation’s truth.
  • Without external reasons or evidence, the skeptic remains unconvinced.
The Necessity of External Justification

To persuade a critic, offer reasons whose warrant does not depend solely on assuming the disputed conclusion. Possible resources include:

  • Empirical Evidence: Demonstrating the consequences of moral actions in the real world.
  • Logical Reasoning: Building arguments based on widely accepted premises.
  • Ethical theories: expose their starting commitments and test consistency; being established does not itself make a theory true.
Avoiding Circularity

To avoid circular reasoning, moralists should:

  • Define Terms Clearly: Ensure that the premises do not contain implicit assumptions of the conclusion.
  • Provide Independent Premises: Use evidence or reasoning not contingent on the truth of the conclusion.
  • Address Counterarguments: Engage with opposing views to strengthen the argument’s validity.
Analysis through Symbolic Logic

To further elucidate the circularity issue, let’s construct a formal logical representation.

Definitions

Let:

  • M(p): “Proposition p is a moral proposition.”
  • E(p): “Proposition p is established by evidence or reasoning.”
  • S(p): “Proposition p is self-evident.”
  • T(p): “Proposition p is true.”
Argument Structure
  1. Assumption: S(p) \rightarrow T(p)
    • If p is self-evident, then p is true.
  2. Question: Why is p self-evident?
  3. Response: T(p) \rightarrow S(p)
    • This proposed reverse implication needs justification; truth does not generally entail self-evidence.

This forms a circular dependency:

  1. S(p) \leftrightarrow T(p)

There is no introduction of E(p), meaning that no independent evidence or reasoning is provided.

Implications
  • The proposed defenses depend on each other. Truth itself does not become dependent on a person’s recognition of it.
  • The problem is lack of independent support, not failure of deductive validity. Validity asks whether true premises could yield a false conclusion.
The Difference Between Analytic and Synthetic Propositions

Understanding the distinction between analytic and synthetic propositions can further clarify the issue.

Analytic Propositions
  • Definition: Statements that are true by virtue of their meaning, such as “All bachelors are unmarried men.”
  • Characteristic: Their truth is self-contained and does not rely on external facts.
Synthetic Propositions
  • Definition: Statements whose truth depends on how the world is, such as “The cat is on the mat.”
  • Synthetic statements are not true solely by meaning. Whether all require empirical verification is disputed; the category of synthetic a priori judgment is central to this debate.
Application to Moral Propositions
  • Many substantive moral claims are treated as synthetic, though accounts of moral language differ.
  • Calling a claim self-evident does not necessarily treat it as analytic: a defender may claim synthetic, non-inferential knowledge.
  • Thus the analytic/synthetic distinction does not prove circularity. The critic must examine the actual chain of support.
The Role of Assumptions in Logical Arguments

A sound argument is valid and has true premises. Agreement among participants is a separate matter affecting persuasion and shared inquiry.

Acceptable Premises
  • Common Ground: Premises should be based on shared understandings or facts.
  • Independent Verification: Premises should be verifiable without assuming the conclusion.
Circular Premises
  • Premises that assume the conclusion do not provide a path to new knowledge or understanding.
  • Unsupported attribution of self-evidence can leave a critic without a reason to accept a premise. It is not automatically equivalent to assuming a conclusion within an argument.
Addressing Potential Counterarguments
Claim: Self-Evidence Is a Valid Foundation

Some may argue that self-evidence is a valid foundation for certain truths, including moral ones.

Response
  • A mathematical analogy cannot settle which moral claims have direct warrant; neither field requires universal actual agreement for truth.
  • People can differ in what seems obvious. Whether any seeming provides justification remains the question, rather than an established premise that morality is subjective.
Claim: Moral Intuitions Provide Self-Evidence

Another argument is that moral intuitions are innate and provide self-evident truths.

Response
  • Moral intuitions vary widely among individuals and cultures.
  • An intuition may be challenged as unreliable or unsupported. It is circular only if its defense relies on the claim it is supposed to justify.
The Importance of Non-Circular Justification in Ethics
Enhancing Persuasion
  • Providing independent reasons for moral propositions makes arguments more persuasive to a broader audience.
  • It allows for meaningful dialogue and potential consensus.
Strengthening Moral Theories
  • Avoiding circular reasoning contributes to the development of robust moral theories that can withstand critical scrutiny.
  • It encourages the use of interdisciplinary approaches, incorporating insights from psychology, sociology, and other fields.
Conclusion

The circular version of an appeal to self-evidence fails to give a doubter independent support. A stronger critique distinguishes that failure from deductive invalidity and from non-inferential justification. It asks defenders how direct warrant is recognized, how error is detected and why dissent should reduce or preserve confidence.

References
  • Aristotle. Prior Analytics. Discusses the principles of logical reasoning and syllogisms.
  • Fallacy Files. “Begging the Question.” An online resource explaining logical fallacies.
  • Hume, David. An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding. Explores the nature of human reasoning and skepticism.
  • Kant, Immanuel. Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals. A primary work on rational foundations of ethics; it should not be summarized as simply rejecting all self-evidence.

Disagreement in Moral Intuitions and the Question of Self-Evidence in Morality

Introduction

Persistent disagreement challenges claims that moral truth is readily available through reflection. This essay examines the strength and limits of that challenge, without inferring non-realism solely from the existence of dissent.

The Nature of Moral Intuitions

Moral intuitions refer to the immediate, instinctive judgments that individuals make about the rightness or wrongness of actions without conscious deliberation. These intuitions are influenced by a myriad of factors, including cultural norms, personal experiences, upbringing, and innate psychological tendencies. While some moral intuitions may seem universally shared, closer examination reveals significant variations across different contexts.

Cultural and Individual Diversity in Moral Judgments
Cultural Variations

Different cultures have diverse moral codes that reflect their unique histories, religions, and social structures. Practices deemed morally acceptable in one culture may be considered immoral in another.

  • Example: The practice of polygamy is morally accepted in some societies but considered unethical in others.
  • Example: Attitudes toward euthanasia vary significantly between cultures, with some viewing it as compassionate and others as morally impermissible.
Individual Differences

Even within the same culture, individuals may hold divergent moral intuitions based on personal experiences or psychological factors.

  • Example: Two individuals exposed to the same cultural environment may disagree on the morality of capital punishment due to personal beliefs or emotional responses.
Logical Analysis of Disagreement in Moral Intuitions

The following argument depends on a strong recognition assumption. Rationality alone may not ensure adequate understanding of a particular proposition.

Syllogistic Analysis
  1. Premise 1 (contested): every rational agent recognizes every self-evident moral truth.
  2. Premise 2: rational agents disagree about at least some specified moral propositions.
  3. Conditional conclusion: those disputed propositions fail this strong recognition condition; the argument does not cover every moral proposition.

A defender can dispute the recognition premise. The critic should then ask for independently identifiable conditions of adequate understanding.

Symbolic Logic

Let us define the following symbols:

  • R(a): “Agent a is rational.”
  • S(p): “Proposition p is self-evident.”
  • K(a, p): “Agent a knows proposition p.”
  • M(p): “Proposition p is a moral proposition.”

We can formalize the argument as follows:

  1. Definition of Self-Evident Moral Truths:
\forall a \ [R(a) \rightarrow (S(p) \rightarrow K(a, p))]
  • The formula states the strong recognition assumption being tested, rather than an agreed definition of self-evidence.

Existence of Disagreement:

\exists a, b \ [R(a) \land R(b) \land K(a, p) \land \neg K(b, p)]
  • The formula assumes one participant knows p and another does not. Mere disagreement supplies conflicting beliefs, not knowledge of which belief is true.

Contradiction of Universal Recognition:

From (1) and (2), we derive:

\neg \forall a \ [R(a) \rightarrow K(a, p)]
  • Not all rational agents know p.

Conclusion:

Therefore, S(p) cannot be true:

\neg S(p)
  • For this p, the conclusion is conditional on the recognition premise and the stronger knowledge assumption.

This identifies the burden of defending recognition conditions; it does not refute all direct moral justification.

Implications for the Concept of Self-Evidence in Morality
The Requirement of Universal Recognition

Some accounts connect self-evidence to adequate understanding. The critic can press whether persistent informed disagreement remains after relevant facts, meanings and circumstances have been aligned.

  • Conditional challenge: if adequate understanding guarantees recognition, disagreement among people meeting that condition threatens the claim.
  • Observation: Rational agents do, in fact, disagree on moral propositions even after careful consideration.
Challenges to Objectivity

Variation can support concern about reliability, but it does not logically establish that there is no objective answer.

  • Subjectivity: Moral intuitions are shaped by individual experiences and cultural contexts.
  • Relativism is one possible explanation, not a consequence of disagreement. A realist can instead attribute error to one or more participants.
The Challenge of Moral Relativism

Moral relativism makes moral truth relative to a standard, culture or perspective. Disagreement alone does not establish that theory, just as scientific disagreement does not establish relative scientific truth.

Ethical Implications
  • Tolerance is a possible additional commitment; it does not follow from relativism, which could also describe an intolerant local standard.
  • Conflict: May lead to difficulties in resolving moral disputes due to the lack of common ground.
Philosophical Responses
  • Moral Universalism: Attempts to establish universal moral principles that transcend cultural and individual differences.
  • Constructivism grounds principles in a specified procedure of practical reasoning; it need not identify truth with whatever a society happens to agree on.
The Need for Objective Justification in Moral Philosophy

Participants can seek publicly assessable reasons beyond the bare assertion of self-evidence. This practical recommendation does not assume that all justification must be inferential.

Rational Discourse

Engaging in rational discourse allows for the examination of moral propositions through logical arguments and evidence.

  • Example: Debating the morality of an action based on its consequences, rights involved, or adherence to universal principles.
Ethical Theories

Utilizing established ethical theories provides frameworks for evaluating moral propositions.

  • Utilitarianism: typically assesses consequences by aggregate welfare; “greatest number” alone can conceal intensity and distribution.
  • Deontology: Focuses on duties and adherence to moral rules.
  • Virtue Ethics: Emphasizes the character of the moral agent.
Conclusion

Informed disagreement weakens a bare assertion that everyone who thinks properly must agree. It calls for an account of understanding, error and reliability. The result is a challenge to justification, not a demonstration of relativism or the nonexistence of moral truth.

References
  • Haidt, Jonathan. The Righteous Mind: Why Good People Are Divided by Politics and Religion. Explores how moral intuitions vary among individuals and cultures.

Contradictory Self-Evident Claims and the Problem of Logical Inconsistency in Morality

Introduction

The notion of self-evidence in moral philosophy suggests that certain moral propositions are so inherently true that they require no further justification. Moralists often appeal to self-evidence to assert the indisputable nature of their moral claims. However, a critical issue arises when different moral systems or individuals present contradictory propositions, each purportedly self-evident. This leads to logical inconsistencies that undermine the reliability of self-evidence as a foundation for moral truths. This essay explores the intrinsic weakness of appealing to self-evidence in morality due to the existence of contradictory self-evident claims. Through syllogisms and symbolic logic, we will demonstrate how such contradictions challenge the validity of self-evidence in moral arguments.

The Concept of Self-Evidence in Morality

Self-evidence in moral philosophy refers to propositions that are immediately known to be true upon understanding their meaning, without the need for further proof. These propositions are considered foundational and are thought to provide a secure basis for moral reasoning.

Characteristics of Self-Evident Moral Propositions
  • Immediate Recognition: They are known to be true intuitively.
  • Recognition: adequate understanding is proposed as a condition of access; universal actual assent is a stronger claim.
  • Justification: apparent obviousness is not a guarantee that a person’s attribution of self-evidence is correct.

However, these characteristics are problematic when self-evident claims conflict with one another.

The Phenomenon of Contradictory Self-Evident Claims

Contradictory self-evident claims occur when two or more propositions, each asserted as self-evident, directly oppose each other. This situation leads to logical inconsistencies, as contradictory propositions cannot both be true simultaneously.

Examples in Moral Philosophy
  • Capital Punishment:
    • Claim A: “It is self-evident that capital punishment is morally permissible as a form of justice.”
    • Claim B: “It is self-evident that capital punishment is morally impermissible due to the sanctity of life.”
  • Euthanasia:
    • Claim A: “It is self-evident that individuals have the right to choose death to alleviate suffering.”
    • Claim B: “It is self-evident that taking a life, even one’s own, is morally wrong.”

These conflicting claims illustrate how self-evidence can lead to contradictory moral assertions.

Logical Analysis of Contradictory Claims

To examine the issue systematically, we can employ syllogistic reasoning and symbolic logic.

Syllogistic Analysis
  1. Premise 1: p says A is obligatory in specified circumstances and is asserted to be self-evident.
  2. Premise 2: q denies the same obligation under those same circumstances and is also asserted to be self-evident.
  3. Conclusion: Both propositions cannot be self-evident truths simultaneously, leading to a contradiction.
Symbolic Logic

Let us define:

  • S(p): “Proposition p is self-evident.”
  • S(q): “Proposition q is self-evident.”
  • p: O(A): “Action A is morally obligatory.”
  • q: \neg O(A): “Action A is not morally obligatory.”

We have:

  1. S(p) \land O(A)
  2. S(q) \land \neg O(A)
  3. If self-evidence entails truth, genuine self-evidence of both would yield O(A) and ¬O(A). Merely reporting that two people claim it does not itself assert a contradiction as true.
  4. Therefore, at least one of the propositions cannot be self-evident.

At least one attribution must be mistaken. This demonstrates fallibility of attribution, not that no proposition could be self-evident.

Implications of Contradictory Self-Evident Claims
Questioning the Universality of Self-Evidence

Conflicting attributions show that a feeling of certainty cannot certify every claim. Universal truth, where it exists, is compatible with people making mistakes about it.

  • Apparent obviousness varies; the status of genuine self-evidence remains the disputed issue.
  • Cultural Relativism: Different societies may hold opposing moral propositions as self-evident.
Undermining the Foundation of Moral Arguments

Relying on self-evidence as a foundation for moral arguments becomes problematic when contradictory claims exist.

  • Resolution: the bare label offers no shared way to choose between claims. A defender may propose criteria, whose adequacy then needs examination.
  • Erosion of Authority: The authority of self-evidence is diminished when it leads to contradictions.
Case Studies of Contradictory Self-Evident Claims
Case Study 1: Freedom of Speech vs. Protection from Harm
  • Proposition p: “It is self-evident that freedom of speech is a fundamental right that must not be restricted.”
  • Proposition q: “It is self-evident that speech causing harm should be restricted to protect individuals.”

These claims conflict only where they apply to the same speech and circumstances. A qualified right can coexist with specified restrictions.

Case Study 2: Individual Rights vs. Collective Good
  • Proposition p: “It is self-evident that individuals have the right to personal autonomy.”
  • Proposition q: “It is self-evident that the needs of the many outweigh the needs of the few.”

Such principles can pull in different directions in cases involving vaccination or conscription. A conflict of defeasible reasons is not automatically a formal contradiction.

The Inadequacy of Self-Evidence in Resolving Moral Disputes
Absence of Objective Criteria

Without objective criteria to determine which self-evident claim is valid, moral disputes remain unresolved.

  • Arbitrariness: Choosing between contradictory self-evident claims can appear arbitrary.
  • Bias and Prejudice: Personal biases may influence which claim individuals accept.
Necessity for External Justification

To resolve contradictions, moral propositions require justification beyond self-evidence.

  • Empirical Evidence: Examining the consequences of actions can inform moral judgments.
  • Rational Discourse: Engaging in logical reasoning and ethical debate helps evaluate conflicting claims.
  • Ethical Theories: Applying ethical frameworks provides structured approaches to moral issues.
Philosophical Perspectives on Contradictory Claims
Ethical Relativism

Relativism interprets moral truth relative to a standard; it is one account of variation, not a conclusion forced by it.

  • Implication: There are no absolute moral truths; morality is context-dependent.
Moral Objectivism

Moral objectivism asserts that there are objective moral truths, despite apparent contradictions.

  • Resolution Attempt: Objectivists seek underlying principles that reconcile conflicting claims.
Constructivism

Constructivism proposes justification through a specified practical procedure; it is broader than actual social agreement.

  • Collaborative Resolution: Contradictory claims can be negotiated and harmonized through collective reasoning.
Addressing Contradictory Self-Evident Claims
Critical Examination of Self-Evident Propositions
  • Questioning Assumptions: Scrutinize the basis of purportedly self-evident claims.
  • Seeking Common Ground: Identify shared values that can bridge conflicting propositions.
Development of Ethical Frameworks
  • Utilitarianism evaluates consequences in terms of aggregate welfare, with variants concerning rules and acts.
  • Deontological Ethics: Focuses on duties and adherence to moral rules.
  • Virtue Ethics: Emphasizes moral character and virtues.

These frameworks offer alternative means of assessing moral propositions without relying solely on self-evidence.

Conclusion

Contradictory claims cannot both be true in the same sense and circumstances. Their competing claims to self-evidence show that attribution is fallible and a bare appeal cannot settle the dispute. That limited conclusion motivates scrutiny without establishing that both claims are false or that moral truth is impossible.

On this non-realist interpretation, the frameworks articulate and organize commitments. Their practical usefulness does not by itself establish stance-independent moral facts, and disagreement among them alone does not disprove those facts.
