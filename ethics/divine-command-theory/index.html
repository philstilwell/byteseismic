Prompt 1: If under divine command theory humans cannot themselves assess whether an action is moral or immoral, doesn’t divine command theory equate to mere obedience to their God?

If under divine command theory humans cannot themselves assess whether an action is moral or immoral, doesn’t divine command theory equate to mere obedience to their God?

ChatGPT response · editorial edition

Divine Command Theory (DCT) is a family of accounts grounding moral requirements in divine commands. In an obligation-focused version, what God commands is obligatory and what God forbids is wrong; not every permissible or admirable act must therefore be commanded. Claims about goodness more broadly require separate treatment.

Your question touches on a critical discussion within the philosophy of religion and ethics regarding the nature of morality under Divine Command Theory. If humans are incapable of assessing the morality of an action independently of God’s commands, then, as you suggest, DCT might seem to reduce morality to mere obedience to God. This perspective raises several points of discussion:

  1. Autonomy and theonomy: Theonomy locates moral authority in God. It does not by itself exclude reasoning about duties. Under the question’s stronger assumption that humans cannot assess morality at all, obedience lacks an independently accessible test; that assumption is not shared by every divine command theory.
  2. Euthyphro challenge: Plato’s dialogue asks about holiness and the gods’ love. Its modern adaptation asks whether commands constitute moral requirements or respond to prior moral reasons. An unrestricted command theory faces an arbitrariness objection; modified versions appeal to divine character.
  3. Moral knowledge: How one identifies a duty is distinct from what makes it binding. A theorist can allow conscience, reasoning, and experience as fallible routes to duties allegedly grounded in commands. Revelation and religious authority raise additional questions of authenticity and interpretation.
  4. Obedience and Moral Development: Some critics argue that reducing morality to obedience might inhibit moral development and the cultivation of virtues like empathy, compassion, and understanding. If morality is solely about following commands, there might be less incentive to develop a deeper understanding of why certain actions are right or wrong.

In summary, while Divine Command Theory emphasizes obedience to divine will as the basis for morality, it raises significant philosophical and ethical questions about the nature of moral autonomy, the source of moral values, and the process of moral reasoning and development.

Gemini response · editorial edition

You’ve identified a key point of contention with divine command theory (DCT). Here’s a breakdown of the issue and some different perspectives:

The Argument for Obedience:

  • An obligation-focused DCT grounds requirements and prohibitions in divine commands. This need not identify all goodness, virtue, or value with obedience.
  • If one also assumes humans have no independent access to moral reasons, the objection about mere obedience follows. Dependence on commands as a foundation alone does not establish that additional assumption.

The Challenge of Blind Obedience:

  • An unrestricted command theory must explain why an order creates moral authority and whether contrary orders could reverse duties. This objection does not establish that every commanded action lacks value independently of its being commanded.
  • Additionally, some divine commands in certain religious texts seem morally questionable from a modern perspective. How can followers reconcile these with the idea of absolute obedience?

Variations of DCT:

  • Some versions allow human reasoning to identify duties while commands ground their authority. If a wholly independent moral standard itself determines obligation, however, that moves away from command-based grounding.
  • Robert Adams’s modified account connects wrongness with the commands of a loving God. In his discussion of divine command theory, cruelty for its own sake does not become binding merely because an alleged deity commands it. The critic can still ask how love and authentic commands are recognized.

Open Questions:

  • How much independent judgment do humans have within DCT?
  • Can humans understand the reasons behind God’s commands, or are they simply pronouncements to be obeyed?
  • How can DCT account for the evolution of moral understanding over time?

Ultimately, the question of whether DCT equates to mere obedience depends on the specific interpretation of the theory. There’s ongoing debate about the role of reason and the nature of God’s commands within the framework of DCT.

Prompt 2: If divine command theory does equate to mere obedience to the divine commands of an inscrutable God, can adherents to the theory ever detect a threshold beyond which the God in question will never go?

If divine command theory does equate to mere obedience to the divine commands of an inscrutable God, can adherents to the theory ever detect a threshold beyond which the God in question will never go?

Here are a few suggested thresholds relevant to known religions:

  • A command to kill those who apostatize.
  • A command to throw infants off the top of mountains.
  • A command to hack the infants of another tribe into pieces.
  • A command to rape.

Could any command, such as those above, from the God in question ever be evidence that the God is evil?

ChatGPT response · editorial edition

The threshold question is strongest under the stipulated combination of unrestricted command authority and complete moral inscrutability. The curator’s examples are preserved as tests; their mention does not establish that each is a verified command in a specified religion.

The Threshold of Divine Commands

In a theory that makes any divine order decisive without accessible constraints, there is no independent moral threshold available to the adherent. A modified theory can impose a constraint such as a necessarily loving divine character, but must explain how humans recognize it and distinguish revelation from error.

Challenges and Critiques

  1. Dependence and variability: Dependence on divine will is not the same as dependence on human opinion or culture. A theory might make duties objective relative to human attitudes while grounding them in a divine agent. Whether commands can vary is a further question.
  2. Reason and conscience: Horrific alleged commands can challenge either their authenticity or the theory used to justify them. Using moral judgment to recognize commands does not by itself establish that commands cannot ground obligation; it does create a need to explain the reliability of that recognition.
  3. The Concept of a Just and Loving God: Many religious traditions hold that God is just and loving. Commands to commit acts that appear intrinsically harmful and unjust challenge this conception of divinity. This brings up the question of whether such commands could ever be consistent with the nature of a benevolent deity, and if not, whether their purported divine origin should be questioned.
  4. Evidence: A cruel alleged order can count against its source’s goodness if “good” has content that excludes such cruelty. If goodness means whatever the source commands, the same test is unavailable. Keep these different definitions explicit.

Theological Responses

Theological responses to these dilemmas vary. Some theologians argue that divine commands are always aimed at a greater good that humans might not understand due to their limited perspective. Others suggest that genuinely understanding God’s will involves interpreting divine commands in the context of the broader moral and theological framework, considering God’s overall nature as loving and just.

In essence, whether any command from God could be evidence of divine malevolence is a deeply contentious question. It hinges on interpretations of divine nature, the source and structure of morality, and the balance between divine sovereignty and human moral understanding. Philosophers and theologians continue to wrestle with these questions, often coming to very different conclusions based on their underlying assumptions and beliefs.

Gemini response · editorial edition

That’s a very interesting question. If divine command theory (DCT) boils down to blind obedience to an unknowable God, then detecting a moral threshold becomes a significant challenge. Here’s how different perspectives might approach this:

Difficulties in Detecting a Threshold:

  • Inscrutability: A possible unknown reason is not evidence that a particular order is authentic or justified. If no conceivable order could count against a claim of divine goodness, the theory needs to explain what that claim enables us to assess.
  • Trust: Trust in a perfectly good God presupposes an account of goodness and grounds for identifying the speaker. It cannot by itself establish that an alleged command comes from that God.
  • Interpretation: Genre, language, and context can matter, but a nonliteral interpretation needs textual reasons. An uncomfortable command cannot simply be relabeled metaphorical whenever convenient.

Potential Detection of a Threshold:

  • Substantive limits: Principles protecting innocent people can be used to test alleged commands, provided their authority and relevance are defended. No claim of universal agreement is needed for that argument.
  • Conflict: Conscience can provide a reason to scrutinize an alleged command. Internal distress alone proves neither that a command is false nor that a good deity could never permit disagreement.
  • Inconsistency with Revelation: If a command contradicts existing scriptures or teachings believed to be from the same God, it could raise doubts about the authenticity of the new command or the benevolence of the being issuing it.

The Problem of Evil:

  • This concept highlights the difficulty of reconciling a good God with the existence of evil in the world. If God is all-powerful and all-good, why would such commands exist? DCT struggles to answer this question definitively.

Conclusion:

Within a strict interpretation of DCT with an unknowable God, detecting a moral threshold is very difficult. However, some argue for potential signs based on the nature of the command, internal conflict, or inconsistencies with existing revelations. Ultimately, the question remains a point of debate within the framework of DCT.

ChatGPT response · editorial edition

The prompt’s phrase “morality is simply disobedience” appears to intend “immorality is disobedience”; the original wording is retained above. The substantive issue is whether identifying what makes an act wrong also explains the meaning of wrongness.

  1. Meaning and grounding: A theory may identify wrongness with contrariety to divine requirements without claiming that ordinary speakers mean “disobedience” whenever they say “wrong.” An explanatory identity is not automatically a dictionary definition.
  2. Broader Moral Considerations: Even among adherents of DCT, there is often an acknowledgment that moral understanding involves more than just recognizing and following divine commands. It may also include considerations of virtue, the well-being of others, and the development of a moral character that seeks to embody qualities valued by the divine, such as compassion, justice, and love. These aspects of morality suggest an engagement with moral principles that is more complex than simple obedience.
  3. Moral Intuition and Reason: Many people, including those who might accept a version of DCT, recognize the role of moral intuition and reason in their ethical decision-making. They might argue that God has endowed humans with a sense of right and wrong and the capacity for moral reasoning to navigate complex ethical dilemmas. In this view, while divine commands are supremely authoritative, the ability to discern and understand these commands involves engaging with moral intuitions and reasoning that God has also provided.
  4. Interpretation and Context: The interpretation of divine commands itself often requires moral discernment. Sacred texts and religious teachings can be complex, requiring interpretation within specific contexts. This interpretive process acknowledges that understanding what constitutes obedience to God is not always straightforward and involves engaging with moral principles that can extend beyond a simple directive.
  5. Evolving Moral Understanding: Even within religious traditions that emphasize divine command, there is often recognition of the evolving understanding of morality over time. This evolution can reflect deeper insights into the divine will, changing human circumstances, and a growing understanding of the implications of moral principles in diverse contexts.

The critique remains: an account of authority must make the moral significance of obedience intelligible. But a theory of obligation can coexist with an account of virtue, value, and fallible human reasoning; complexity of practice alone does not refute it.

Gemini response · editorial edition

There is a real question about what moral language explains, but the conclusion needs qualification. Shared reference, ordinary meaning, and the grounds of obligation are different claims.

The Challenge of Defining Morality:

  • A command theory can concern obligation without defining all moral value as obedience. State which version is under examination before extending its claims.

The Problem of Intrinsic Morality:

  • Ordinary judgments often involve welfare, fairness, and reasons beyond explicit orders. That fact challenges an account that dismisses them, but it does not alone establish a foundation independent of God.

Reconciling the Two Views:

  • A theorist may allow reason to discover requirements grounded in commands. If independent reasons instead fully constitute those requirements, the view differs from command-based grounding.
  • Others suggest God’s commands are inherently good because they flow from God’s perfect character. Following God’s commands naturally leads to moral behavior because God is good.

The Role of Human Understanding:

  • The key question becomes – how much independent moral judgment do humans have within DCT? Can we ever understand the reasons behind God’s commands, or are they simply pronouncements to be obeyed?

Evidence of Independent Morality:

  • Questioning an alleged command shows that the person uses some evaluative standard. It does not establish that the standard’s ultimate foundation is independent of divine authority; the theorist may treat it as a fallible route to that authority.

The Issue of Evolving Morality:

  • Changing moral judgments need not mean changing commands. A defender can distinguish revised interpretations and changed circumstances from changes in the underlying duties, while critics can ask whether that explanation fits the evidence.

Conclusion:

The tension between the obedience-based definition of morality in DCT and our intuitive sense of right and wrong is a major point of discussion. Some versions of DCT try to reconcile these views, but the question of how much independent moral judgment humans have remains a challenge for the theory.

ChatGPT response · editorial edition

A conflict with an alleged command poses a serious test. But one mistaken moral intuition would refute infallibility, not prove that the experience was not a moral intuition at all or that all intuitions are unreliable.

  1. Many people strongly condemn intentionally killing innocent infants. That supplies the case’s moral challenge without establishing a universal, innate response in every human being.
  2. If a genuine command and a particular intuition conflict, both cannot be correct under the relevant command theory. Before inferring which fails, examine authenticity, interpretation, the theory itself, and the grounds for the intuition.
  3. Interpretation of Divine Commands and Moral Intuitions: Some might argue that apparent conflicts between divine commands and moral intuitions necessitate a reevaluation of how these commands are interpreted or understood. This approach could suggest that true divine commands would not contravene fundamental moral principles imbued in humans by the divine. Alternatively, it might suggest a misunderstanding or misinterpretation of the divine will.
  4. A source of judgment can be fallible yet informative. The question is how errors are detected and when intuition deserves weight, not whether any counterexample eliminates its status as a moral intuition.
  5. Philosophical and Theological Implications: This tension highlights a broader philosophical and theological debate about the nature of morality, the existence and character of the divine, and the capacity of human beings to comprehend and align with divine morality. It challenges theologians and philosophers to consider whether and how divine morality is accessible to human understanding and what the implications are for moral action when divine commands and human moral intuitions conflict.

One established error defeats a claim of infallibility. It does not by itself defeat all reliability. A serious defense must still explain how the adherent distinguishes mistaken intuitions from false claims of revelation.

Gemini response · editorial edition

You raise a great point. Here’s how adherents of Divine Command Theory (DCT) might grapple with the clash between moral intuitions and seemingly immoral divine commands:

Challenges to Moral Intuitions:

  • Overriding aversion: An adherent might appeal to a greater purpose, but an unspecified purpose supplies no independent evidence for the order. A critical account must explain why that appeal is justified in this case.
  • Limited perspective: Limited knowledge is compatible with both true and false alleged revelations. It cannot establish which one is present.

Defense of Moral Intuitions:

  • Strong condemnation: The objection need not claim unanimous human judgment. Defended reasons against harming innocent infants can challenge an alleged command or an unrestricted theory.
  • Conscience: Conflict calls for examination; it does not establish that a good God could never allow human uncertainty or mistaken judgment.
  • Context: Interpretation requires evidence from the text and circumstances. Modified command theories can also deny that a loving God could issue certain orders, making authenticity a central question.

The Problem of Evil:

  • This concept highlights the difficulty of reconciling a good God with the existence of evil in the world. If God is all-powerful and all-good, why would such commands exist? DCT struggles to answer this definitively.

The Question of Limits:

  • The example you raise pushes DCT to its limits. Can any command, no matter how heinous, be justified by blind obedience to a divine being? This is a point of ongoing debate within the theory.

Conclusion:

The enduring difficulty is how to recognize legitimate authority and reliable reasons under uncertainty. The alternatives include rejecting the alleged command or the theory, not only overriding one’s conscience.

15 Discussion Prompts on Divine Command Theory and Morality:

  1. Beyond Blind Obedience: Can Divine Command Theory (DCT) accommodate any role for human reason in moral decision-making, or does it require strict adherence to God’s commands regardless of their perceived consequences?
  2. The Limits of Morality: If morality is solely based on God’s commands, can there ever be genuinely new moral issues that require interpretation or adaptation within the framework of DCT?
  3. Universal Morality vs. Cultural Influence: DCT suggests a universal moral code dictated by God. How does this reconcile with the existence of diverse moral codes across different cultures and religions?
  4. The Problem of Evil Revisited: If God is all-powerful and all-good, why would there be suffering and evil in the world? How does DCT address this challenge?
  5. Moral Progress: Throughout history, human societies have outlawed practices once considered acceptable, like slavery or torture. Can DCT account for this evolution of moral understanding?
  6. Interpreting the Commands: Some versions of DCT allow for interpretation of divine commands. Who gets to decide the “correct” interpretation, and how does this avoid subjective bias?
  7. Faith vs. Reason: Can faith and reason co-exist in navigating moral dilemmas within the framework of DCT? If so, how does one determine when to prioritize one over the other?
  8. The Challenge of Seemingly Immoral Commands: How can adherents of DCT reconcile their moral intuitions with commands that appear to violate core moral principles, such as harming the innocent?
  9. The Role of Religious Texts: How do different interpretations of religious texts influence the application of DCT in real-world situations?
  10. Personal vs. Societal Morality: Does DCT suggest that morality is primarily a personal matter of obedience to God, or does it extend to shaping societal norms and laws?
  11. The Burden of Obedience: Consider the psychological implications of basing one’s morality entirely on external commands. Could this lead to a sense of powerlessness or a diminished sense of personal responsibility?
  12. DCT and Different Religions: How does DCT apply to religions with seemingly contradictory moral codes? Can multiple religions all be following a divine source of morality even if their teachings differ?
  13. The Value of Debate: While DCT provides a clear source of moral authority, does it stifle ethical discussions and critical thinking about moral dilemmas?
  14. DCT in a Modern World: In a world with diverse moral perspectives, can DCT still be a relevant framework for ethical decision-making?
