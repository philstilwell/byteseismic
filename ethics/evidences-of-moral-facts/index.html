Prompt 1: Could what humans consider to be their “moral intuitions” be simply their emotions coupled with socially-derived expectations? Would that not be a far more parsimonious explanation than invoking an entire moral realm?

Moral intuitions and emotional explanation

ChatGPT response · editorial edition

Your question touches on a significant debate in the fields of ethics and moral psychology, one that examines the origins and nature of moral intuitions. The suggestion that moral intuitions could be primarily the result of emotions intertwined with socially-derived expectations offers a naturalistic explanation for how humans discern right from wrong without appealing to the existence of an objective moral realm. This viewpoint aligns with several theories in moral psychology and evolutionary ethics, and we can explore both the arguments for this perspective and the implications of adopting it.

Emotions and Social Expectations in Moral Intuitions

  1. Emotional Foundations of Moral Judgments:
  • Emotional foundations: the social-intuitionist model emphasizes rapid evaluative intuitions and the frequent social or justificatory role of reasoning. An intuition is not simply identical to an emotion, and the model does not claim that every moral judgment is an after-the-fact rationalization.
  1. Socially-Derived Expectations:
  • Humans are inherently social creatures, and much of our understanding of what is “right” and “wrong” is learned through social interactions and cultural norms. From this perspective, moral intuitions are seen as internalized versions of societal rules and expectations, which are shaped by the cultural and social context one grows up in. This can explain the variability of moral norms across different societies and time periods.
  1. Evolutionary Perspectives:
  • Evolutionary theories of morality suggest that moral intuitions may have developed as adaptive responses to promote group cohesion and cooperation, which were beneficial for our ancestors’ survival. This view posits that emotions such as empathy, guilt, and indignation are evolutionary products that guide social behavior in ways that typically align with what is considered moral in a given group.

Critiques and Limitations

While the explanation based on emotions and social expectations is parsimonious and grounded in empirical research, there are several critiques and limitations to consider:

  1. Complexity of Moral Reasoning:
  • Some philosophers and psychologists argue that reducing moral intuitions to emotions and social expectations oversimplifies the complexity of moral reasoning. They point out that individuals often engage in principled reasoning that goes beyond immediate emotional responses and societal norms, especially in novel or complex situations.
  1. Existence of Universal Moral Principles:
  • Cross-cultural overlap: some realists appeal to shared judgments, but common needs, learning and evolved dispositions are rival explanations. The agreement must be specified and its independence assessed before it can discriminate between accounts.
  1. Moral Progress and Critique:
  • Moral change: a psychological account can explain criticism of prevailing norms through competing emotions, new information and reflection. Whether a change is objectively better is a further normative question; the occurrence of reform alone does not answer it.

Your explanation is a serious competitor because it uses familiar mechanisms to explain why moral claims feel compelling. Parsimony favors it only if explanatory coverage and fit are comparable. Explaining the origin of a judgment is also distinct from deciding whether its content is true.

  • Parsimony: Your explanation is simpler, relying on known psychological mechanisms. This appeals to some philosophers.
  • Challenge: the emotional account must explain both conformity and reflective resistance to prevailing norms. A feeling of rightness is part of what needs explaining; simply declaring that emotion cannot explain it would beg the question.

Alternative view: moral realists hold that some moral claims are true in a relevantly objective sense. Many naturalist realists identify moral properties with natural properties; realism does not always posit a separate realm. See the Stanford Encyclopedia account.

The Debate Continues: There’s no consensus on the origin of moral intuitions. It’s likely a complex interplay of emotions, social learning, and perhaps even evolved predispositions.
