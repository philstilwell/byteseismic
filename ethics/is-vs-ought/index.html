Prompt 1: Present a rigorous formulation of the is/ought problem.

Formulating the inference problem

ChatGPT response · editorial edition

The is/ought problem, also known as Hume’s guillotine, is a philosophical question that concerns the relationship between descriptive statements (what is) and prescriptive or normative statements (what ought to be). This problem is central to meta-ethics and was prominently articulated by the Scottish philosopher David Hume. Here is a rigorous formulation of the is/ought problem:

Background

In Book III of A Treatise of Human Nature, published in 1740 after Books I–II in 1739, Hume challenges an unexplained transition from descriptions to obligations. The passage is 3.1.1.27. The formal formulation below is a reconstruction of the inference problem, not a claim that Hume supplied this notation.

Hume’s Observation

Hume points out that many writers make claims about what ought to be on the basis of what is, but he notes a significant gap in their reasoning. According to Hume, the premises in their arguments typically consist entirely of ‘is’ statements, but their conclusions suddenly switch to ‘ought’ statements without justification.

Quote from Hume

In A Treatise of Human Nature, Hume writes (the original post’s modernized transcription is retained):

“In every system of morality, which I have hitherto met with, I have always remarked, that the author proceeds for some time in the ordinary ways of reasoning, and establishes the being of a God, or makes observations concerning human affairs; when all of a sudden I am surprised to find, that instead of the usual copulations of propositions, is, and is not, I meet with no proposition that is not connected with an ought, or an ought not. This change is imperceptible; but is however, of the last consequence. For as this ought, or ought not, expresses some new relation or affirmation, ’tis necessary that it should be observed and explained; and at the same time that a reason should be given; for what seems altogether inconceivable, how this new relation can be a deduction from others, which are entirely different from it.”

Philosophical Implications

  1. Logical gap: Let D be a consistent set of descriptive premises and O a substantive obligation. D entails O only if every interpretation making D true also makes O true. If two interpretations agree on all of D but disagree on O, D does not entail O. Treating the normative vocabulary as independently interpretable yields this result; whether a defensible naturalistic identification removes that independence is part of the dispute.
  2. Need for a bridge: “This action causes avoidable suffering” does not by itself yield “We ought not perform it.” Add “Actions causing avoidable suffering ought not be performed,” and the conclusion follows. That repairs this inference, but the added principle still needs defense, including its scope and exceptions. The general warning concerns substantive conclusions from consistent premises: contradictions entail anything in classical logic, and merely inserting “ought” into a disjunction proves no obligation.
  3. Meta-Ethical Consequences: This issue raises significant questions about the objectivity and grounding of moral statements. It challenges the view that moral truths can be derived solely from empirical facts.
  4. Ethical theorizing: Facts can inform, test and apply moral reasoning. The challenge is to explain the authority of its evaluative commitments or defend an identification of moral and natural properties. The gap alone does not show that values are subjective, that moral reasoning is impossible, or that every justification must exclude empirical evidence.

Conclusion

Hume’s guillotine suggests that ethical reasoning requires a careful examination of the premises and their logical connectivity. It challenges philosophers, ethicists, and indeed anyone engaged in moral reasoning, to clearly distinguish between how things are and how they believe things should be, thereby underlining the need for robust normative justifications in ethical arguments.

The Is/Ought Problem: A Rigorous Formulation

The is/ought problem, championed by Scottish philosopher David Hume, highlights the difficulty of deriving normative statements (“oughts”) from purely factual statements (“is”). Here’s a rigorous breakdown:

1. Types of Statements:

  • Is-statements (descriptive) purport to describe what is the case. They can be true or false, uncertain or difficult to test; being descriptive does not guarantee objectivity or verification. Examples:
    • The grass is green. (Observable fact)
    • All bachelors are unmarried men. (Logical definition)
  • Ought-statements (normative) assess or prescribe conduct. Their objectivity is disputed, not ruled out by their grammar. Moral requirements and advice relative to a goal should be distinguished. Examples:
    • You ought to help those in need. (Moral value)
    • If you aim to protect your health, you ought to choose suitable foods. (Advice relative to a goal; it is not automatically a moral duty.)
    • Stealing is wrong. (Obligation)

2. The Gap:

A rigorous test is whether the descriptive premises could all hold while the proposed obligation fails. Suppose a community approves of a practice. Unless a premise connects approval to moral authority, that description remains compatible with condemning the practice. Agreement about the facts therefore need not settle the obligation.

  • Can we logically bridge this gap and derive “oughts” solely from “is”?

3. Hume’s Fork:

Hume’s distinction between relations of ideas and matters of fact provides background to his philosophy. It is not another name for the is/ought distinction: one concerns kinds of knowledge, the other a proposed inference from descriptions to requirements.

  • Relations of Ideas: These are truths based on logic and definitions (e.g., all bachelors are unmarried). They hold true regardless of the world’s state.
  • Matters of Fact: These are truths based on observation and experience (e.g., the grass is green). They depend on the world being a certain way.

4. The Challenge:

To derive an obligation, identify every premise and explain why the conclusion follows. If “good,” “rational” or “proper” occurs in a premise, ask whether its meaning already includes evaluation. Calling the premise factual does not make it normatively neutral.

  • For example: “Cooperation enables survival; therefore we ought to cooperate” requires an account of why survival has the relevant authority and when cooperation is required. A useful causal explanation has not yet supplied that account.

5. The Problem:

The failure of a purely descriptive derivation leaves further questions open; it does not answer them by itself:

  • Are moral judgments subjective and arbitrary?
  • Is there any objective basis for morality?

Conclusion:

The is/ought problem remains a central debate in ethics. While there’s no universally accepted solution, philosophers continue to explore how to bridge the gap between facts and values.

Prompt 2: Provide rigorous formulation of the best arguments against the proposed irreconcilable is/ought divide.

Proposed crossings and what they require

ChatGPT response · editorial edition

The following five approaches challenge different claims: the independence of moral properties, the isolation of moral judgment from action, or the adequacy of a facts-only picture of practical reasoning. Here are their premises and disputed steps. They are not five demonstrated deductions of obligation from neutral facts.

  1. Naturalistic Ethics:
    • Argument: (1) Suppose a moral property M is identical with a natural property N. (2) An action has N. (3) It therefore has M. This is valid if the identity in (1) is warranted. The naturalist must defend an identity, not merely observe that people approve of N.
    • Philosophical basis: Ethical naturalism identifies or grounds moral properties in natural properties. Some versions are reductive, others are not. An identity may be offered as a substantive discovery rather than a definition; its epistemic warrant remains a live issue.
    • Example: A proposed identification of badness with a kind of suffering would let evidence of that suffering support a moral judgment. Evidence that pain is disliked establishes only a psychological fact unless the identification is separately defended. Pain’s warning function also cautions against a simple equation of all pain with what ought to be eliminated.
  2. Internalism in Meta-Ethics:
    • Argument: (1) On motivational internalism, sincerely judging that one morally ought to act has a necessary connection to motivation, often subject to conditions such as practical rationality. (2) A person makes that judgment under those conditions. (3) Some relevant motivation follows. This connects judgment to motivation; it does not derive the judgment’s truth from a neutral fact.
    • Philosophical basis: The thesis concerns the relation between moral judgment and motivation. It does not say that everyone who notices suffering must care, or that motivation proves an obligation.
    • Example: Someone who judges that they ought to relieve a person’s pain may thereby be motivated to help. Merely noticing pain supplies neither that moral judgment nor proof that helping is required in the circumstances.
  3. Pragmatic Integration:
    • Argument: (1) Practical inquiry starts with problems and evaluative commitments. (2) Evidence can expose failures and support revising means and ends. (3) Values can therefore be subject to informed criticism rather than insulated from facts. This contests a separation of inquiry into sealed factual and evaluative compartments, not the validity test for deduction.
    • Philosophical basis: A pragmatist can defend norms through their role in reflective practice and the consequences of acting on them. A critic can still ask why the selected consequences count as improvements. Practical success alone is not a proof of categorical moral authority.
    • Example: A community committed to fair treatment may revise a policy when evidence reveals exclusion. The evidence changes its judgment through an existing standard of fairness; the authority of that standard remains open to examination.
  4. Constructivism in Ethics:
    • Argument: (1) A specified procedure of practical reasoning is claimed to be authoritative. (2) Principles P follow from that procedure. (3) P are justified for the agents within its scope. The first premise carries the burden; actual agreement alone does not establish it.
    • Philosophical basis: Ethical constructivists seek justification through practical reasoning under specified constraints. They differ over the procedure and its scope. Consistency, impartiality and hypothetical agreement need explanation rather than being treated as self-certifying facts.
    • Example: If a fair deliberative procedure rejects gratuitous harm, its defender must explain why the procedure is fair, why its constraints are warranted and whom its outcome binds. Counting votes is insufficient.
  5. Rule-Based Consequentialism:
    • Argument: (1) We ought to follow rules selected by a specified standard of consequences. (2) Evidence favors rule R under that standard. (3) We ought to follow R, within the theory’s qualifications. This uses an explicit evaluative premise; it is not a derivation from outcomes alone.
    • Philosophical basis: Rule consequentialism evaluates rules or codes by their consequences under specified conditions of acceptance or compliance. The measure of value and the treatment of competing interests must be defended.
    • Example: Evidence that honesty supports trust is relevant to evaluating rules. A demand to tell the truth still requires a value premise and attention to cases such as protecting someone from an attacker. “Society flourishes” must also specify whose welfare counts.

Naturalism directly challenges a sharp separation between moral and natural properties. The other approaches illuminate justification, motivation or inquiry, but the formulations above do not establish an obligation from neutral facts alone. The curator’s next question appropriately tests the premises doing the normative work.

Gemini response · editorial edition

The source’s six approaches are retained below, with their arguments clarified. Error theory and emotivism are alternatives to certain accounts of moral truth, not successful bridges to objective obligation.

1. Natural Law Arguments:

  • Natural law: (1) Human goods and practical reason supply standards for action. (2) A proposed act accords with or frustrates those standards. (3) This supports a practical judgment about it. The disputed work lies in (1): a theory of human nature must explain why its goods have authority. Natural law is not a scientific law discovered by measurement.
  • Natural goodness: A related approach assesses capacities and conduct in relation to forms of human life. It must defend the move from functioning well as a human being to reasons for action. The fact that humans cooperate cannot by itself establish that every cooperative project is good; oppressive projects also require cooperation.

2. Error Theory:

  • Correction: Moral error theory does not identify moral truths with hidden facts about flourishing. It holds that ordinary positive moral judgments attribute properties that are not instantiated, so those judgments are systematically mistaken. Mackie is a central example; Moore defended non-naturalist realism. Error theory therefore rejects a target of the proposed bridge rather than constructing that bridge.

3. Emotivism:

  • Moral Statements Express Feelings: Emotivists suggest that “ought” statements don’t describe the world; they express emotions and attitudes towards certain actions. They function more like exclamations (“Boo to stealing!”) than factual claims. (Thinkers like A.J. Ayer)

4. Pragmatist Arguments:

  • Pragmatic argument: If ethical inquiry can revise commitments in light of experience, facts and values interact in practical reasoning. But “This produces desirable outcomes” includes an evaluation of desirability. A defense must explain that evaluation, not merely report the outcome. This challenges a sterile separation of inquiry without proving an unconditional duty from neutral descriptions.

5. Moral Psychology:

  • Moral psychology: Evidence about emotion and reasoning can explain how judgments form and expose bias. To conclude that a psychologically produced judgment is justified requires a further account of reliability or authority. Explaining why a conviction is strong does not show that it is true.

6. Value-laden Descriptions:

  • Value-laden descriptions: “Cruel” combines a description of conduct with condemnation in ordinary use. An inference from cruelty to a reason against an act may therefore draw on evaluation already in the premise. “Wealthy” is a poor example of inherent moral praise: wealth can be described without approving it. Mixed vocabulary challenges a simple linguistic division, not necessarily the underlying logical warning.

A further, direct challenge uses institutional facts. In Searle’s discussion of deriving ought from is, promising is central: a valid promise is said to place its maker under an obligation. The critic asks whether “promise” already includes a normative commitment and whether an institutional obligation establishes an overriding moral duty. This makes the disputed step visible instead of treating agreement as sufficient.

The curator’s challenge to axioms and emotional accounts

ChatGPT response · editorial edition

Your objection identifies two different failures in the earlier list: importing an undefended standard, and replacing objective obligation with an account of attitudes. Neither is a demonstrated derivation of an objective ought from neutral descriptions. Some approaches may still offer independent arguments, so the next task is to assess those arguments rather than count them as refutations.

1. Reliance on Unsubstantiated Axioms or Intersubjective Facts

Many of the arguments against the is/ought divide do indeed seem to rely on some form of normative assumption or axiom that itself may not be justifiable purely through empirical or descriptive means. This challenge can be seen as follows:

  • Naturalistic ethics: If the premise merely says that well-being is good, its authority has been assumed. A stronger naturalist proposes an identity between a moral and natural property and argues for it. Calling that identity an axiom does not refute it; calling it a fact does not establish it. The evidence and explanatory case must bear the weight.
  • Internalism: A connection between moral judgment and motivation does not certify the judgment. One can be sincerely motivated by a mistaken belief. The earlier presentation incorrectly treated motivation as a bridge to truth.
  • Constructivism: Actual agreement establishes an intersubjective fact: people agree. A constructivist who invokes an ideal procedure owes an account of its constraints, authority and scope. Without that account, the conclusion is conditional on accepting the procedure, not a demonstration binding every dissenter.

These objections show where justification remains outstanding. They do not require denying that empirical facts matter, or that practical reasoning can be coherent. They prevent a successful inference inside a moral framework from being mistaken for a proof of the framework itself.

2. Reduction of Moral Statements to Expressions of Emotions

Your second objection applies most directly to emotivism. It changes the account of what a moral utterance does, rather than discovering an attitude-independent moral property:

  • Emotivism and related theories: Expressing disapproval differs from reporting “I disapprove.” The report can be true or false as a psychological statement; the expression need not assert such a fact. An emotivist therefore does not deliver the objective obligation demanded here. More developed accounts must also explain moral reasoning and disagreement, not merely emotional intensity.
  • Pragmatism and rule consequentialism: These do not automatically reduce morality to emotion. A consequentialist may be a realist about value. The relevant challenge is the warrant for the value standard, while a pragmatist must explain what makes a revision an improvement. Their labels alone settle neither question.

A critic can reasonably say that an account of attitudes has not supplied the requested objective authority. But the authority in question also needs specification: social enforcement, motivational force and truth independent of attitudes are different claims. Explaining one is not establishing the others.

Conclusion

The strongest version of your challenge is: identify the alleged source of authority, show where it enters the premises, and defend it without merely repeating the desired conclusion. On that test, the earlier list overstated its success. Acknowledging this leaves room for evaluating more developed defenses without declaring the gap closed.

Gemini response · editorial edition

The earlier answer overstated several proposals and misidentified error theory. Your criticism warrants correction, not a claim that partial agreement has somehow narrowed a logical gap.

1. Unsubstantiated Axioms and Intersubjective Facts:

  • Natural law and the error-theory correction: Natural law needs a defense of its account of goods and practical authority. Error theory denies the relevant objective values; it cannot provide the hidden factual grounding previously attributed to it. These are different positions, not two versions of the same bridge.

2. Moral Statements as Mere Emotions:

  • Emotivism: An account of moral utterances as expressions of attitudes does not establish attitude-independent obligation. Its challenge is also to explain inference and disagreement: “If lying is wrong, inducing a child to lie is wrong” must function in an argument even when no one is currently expressing disapproval. Treating every utterance as an isolated emotional outburst is inadequate.

These theories can clarify the dispute without partially completing an invalid deduction. The useful gains are more specific:

  • Natural law and naturalistic realism: A defended account of goods or of moral properties could provide reasons for accepting a foundation. Merely granting objectivity for the sake of argument gives a conditional result, not evidence that the foundation is true. Error theory remains a contrasting denial.
  • Emotivism: It offers an account of the practical and expressive role of moral language. That is explanatory work, but it should not be advertised as the objective foundation you requested.

Keeping the questions distinct:

Agreement can help people coordinate; emotion can explain motivation; evidence can correct factual beliefs. None of those achievements by itself establishes that a moral demand is categorically binding.

  • Moral judgments likely involve both descriptive elements (e.g., facts about human well-being) and prescriptive elements (e.g., moral principles).
  • Moral psychology concerns how people reach judgments. To use its findings to justify a judgment, one must explain why the relevant process tracks truth or satisfies a defensible standard.

Your central demand therefore stands unanswered by a bare appeal to consensus, usefulness or feeling. A defender must supply and defend the missing account of authority. Recognizing this is compatible with practical deliberation under openly stated values; it does not require pretending that those values have already been objectively grounded.

Is/Ought Problem Discussion Questions:

  1. Do you think there’s a fundamental difference between facts and values, or is there a spectrum between them? Why or why not?

  4. How can emotions play a positive role in moral decision-making? Are there any downsides to emotions influencing our morality?
  5. Can you think of any examples of “value-laden descriptions” in everyday life? How might these influence our judgments?
  6. In your opinion, does the is/ought problem make morality subjective and unreliable? Why or why not?
  7. If moral statements are simply expressions of feeling, can we ever have genuine moral disagreements? Explain your position.
  8. Does the is/ought problem pose a bigger challenge for religious ethics or secular ethics? Why do you think so?
