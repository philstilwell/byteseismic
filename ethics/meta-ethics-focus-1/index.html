Prompt 2: ChatGPT suggest that if Tim knows he is in violation of social norms, he could still be morally culpable for sleeping with his cousin in the absence of his awareness it is contrary to moral facts. Isn’t this conflating social norms with any actual moral facts?

Isn’t this conflating social norms with any actual moral facts?

A likely objection is that people often feel the moral pull of a case before they can analyze it cleanly. The section should grant that first intuition while still asking what justifies it and where it may mislead.

The ethical pressure here is not only emotional intensity but justificatory discipline. A position may feel compassionate, scandalous, or obvious and still need a clearer account of what gives it authority.

Social Norms Often Reflect Morality Many social norms are grounded in underlying moral principles. For instance, the social norm against cousin marriage might stem from a moral concern about genetic risks or maintaining family boundaries.

Blurred Lines Between Social and Moral Sometimes, social norms and moral facts are so intertwined that violating one feels like violating the other. In Tim’s case, even if he’s unaware of a specific moral condemnation, the act might go against a deeply ingrained social norm that carries moral weight for him.

Social Norms Can Be Arbitrary Social norms can vary widely across cultures. What’s considered normal behavior in one place might be taboo in another. Morality, on the other hand, is often seen as having a more universal core.

Moral Culpability and Societal Awareness If culpability hinges on moral facts, then shouldn’t true moral violations carry a sting of conscience even if social norms aren’t involved? Imagine Tim discovering the genetic risks of cousin marriage on his own. Wouldn’t there be a moral dimension to his actions even if his society didn’t have a norm against it?

Moral Realism Often Emphasizes Universal Truths Moral realists typically argue for moral facts that transcend specific cultures or social norms.

Social Pressures Can Mimic Moral Culpability Even if Tim doesn’t feel a deep moral violation, the social consequences of his actions (disapproval, ostracization) might create a feeling similar to moral culpability.

You highlight an important aspect the distinction between proposed moral facts (which are subject to debate and interpretation) and actual, established moral facts (which are recognized as objectively true). According to moral realism, for moral culpability to be legitimately assigned, it would be necessary for the individual to have violated what are objectively understood as moral facts, not merely social norms or proposed moral facts.

This raises a significant challenge How do we establish what constitutes an actual moral fact? Within moral philosophy, this is a matter of considerable debate. Moral realists argue that moral facts can be discovered or understood through reason, moral intuition, or other means. Critics, such as moral relativists or subjectivists, deny that moral facts exist in this objective sense, suggesting instead that moral judgments are based on personal or cultural perspectives.

Moral Realism and Grounding Moral realists often argue that social norms are informed by, or even derive from, deeper moral principles. However, this doesn’t mean every social norm automatically translates to a proven moral fact.

Establishing Moral Facts Moral realists acknowledge the ongoing debate about how to identify and justify moral facts. There’s no single universally accepted method.

Culpability and Established Facts As you point out, within moral realism, culpability would hinge on established moral facts, not just on violating social norms. Someone can’t be legitimately blamed for violating an unestablished moral principle, even if it’s reflected in a social norm.

  1. Implications for Moral Culpability: If we accept the premise that actual moral facts must be established for legitimate moral culpability, then the assignment of culpability becomes contingent on the recognition and acceptance of certain moral truths.
  2. Semantic discipline: The page should ask whether the contested moral term names a fact, an attitude, a recommendation, a prohibition, or a social warning.
  3. Anti-realist burden: Denying stance-independent moral facts does not by itself erase reasons, empathy, law, criticism, or the need to live with other humans.
  4. Realist objection: If moral language is reconstructed as emotional or social practice, the page should explain why some condemnations feel non-negotiable.
  5. Practical residue: The question becomes how to criticize cruelty, coercion, or hypocrisy without pretending the criticism has floated down from an objective moral realm.
