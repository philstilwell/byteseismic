Prompt 1: List and define 30 key terms in the field of ethics.

Thirty terms from each response

ChatGPT response · editorial edition

  1. Ethics: The branch of philosophy dealing with values relating to human conduct, focusing on the rightness and wrongness of actions, the goodness and badness of motives and ends.
  2. Morality: Standards of right and wrong, and the practices and judgments organized around them. The word can describe a group’s actual code or the standards a theory argues are justified; acceptance alone does not establish justification.
  3. Virtue Ethics: Approaches that give character, cultivated virtues and practical judgment a central role in ethical life. They can consider rules and consequences without treating either as the sole foundation.
  4. Deontology: Approaches on which duties, rights or constraints help determine what is permissible independently of simply maximizing good consequences. This is not obedience to whatever rules happen to exist.
  5. Consequentialism: Approaches that ground moral assessment in consequences. Versions differ over which outcomes matter and whether they assess individual acts, rules or other objects.
  6. Utilitarianism: A family of consequentialist views giving impartial weight to well-being and seeking to maximize it overall. It is not merely a vote for whichever option benefits the largest number.
  7. Categorical Imperative: Kant’s unconditional principle of morality. Its universal-law formulation asks whether one can will the maxim of one’s action as a universal law; its humanity formulation requires treating persons as ends, never merely as means.
  8. Moral Relativism: Views on which moral truth or justification is relative to a framework, such as a culture’s standards. The observation that cultures disagree is a separate descriptive claim and does not by itself establish relativism.
  9. Social Contract Theory: Approaches that explain or justify moral or political arrangements through agreement. The agreement may be hypothetical or idealized; people need not have signed an actual contract.
  10. Natural Law Theory: Approaches that connect moral requirements with human nature, human goods and practical reason. They do not equate whatever occurs in nature with what ought to happen.
  11. Virtue: A quality considered morally good or desirable in a person, such as courage, integrity, and honesty.
  12. Justice: The fair treatment of persons, including questions of distribution, rights, procedures and responses to wrongdoing. A law’s existence does not by itself make it just.
  13. Eudaimonia: Often translated as happiness or welfare; a central concept in Aristotelian ethics, referring to the condition of human flourishing or living a fulfilling life.
  14. Autonomy: The right or condition of self-governance, especially in moral decision-making.
  15. Duty: Something an agent is required to do or refrain from doing. Moral and legal duties can overlap or conflict; identifying a duty requires stating its source and scope.
  16. Altruism: The principle or practice of concern for the welfare of others, often at one’s own expense.
  17. Moral Absolutism: The ethical belief that there are absolute standards against which moral questions can be judged, and that certain actions are right or wrong, regardless of the circumstances.
  18. Normative Ethics: Inquiry into standards for right action, good character and a good life, including what is required, permitted or admirable.
  19. Meta-ethics: The branch of ethics that seeks to understand the nature, scope, and meaning of moral judgment and ethical language.
  20. Applied Ethics: The practical application of moral considerations to specific problems in personal, social, and professional contexts.
  21. Moral Dilemma: A situation in which a person is faced with conflicting moral obligations and choosing one means failing another.
  22. Moral Realism: The position that ethical statements describe objective facts about the world, and that some moral claims are true regardless of individual or cultural beliefs.
  23. Moral Subjectivism: Views that make moral truth depend on subjects’ attitudes. Unlike noncognitivism, a subjectivist theory can treat moral judgments as truth-apt claims; the relevant subject and attitudes must be specified.
  24. Philanthropy: The desire and action to promote the welfare of others, usually expressed through generous financial donations to causes aimed at improving societal well-being.
  25. Integrity: The quality of being honest and having strong moral principles; moral uprightness.
  26. Empathy: The ability to understand and share the feelings of another person.
  27. Equity: Fairness or justice in the way people are treated, often considered in contexts of social inequality.
  28. Compassion: Sympathetic consciousness of others’ distress together with a desire to alleviate it.
  29. Pragmatism: A philosophical tradition that connects inquiry with practical consequences, experience and revisability. In ethics it encourages testing proposed solutions in lived situations, rather than identifying rightness with whatever is immediately convenient.
  30. Moral Nihilism: Usually, denial that objective moral values or requirements exist. A moral error theory says ordinary affirmative moral judgments purport to describe such facts but fail. This does not by itself eliminate preferences, compassion or social commitments.

Supplementary source term — Stewardship: The responsible overseeing and protection of something considered worth caring for and preserving, often related to environmental conservation and ethical management of resources. The original response supplied this thirty-first term; it is retained here after the requested thirty-item list.

Gemini response · editorial edition

30 Key Terms in Ethics

Here are thirty terms, including moral anti-realism. Some name ethical approaches, some name practical principles, and others name areas of inquiry; they are not thirty competing theories.

  1. Ethics: The branch of philosophy that studies the good, right, and proper course of action.
  2. Morality: Standards governing right and wrong conduct, used either to describe a person’s or society’s code or to identify the standards taken to be justified.
  3. Virtue Ethics: A theory that focuses on developing good character traits, such as courage, honesty, and compassion.
  4. Deontological Ethics: Approaches that give duties or constraints a role not reducible to maximizing good outcomes. Their rules need justification; following an unjust order is not automatically ethical.
  5. Consequentialism: Approaches that ground moral assessment in consequences, with disagreements about what counts as good and what should be evaluated.
  6. Utilitarianism: Consequentialist approaches that assess options by their contribution to overall well-being, counting each affected person impartially. A small majority’s gain need not outweigh a minority’s severe loss.
  7. Kantian Ethics: A deontological theory based on the ideas of Immanuel Kant, which emphasizes acting according to universal moral laws.
  8. Duty: An obligation to act in a certain way, often based on moral principles.
  9. Rights: Moral or legal entitlements that people have.
  10. Justice: Fair treatment and defensible arrangements for rights, resources, procedures and responsibility. Equal respect does not always require identical treatment, as differing needs can matter.
  11. Fairness: Impartiality and justness.
  12. Autonomy: The capacity or standing to govern one’s choices through reflection rather than coercion or manipulation. In some ethical contexts it also names a right to make those choices.
  13. Beneficence: The act of doing good or being helpful.
  14. Non-maleficence: The principle of avoiding doing harm.
  15. Verity: Truth or truthfulness. For the ethical duty of honest communication, “veracity” is the more usual term; it concerns avoiding deception, not merely uttering technically true sentences.
  16. Fidelity: Loyalty and faithfulness to commitments.
  17. Confidentiality: The responsibility to protect information entrusted in a relationship or role. Its scope and any justified exceptions must be explained rather than assuming all secrecy is ethical.
  18. Informed Consent: Voluntary authorization based on adequate understanding of relevant information, including material risks, benefits and alternatives, by a person with the capacity to decide. It does not require knowledge of every imaginable consequence.
  19. Ethical Dilemma: A conflict between moral requirements. In a strict dilemma, an agent cannot satisfy all applicable requirements; mere uncertainty about the right answer is not sufficient.
  20. Moral Distress: Distress associated with perceived moral constraints or wrongdoing. In its influential narrower usage, it arises when someone believes they know the right action but is prevented from taking it; broader definitions also exist.
  21. Ethical Egoism: The theory that the best course of action is the one that best serves one’s own interests.
  22. Altruism: The unselfish concern for the welfare of others.
  23. Moral Relativism: Views that make moral truth or justification relative to a framework, often cultural. Disagreement between cultures does not alone prove that view.
  24. Bioethics: The branch of ethics that deals with the ethical issues raised by advances in biology and medicine.
  25. Neuroethics: The field of ethics that studies the ethical implications of neuroscience research and technology.
  26. Environmental Ethics: The branch of ethics that examines the relationship between humans and the environment.
  27. Business Ethics: The standards of moral conduct that guide businesses and professionals in the marketplace.
  28. Professional Ethics: Moral responsibilities associated with a professional role. A written professional code is one expression of these responsibilities, and can itself need criticism or revision.
  29. Ethical Leadership: Leadership that is grounded in ethical principles and values.
  30. Moral Anti-Realism: The meta-ethical view that denies the existence of objective moral facts or truths. Moral anti-realists believe that moral statements do not describe mind-independent moral properties of the world.

On the narrower and broader senses of moral distress, see Andrew Jameton’s retrospective and Carina Fourie’s distinction.

Prompt 2: List and provide explanations of key concepts in ethics.

What the concepts help us distinguish

ChatGPT response · editorial edition

Ethics examines how we should act, what we should value and how such judgments can be justified. The following concepts answer different questions: some offer standards for conduct, others concern the status of moral judgments, and applied ethics names a form of inquiry.

  1. Moral Absolutism: The view that at least some moral requirements admit no exceptions. Distinguish this from moral objectivity: an objective standard could itself make circumstances relevant.
  2. Moral Relativism: The view that moral truth or justification depends on a framework such as cultural standards. It goes beyond noticing disagreement. Rejecting exceptionless rules does not force one to become a relativist.
  3. Utilitarianism: This approach assesses conduct through overall well-being, with impartial consideration of those affected. For example, many tiny conveniences may not compensate for a few people’s profound suffering. Counting beneficiaries alone is not a calculation of utility.
  4. Deontology: Duties and constraints can limit what we may do even for a beneficial outcome. A promise matters as a commitment to another person, not only as an instrument of social convenience. Deontologists disagree about exceptions and how conflicting duties should be resolved.
  5. Virtue Ethics: This approach asks what character traits and practical wisdom enable good conduct and a good life. Honesty involves recognizing when and how truthfulness matters; merely labeling an act “what a virtuous person would do” leaves the real explanatory work unfinished.
  6. Ethical Egoism: The normative view that agents ought to advance their own interests. It is different from the psychological claim that everyone always acts selfishly. Whether self-interest supplies an adequate ethical standard remains disputed.
  7. Social Contract Theory: Agreement supplies a way to explain or justify shared rules. In hypothetical versions, the question is what people would accept under specified conditions, not whether everyone actually consented. Whose interests count and what makes the conditions fair are central questions.
  8. Natural Law Theory: These approaches connect practical reason with accounts of human nature and basic human goods. They require an argument about flourishing and obligation, not an inference that whatever is biologically common must be right.
  9. Applied Ethics: Applied ethics involves the examination of particular moral issues in private and public life. It is a practical application of moral considerations. Areas within applied ethics include medical ethics, business ethics, and environmental ethics, among others.

Consider returning a lost wallet. Consequences, the owner’s claim and the finder’s honesty illuminate different aspects of the act. Whether any resulting moral judgment reports a stance-independent fact is a further, metaethical question. Agreement on what to do need not settle that question.

Gemini response · editorial edition

Ethics is the branch of philosophy that studies morality. It explores questions about what is right and wrong, good and bad, and how we should live our lives. Here are some key concepts in ethics:

  • Morality: Beliefs, practices and standards concerning right and wrong. Their social origins help explain how people acquire them; their justification requires further argument.
  • Values: What people regard as important, or what a theory argues is worth caring about. These senses can diverge: a society can prize something that a critic judges harmful or unjust.
  • Norms: Standards of expected behavior, including formal rules and informal conventions. A widespread norm can still be morally objectionable, so description is not endorsement.
  • Moral dilemma: A conflict between moral requirements that cannot all be fulfilled. Uncertainty is different: one may not know what to do even where the requirements are compatible. An apparent dilemma may dissolve when further options become visible.
  • Consequentialism: This family grounds moral assessment in consequences. Maximizing versions favor the best outcomes; variants differ over the relevant goods, whose outcomes count and whether acts or rules are assessed.
  • Deontology: Duties, rights or constraints can make actions required or forbidden independently of maximizing benefits. The question is which principles are justified, not simply whether an action obeys a pre-existing rule.
  • Virtue ethics: Character and practical wisdom guide action. Courage, for example, requires judging which dangers are worth facing; recklessness is not made virtuous by confidence.
  • Utilitarianism: This consequentialist family seeks overall well-being with impartial consideration of those affected. It is not simply majority advantage, and disputes about measuring and distributing well-being remain important.
  • Rights: Entitlements, claims, liberties or protections attributed to persons or other rights-holders. Moral rights and legally recognized rights need not coincide. A right’s content matters: who has it, against whom, and what does it require?
  • Justice: Justice is the fair and impartial treatment of all people. There are different theories of justice, such as distributive justice (which focuses on the fair distribution of resources) and retributive justice (which focuses on punishing wrongdoing).

These are just a few of the key concepts in ethics. The field of ethics is complex and there are many different theories and perspectives. However, by understanding these concepts, we can begin to think critically about moral issues and make informed decisions about how to live our lives.

Prompt 3: Provide a timeline of major developments in ethics. Include both the relevant thinkers and the concepts introduced.

Two selective timelines of ethical thought

ChatGPT response · editorial edition

This selective timeline follows the original response’s mainly European sequence. It records prominent contributions rather than claiming that each thinker invented the associated concept. The companion timeline adds Chinese and Indian traditions; neither provides a global history.

  1. Ancient Greek Ethics (sixth–fourth centuries BCE)
  • Pre-Socratic Philosophers: Discussions of order, character and human life appear alongside cosmological inquiry. Surviving evidence is fragmentary; this is not the beginning of all ethical reflection.
  • Socrates (c. 470–399 BCE): In Plato’s dialogues, questions inherited claims about virtue and the good life through examination and argument. Our knowledge is mediated by other authors, not surviving writings by Socrates.
  • Plato (c. 427–347 BCE): Explores justice, the ordering of the soul and the Good. His account seeks standards beyond fluctuating opinion, though its interpretation remains debated.
  • Aristotle (384–322 BCE): Developed virtue ethics in his works “Nicomachean Ethics” and “Eudemian Ethics,” focusing on character and virtue as the basis for ethical living.
  1. Hellenistic Ethics (323–31 BCE)
  • Epicurus (341–270 BCE): Makes pleasure central to the good life, emphasizing freedom from bodily pain and mental disturbance, prudent desires and friendship. This is not an invitation to unlimited indulgence.
  • Zeno of Citium (c. 334–262 BCE): Introduced Stoicism, focusing on living in accordance with nature and virtue as sufficient for happiness.
  1. Late Antique and Medieval Christian Ethics (fourth–fifteenth centuries CE)
  • Augustine of Hippo (354–430): Develops Christian accounts of love, will, sin and grace in conversation with Platonism. Reducing his ethics to obedience to divine commands misses much of that account.
  • Thomas Aquinas (1225–1274): Integrated Aristotelian ethics with Christian theology, developing a comprehensive natural law theory.
  1. Early Modern Ethics (1500–1800)
  • Thomas Hobbes (1588–1679): In Leviathan (1651), connects peace and political authority with covenants and laws of nature. The argument concerns conditions for secure cooperation, not a claim that any agreement automatically makes its terms right.
  • John Locke (1632–1704): Further developed social contract theory, emphasizing natural rights and the consent of the governed.
  • Immanuel Kant (1724–1804): Develops a systematic ethics of duty, autonomy and the categorical imperative, including the Groundwork (1785) and Critique of Practical Reason (1788). He did not originate every duty-based approach.
  1. Late Eighteenth- and Nineteenth-Century Ethics
  • Jeremy Bentham (1748–1832) and John Stuart Mill (1806–1873): Developed and refined utilitarianism, emphasizing the greatest happiness principle as the standard for morality.
  1. 20th Century to Present
  • G. E. Moore (1873–1958): In Principia Ethica (1903), argues that goodness is not definable as a natural or other nonidentical property. He criticizes what he calls the naturalistic fallacy; he does not advocate committing it.
  • John Rawls (1921–2002): Introduced a modern form of social contract theory in “A Theory of Justice,” emphasizing the principles of justice as fairness.
  • Alasdair MacIntyre (1929–2025): In After Virtue (1981), criticizes fragmented modern moral discourse and develops an account of virtues through practices, narratives and traditions. He is one contributor to the modern revival of virtue ethics.

These developments overlap and remain contested; later approaches do not simply replace earlier ones. The omissions here reflect the original list’s limited scope, not the absence of ethical thought in other traditions.

Sources for specific corrections: Moore, Principia Ethica, §35; Notre Dame’s MacIntyre memorial.

Gemini response · editorial edition

Timeline of Major Developments in Ethics

Ancient World (before 500 CE):

  • Sixth–fifth centuries BCE (approximate; the Buddha’s dates are disputed):
    • Confucius (China): Emphasizes ren (humaneness), li (ritual propriety) and ethical cultivation in relationships. His tradition offers an account of character and social responsibility distinct from, though comparable with, Greek virtue ethics.
    • The Buddha (India): The early Buddhist teachings connect suffering and its cessation with an ethical path, including right speech, action and livelihood. They reinterpret karma through intention; they do not introduce the pre-existing idea of karma for the first time.
  • Late fifth–fourth centuries BCE:
    • Socrates (Greece): Active in fifth-century Athens and executed in 399 BCE; remembered for critical questioning about virtue and the good life.
    • Plato (Greece): Proposes the concept of the ideal form of the Good, which guides moral behavior.
  • Fourth century BCE:
    • Aristotle (Greece; 384–322 BCE): Develops an account of eudaimonia (human flourishing), virtues, habituation and practical wisdom. His work belongs to the fourth century BCE; he gives the existing Greek idea of flourishing a detailed philosophical treatment.

Medieval Era (500–1500 CE):

  • Thirteenth century CE:
    • Thomas Aquinas: Integrates Aristotelian thought with Christian theology. Natural law involves practical reason’s participation in a divinely ordered account of human goods, rather than merely an implanted feeling of approval.

Early Modern Period (1500–1800 CE):

  • Sixteenth century CE:
    • Niccolò Machiavelli: In The Prince, written in 1513, examines political necessity, power and the tension between effective rule and conventional virtue. “Realpolitik” is a later label; he did not coin it.
  • Seventeenth century CE:
    • Thomas Hobbes (England): Argues for a social contract to create order and avoid the “state of nature”. He emphasizes self-preservation as a fundamental right.
    • John Locke (England): Champions natural rights like life, liberty, and property.
  • Eighteenth century CE:
    • Immanuel Kant: Develops the categorical imperative, including the demand to treat persons as ends and never merely as means. Ordinary cooperation can involve others as means while respecting their agency.
    • David Hume: Gives sentiment and sympathy a central role in moral judgment. Reason still informs us about facts, relations and consequences; his account is not simply a license to follow any immediate feeling.

Late Eighteenth Century to the Present:

  • Late eighteenth–nineteenth centuries CE (the original order below lists Mill before his predecessor Bentham):
    • John Stuart Mill: In Utilitarianism (1861/1863), develops the greatest-happiness approach, including qualitative differences among pleasures. Overall well-being matters, not simply the number of people on a winning side.
    • Jeremy Bentham: His Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation (published 1789) develops an influential utility-based approach to conduct and institutions, attending to pleasure and pain across those affected.
  • Twentieth century CE:
    • G. E. Moore: Principia Ethica (1903) strongly influences analytic metaethics. Questions about the nature and justification of moral judgments are much older; he did not invent the whole inquiry.
    • Emmanuel Levinas: Makes responsibility to another person central to his philosophical project. His account of the Other is not simply interchangeable with the distinct tradition of care ethics.
  • Late twentieth–twenty-first centuries CE:
    • Bioethics: An established twentieth-century field addressing medicine and the life sciences, with continuing questions about consent, biotechnology and access to care.
    • Environmental Ethics: An established field examining human responsibilities toward animals, ecosystems and future generations; it was not first created in the twenty-first century.
    • Machine Ethics: Inquiry into how artificial systems might follow ethical constraints or make ethically significant choices, distinct from the broader ethics of how people build and deploy AI.

This remains a selective timeline with large geographic and historical omissions. Dates name periods of contribution, not a claim that every concept was invented then. The original thinker order is retained, including the explicitly noted Mill/Bentham reversal.

For dating and karma qualifications, see the Buddha entry, sections 1a and 3a; for the name and project, see Emmanuel Levinas.

Prompt 4: List and describe new areas of interest in ethics.

New questions within established fields

ChatGPT response · editorial edition

New technologies and social conditions create new questions within both established and newer fields. The following areas overlap; this list identifies active lines of inquiry rather than ranking current popularity or claiming that every field is new.

  1. Digital Ethics: This area examines the moral issues associated with the digital age, including privacy concerns, data security, digital surveillance, and the ethical implications of social media behavior. It also explores the responsibilities of those who create, manage, and use digital technologies.
  2. Artificial Intelligence Ethics: Examines bias, accountability, privacy, labor and the distribution of benefits and harms from AI. Possible moral status of artificial systems is a separate disputed question; performance or fluent language alone does not establish sentience or rights.
  3. Environmental Ethics: While not entirely new, environmental ethics has gained renewed interest in light of climate change and global ecological crises. It addresses the moral relationship between humans and the natural environment, focusing on issues such as conservation, sustainability, biodiversity, and the ethical treatment of animals.
  4. Bioethics: Bioethics covers the ethical questions arising from advances in medicine and biology. It includes debates over medical decision-making, consent, genetic engineering, cloning, stem cell research, and end-of-life care. The field has expanded to address emerging issues like CRISPR, personalized medicine, and the ethics of pandemic response.
  5. Neuroethics: Neuroethics explores the ethical, legal, and social implications of neuroscience, including the consequences of brain enhancements, the treatment of neurological disorders, and the implications of neurotechnology for identity, privacy, and autonomy.
  6. Business Ethics: While business ethics itself is not new, it continuously evolves to address current issues like corporate social responsibility, ethical leadership, the impact of globalization, fair trade, sustainability, and the ethical implications of new business models and technologies.
  7. Technology Ethics: Beyond digital ethics and AI ethics, technology ethics broadly examines the ethical aspects of technological innovation and deployment. This includes issues related to emerging technologies like blockchain, nanotechnology, and biotechnology, focusing on their societal impacts, privacy concerns, and potential for misuse.
  8. Global Ethics: This area focuses on ethical issues that transcend national boundaries, such as global justice, human rights, global poverty, and the equitable distribution of resources. It also deals with ethical questions related to migration, global health disparities, and international collaboration.
  9. Ethics of War and Peace: New forms of warfare, such as cyberwarfare and drone strikes, raise fresh ethical questions about conflict, the just war theory, and the moral responsibilities of combatants and non-combatants.
  10. Media Ethics: With the rise of digital media, issues related to media ethics have become more prominent, including concerns about misinformation, disinformation, journalistic integrity, media bias, and the ethical responsibilities of content creators and platforms.

A useful way to connect these fields is to ask who may be affected, who can consent or object, who bears uncertainty, and who must answer for mistakes. New applications can expose old conflicts between benefit, rights, responsibility and fair distribution.

Gemini response · editorial edition

These are areas where changing technologies or social conditions create further ethical questions. Several, including care and animal ethics, have long histories and should not be described as newly invented.

  • Machine Ethics: Studies how artificial systems might be designed to act within ethical constraints. Broader AI ethics also asks whether people should deploy a system at all, who can challenge its decisions and who remains responsible for its harms.
  • Bioethics: Examines moral issues in medicine and the life sciences. Gene editing, brain–computer interfaces and related technologies raise questions about consent, access and effects on future people; identifying an ethical issue is not a claim that a proposed intervention is safe or effective.
  • Robotics and Automation: Raises questions about workplace change, safety and responsibility when machines act with limited supervision. Moral agency requires further argument; increased technical capability does not automatically make a machine morally responsible.
  • Data Ethics: The vast collection and analysis of personal data raises ethical concerns about privacy, security, and the potential for discrimination based on data profiles.
  • Global Ethics: In an increasingly interconnected world, there’s a growing need for ethical frameworks that address global challenges like climate change, pandemics, and resource scarcity. This requires considering the ethical responsibilities of nations and corporations operating across borders.
  • Care Ethics: An established approach emphasizing relationships, dependency and attentive response to particular needs. It also asks who performs care, under what conditions, and whether caring relationships distribute power and burdens unfairly.
  • Animal Ethics: The moral status of animals and the ethical treatment of animals in research, agriculture, and entertainment continue to be debated. There’s growing interest in the concept of animal sentience and the rights of animals.

The task is not only to devise rules for new devices. It is also to examine whose needs existing institutions overlook and whether their arrangements can be justified to those affected.

Prompt 5: List the most popular schools of thought in ethics along with their major proponents.

Ethical approaches and their proponents

ChatGPT response · editorial edition

  1. Virtue Ethics
  • Major Proponents and Influences: Aristotle and Alasdair MacIntyre; Elizabeth Anscombe’s criticism of modern moral philosophy helped renew attention to virtue, though these thinkers do not share one complete theory.
  • Key Concepts: Emphasizes the virtues or moral character as the foundation for ethical behavior, focusing on the development of good character traits and the pursuit of a virtuous life.
  1. Utilitarianism
  • Major Proponents: Jeremy Bentham, John Stuart Mill, Peter Singer
  • Key Concepts: Evaluates conduct through overall well-being with impartial consideration of those affected. Different versions specify utility and the assessment of acts or rules differently; majority benefit alone is not enough.
  1. Deontology
  • Major Proponents: Immanuel Kant, W.D. Ross
  • Key Concepts: Duties, rights or constraints are not reducible to maximizing outcomes. Kant’s unconditional principle differs from Ross’s account of prima facie duties: genuine duties that can be outweighed in a particular conflict.
  1. Social Contract Theory
  • Major Proponents: Thomas Hobbes, John Locke, Jean-Jacques Rousseau, John Rawls
  • Key Concepts: Uses agreement to explain or justify shared arrangements. The original proponents disagree substantially; Rawls’s hypothetical choice under fair conditions is not a claim that actual citizens have all consented.
  1. Existentialist Ethics
  • Major Proponents and Precursor: Jean-Paul Sartre and Simone de Beauvoir; Friedrich Nietzsche is an influential precursor rather than an adherent of the later existentialist movement.
  • Key Concepts: Examines freedom, responsibility and making commitments within concrete situations. De Beauvoir emphasizes that one’s freedom is entangled with others’ freedom; this is not permission to invent values without accountability.
  1. Natural Law Theory
  • Major Proponents: Thomas Aquinas, Cicero
  • Key Concepts: Connects moral reasoning with human nature and basic goods. The relevant “law” is a proposed standard for action, not a physical regularity or the claim that every natural impulse is good.
  1. Moral Relativism
  • Key Concepts: Moral truth or justification is relative to a framework. Major Proponents: Gilbert Harman and David Wong develop different versions. Cultural disagreement alone does not establish either version, and tolerance requires its own argument.
  1. Feminist Ethics
  • Major Contributors: Carol Gilligan and Nel Noddings are associated with care-centered approaches; Martha Nussbaum develops a capabilities approach. Their inclusion does not make all feminist ethics a single care theory.
  • Key Concepts: Examines gendered power, exclusion and the neglected experiences of women and other marginalized people. Care and relationships are important strands alongside arguments about justice, autonomy, institutions and oppression.
  1. Moral Nihilism
  • Relevant Thinkers: J. L. Mackie is a clearer example of moral error theory than Nietzsche. Nietzsche diagnoses and challenges nihilism while criticizing inherited morality; calling him simply a proponent is misleading.
  • Key Concepts: Denies objective moral values or requirements, with moral error theory offering one developed account. This does not entail that every preference is arbitrary or that compassion and practical criticism must disappear.

Compare these entries at the right level. A theory may guide action by consequences while rejecting stance-independent moral facts. Conversely, realism alone does not settle which moral standards are correct. No survey of popularity is supplied here.

Gemini response · editorial edition

Here are five prominent positions and families of theories, including moral realism and anti-realism. This is not a measured ranking of popularity: the first and last concern the status of moral judgments, while the middle three principally concern ethical assessment.

Moral Realism:

  • Core Belief: On the robust sense used here, some moral judgments are true independently of our approving them. Realists disagree about the nature of moral facts and how we know them; realism is not by definition a theory that reason alone discovers every answer.
  • Major Proponents:
    • Plato (Greece) – Believed in the ideal form of the Good.
    • G. E. Moore — Defended a non-naturalist account of goodness and criticized identifying it with another property, calling such identifications the naturalistic fallacy.

Consequentialism:

  • Core Belief: Grounds moral assessment in consequences. Maximizing versions require the best outcomes; members of the family disagree about which outcomes matter and what should be assessed.
  • Major Proponents:
    • John Stuart Mill (England) – Championed utilitarianism, maximizing overall happiness.
    • Jeremy Bentham — Assessed utility through pleasure and pain across those affected, rather than equating morality with the agent’s own pleasure.

Deontology:

  • Core Belief: Gives duties or constraints a role independent of simply producing the best aggregate outcome. Justification and conflicts between duties matter; existing rules do not automatically deserve obedience.
  • Major Proponents:
    • Immanuel Kant — Developed the categorical imperative, including the requirement to treat persons as ends and never merely as means.

Virtue Ethics:

  • Core Belief: Gives virtues and practical wisdom a central role in good conduct and a good life. Courage, honesty and care require judgment about circumstances, not simply habitual conformity.
  • Major Proponents:
    • Aristotle (Greece) – Emphasized eudaimonia (human flourishing) as the goal of ethics and the importance of reason.
    • Confucius (China) – Highlighted the importance of ren (humaneness) and li (ritual propriety) for social harmony.

Moral Anti-Realism:

  • Core Belief: Challenges robust moral realism. Different versions dispute moral truth, its independence from attitudes, or the function of moral language. Anti-realism is broader than the two noncognitivist approaches listed below.
  • Sub-Theories:
    • Emotivism: A form of noncognitivism on which moral utterances express attitudes and can influence others (A. J. Ayer, C. L. Stevenson).
    • Noncognitivism: Treats moral language primarily as expressing attitudes or prescriptions rather than describing moral facts. R. M. Hare’s prescriptivism is one version; emotivism is another, not a separate rival category at the same level.
  • Note: Error theory, associated with J. L. Mackie, treats ordinary affirmative moral judgments as truth-apt but false; subjectivist approaches can make truth depend on attitudes. Neither is captured by equating all anti-realism with emotional expression. These distinctions alone do not supply a practical decision rule.

Further distinctions: Kant, Groundwork, second section; Mill, Utilitarianism, chapter II; Moral Anti-Realism.
