Prompt 1: List and explain the primary areas of focus in meta-ethics.

The questions meta-ethics asks

ChatGPT response · editorial edition

Meta-ethics examines what moral judgments mean, what could make them true or justified, and why they might give us reasons to act. It asks about the assumptions behind ethical theories rather than directly prescribing a solution to each practical problem. Its primary areas include:

  1. Moral ontology: What moral facts or properties, if any, exist? A robust moral realist holds that at least some moral judgments are true independently of our approval. Anti-realism includes several alternatives, not just personal preference. For example, error theory treats ordinary moral judgments as truth-apt but systematically mistaken in their claim to objective authority.
  2. Moral Epistemology: This focuses on how we can know or understand moral truths, if they exist. Moral epistemology examines the sources of our moral beliefs and how those beliefs can be justified. It questions whether moral knowledge is possible and, if so, how we acquire it—whether through reason, emotion, intuition, or some other means.
  3. Moral semantics: What do “good,” “wrong” and “ought” mean? Cognitivism treats moral judgments as expressing beliefs that can be true or false. Non-cognitivist approaches emphasize attitudes, commitments or prescriptions; sophisticated versions also explain why moral language supports reasoning and apparently factual talk. The contrast is not between rational discourse and mere noise.
  4. Moral psychology: How are judgment, motivation and emotion connected? One meta-ethical question is whether sincerely judging an action obligatory necessarily gives an agent some motivation to perform it. That differs from the empirical question of what usually motivates people, and from the normative question of what they ought to do.
  5. The nature of moral reasoning: What makes a moral inference valid, and what could justify its premises? “This policy reduces suffering” describes a consequence; reaching “we ought to adopt it” also requires a normative connection. Naming that connection makes it available for scrutiny rather than silently assuming it.

Meta-ethics, through these areas of focus, seeks to understand the underlying aspects of ethical practice and theory, rather than prescribing specific moral actions or codes. It provides a foundational framework for further ethical analysis and discussion in normative ethics and applied ethics.

Gemini response · editorial edition

Meta-ethics, a branch of philosophy, delves into the nature, foundation, and meaning of moral judgments. Unlike applied ethics (which tackles specific moral dilemmas) or normative ethics (which proposes moral principles), meta-ethics asks more fundamental questions about the very nature of morality itself. Here are the primary areas of focus in meta-ethics:

  1. Moral Realism vs. Moral Anti-Realism:
    • Moral realism: In the robust sense used here, at least some moral judgments are true independently of our approval. Realism need not posit a separate Platonic realm: naturalist realists seek moral facts within the natural world.
    • Moral anti-realism: A family of challenges to robust realism. Emotivists emphasize attitudes, but error theorists treat moral judgments as beliefs and reject their claim to objective truth. These alternatives must not be collapsed into one theory of language.
  2. Meaning of Moral Statements:
    • Cognitivism: Moral judgments express beliefs with truth values. Saying “stealing is wrong” purports to state something true; cognitivism alone does not establish that it succeeds. This is why error theory can be cognitivist and anti-realist.
    • Non-cognitivism: Moral judgments primarily express attitudes or practical commitments rather than ordinary descriptive beliefs. The exclamation “Boo to stealing!” illustrates a simple emotivist account, but does not capture the full ambitions of contemporary expressivism.
  3. Justification of Moral Beliefs:
    • Foundationalism: Some justified beliefs do not derive all their justification from other beliefs. A moral foundationalist may appeal to basic moral judgments; foundationalism does not by itself require that every basic judgment be self-evident or infallible.
    • Non-foundationalism: Alternatives reject that structure of basic and derived justification. Coherentism, for example, tests how judgments and principles support one another. Merely citing an accepted social practice does not yet justify it.
  4. Metaethical Inquiry and Moral Disagreement:
    • Explores how meta-ethical views can explain or influence the existence of moral disagreement. Can people with different meta-ethical stances still have productive moral discussions?

These are some of the key areas of focus in meta-ethics. By exploring these questions, philosophers aim to understand the very foundation upon which we build our moral frameworks.

Prompt 3: Provide rough estimates on the percentage of career philosophers taking various positions in meta-ethics.

What the 2020 survey actually measured

ChatGPT response · editorial edition

The 2020 PhilPapers Survey provides measured estimates for its target-group respondents, not a census of all career philosophers. The figures below combine acceptance and leaning toward a view and include multiple-answer endorsements. Each question excludes skipped and insufficiently familiar answers, so denominators differ. These are dated survey findings, not estimates of philosophers’ views today.

  1. Moral Realism vs. Moral Anti-Realism: Moral realism posits that there are objective moral facts that are independent of human opinions, whereas moral anti-realism denies this.
  • Moral realism: about 62% (62.07%) of 1,719 qualifying respondents.
  • Moral anti-realism: about 26% (26.12%) of the same 1,719 respondents. Sixty-six responses were excluded from this question’s denominator.
  1. Cognitivism vs. Non-Cognitivism: Cognitivism is the view that moral statements express beliefs that can be true or false, while non-cognitivism holds that moral statements do not express beliefs but rather express emotions, prescriptions, or something other than beliefs that can be true or false.
  • Cognitivism: about 69% (69.25%) of 1,636 qualifying respondents.
  • Non-cognitivism: about 21% (20.72%) of that group; 149 responses were excluded. This question concerns moral judgment, not the same classification as realism versus anti-realism.
  1. Naturalism and non-naturalism: Ethical naturalists treat moral properties as natural; some accept reduction and others resist it. Non-naturalists reject that identification. The survey’s “Morality” question includes these alongside constructivism, expressivism and error theory; it is distinct from its question about naturalism in philosophical method.
  • Naturalist realism: about 32% (31.64%) of 1,024 qualifying respondents to the Morality question.
  • Non-naturalism: about 27% (26.56%) of the same group. The denominator excludes 117 skipped or insufficiently familiar responses.

These are overlapping endorsements, not mutually exclusive slices of a single pie. The response population and selection process limit generalization. Popularity neither proves moral realism nor refutes the curator’s demand that moral authority be justified. To compare years, use the survey’s dedicated longitudinal analysis rather than subtracting figures from different populations.

Gemini response · editorial edition

There is relevant survey evidence, although no definitive census of every career philosopher. The original speculative ranges should be replaced by the dated 2020 PhilPapers results. Two qualifications matter:

  • Philosophical Landscape: Meta-ethics is a complex and multifaceted field with ongoing debates. Philosophers might not subscribe to a single, rigid position but rather hold nuanced views that draw from different perspectives.
  • Survey coverage: The target group and those who answered a given question are not the entire worldwide profession. Missing responses and the survey’s population design matter when interpreting the percentages.

However, here’s what we can say:

  • Measured positions: The 2020 target-group results show about 62% endorsing realism and 26% anti-realism; separately, about 69% endorse cognitivism and 21% non-cognitivism. The first pair uses 1,719 responses and the second 1,636. These are inclusive acceptance-or-leaning figures, not exclusive categories. See the linked results in the preceding response.
  • Keep the questions separate: Among 1,024 qualifying answers to the Morality question, about 32% endorse naturalist realism and 27% non-naturalism. A cognitivist may also be an error theorist, so realism/cognitivism cannot be merged into one numerical camp opposed to anti-realism/non-cognitivism.

These figures answer the request more responsibly than invented ranges. They describe one survey and establish neither current worldwide proportions nor the truth of a position. Claims that a view is growing require a comparable time series.

Prompt 4: Comment on the importance of a good understanding of meta-ethics prior to looking at specific moral systems.

Examining a moral system’s assumptions

ChatGPT response · editorial edition

Understanding meta-ethics helps us evaluate a moral system’s claims to meaning, knowledge and authority. It is useful preparation, not a requirement to resolve every foundational debate before studying a theory or making a decision. Its benefits include:

  1. Clarifies moral claims: Separate what a statement means from whether it is true and what authority it claims. Anti-realism is not synonymous with expressing a personal attitude: an error theorist can understand a claim as factual while rejecting it.
  2. Informs the Basis of Moral Reasoning: Understanding the debates between cognitivism and non-cognitivism, for instance, equips one with insights into the nature of moral reasoning—whether moral statements are capable of being true or false and what that means for moral debate and dialogue. This is foundational for evaluating the coherence and validity of arguments within specific moral systems.
  3. Facilitates Critical Engagement: Knowledge of meta-ethics allows for a deeper, more critical engagement with specific moral theories. By understanding the underlying assumptions about moral ontology, semantics, and epistemology, one can better assess the strengths and weaknesses of ethical theories and their applicability to real-world situations.
  4. Encourages Reflective Thinking: Meta-ethics encourages individuals to reflect on their own moral beliefs and the reasons behind them. It prompts questions about the origin of our moral intuitions and whether our ethical judgments are influenced by reason, emotion, cultural norms, or other factors. This reflective process is crucial for developing a nuanced, personal ethical perspective.
  5. Locates disagreements: Some disputes concern moral foundations; others concern facts, priorities or applications despite shared foundations. Ask which premise divides the speakers rather than assuming every practical conflict is meta-ethical.
  6. Prepares for Interdisciplinary Dialogue: Meta-ethics intersects with other disciplines, such as psychology, anthropology, and sociology, in exploring how humans understand and engage with moral concepts. A good understanding of meta-ethics can enrich discussions across these fields, contributing to a more comprehensive understanding of human moral behavior and beliefs.
  7. Encourages careful engagement: Understanding alternatives can make criticism more accurate. It does not itself entail tolerance or pluralism; those are further normative commitments requiring their own defense.

In essence, meta-ethics provides the tools and vocabulary necessary for a nuanced examination of moral issues, serving as a critical foundation for engaging with and evaluating the myriad moral systems and theories that guide human conduct.

Gemini response · editorial edition

Meta-ethics provides useful preparation for examining moral systems, especially where they claim binding authority. Study can also proceed in both directions: a practical dispute may expose a foundational question we had missed.

  • Foundations of Morality: Meta-ethics explores the very foundation upon which we build our moral frameworks. It asks questions about the nature of moral truths, justification of beliefs, and the meaning of moral statements. By understanding these issues, you can approach moral systems with a critical eye, questioning their underlying assumptions and how they justify their moral principles.
  • Evaluating systems: Utilitarianism identifies consequences as central to right action; deontological approaches emphasize duties or constraints. Neither label alone settles realism versus anti-realism. Ask separately what the system recommends and what supposedly makes that recommendation true or authoritative.
  • Avoiding invalid inferences: “It is natural, therefore good” is an appeal to nature unless a further premise connects nature with value. Moore’s “naturalistic fallacy” concerns identifying goodness with another property; the is–ought problem concerns deriving a normative conclusion from purely descriptive premises. These related issues are not interchangeable, and ethical naturalists dispute Moore’s objection.
  • Building a moral framework: Choosing principles that fit our preferences does not establish that anyone else ought to obey them. Examine how the principles fit together, what supports them, and whether their claimed authority exceeds that support.
  • Engaging in Ethical Discourse: Meta-ethics equips you with the tools to effectively participate in discussions about morality. You can engage with different viewpoints by understanding the underlying philosophical assumptions and how they influence moral conclusions.

In conclusion, understanding meta-ethics provides a critical lens for examining moral systems. It allows you to analyze their foundations, compare their approaches, and ultimately develop your own informed moral perspective.

Two sets of twelve discussion questions

ChatGPT response · editorial edition

  1. Objective vs. Subjective Morality: How does the distinction between objective and subjective moral values influence our understanding of ethical disagreements?
  2. Moral Realism’s Implications: If moral realism is true, what does this imply about the universality of moral obligations across different cultures?
  3. Challenges to Moral Anti-Realism: What are the main challenges facing moral anti-realism, especially in explaining moral disagreements and social reforms?
  4. The Role of Intuition in Moral Epistemology: How significant is intuition in our acquisition of moral knowledge, and can it be considered a reliable source?
  5. Cognitivism vs. Non-Cognitivism: What implications does the debate between cognitivism and non-cognitivism have for moral discourse and ethical argumentation?
  6. Moral Language Meaning: In what ways do our interpretations of moral language affect our moral reasoning and ethical discussions?
  7. The Psychological Basis of Ethics: How does understanding the psychological aspects of morality contribute to or complicate ethical theory?
  8. Ethical Naturalism and Its Critics: What are the strongest arguments for and against ethical naturalism, and how do they impact the naturalistic fallacy debate?
  9. Non-Naturalism’s Appeal: Considering the challenges to both naturalism and non-naturalism, why might someone prefer a non-naturalistic meta-ethical theory?
  10. Moral Reasoning’s Structure: How does the structure of moral reasoning impact the effectiveness and persuasiveness of ethical arguments?
  11. Meta-Ethics and Moral Pluralism: How can an understanding of meta-ethics foster greater tolerance and understanding between different moral perspectives?
  12. Interdisciplinary Insights into Meta-Ethics: What insights can fields such as psychology, anthropology, and sociology offer to meta-ethical discussions, and how can these insights challenge or support traditional philosophical views on morality?

Gemini response · editorial edition

Discussion Questions on Meta-Ethics:

  1. Is it possible to have a strong moral compass without understanding meta-ethics? Why or why not?
  2. Think of a specific moral dilemma. How could understanding different meta-ethical positions (e.g., moral realism vs. anti-realism) impact your approach to resolving it?
  3. Can a moral system be internally consistent even if its underlying meta-ethical assumptions are flawed? Explain your answer.
  4. How do an appeal to nature, Moore’s naturalistic fallacy and the is–ought problem differ? What additional premise would be needed in an example of your own?
  5. Imagine a society with completely different meta-ethical beliefs from your own. How would this impact communication and moral decision-making within that society?
  6. Should meta-ethical questions be kept separate from practical moral issues, or do they necessarily influence each other? Defend your position.
  7. Does a focus on meta-ethics risk leading to moral paralysis, as people get bogged down in theoretical debates rather than taking action?
  8. In your opinion, are there any areas of morality where the “what” (specific moral principles) is more important than the “why” (meta-ethical foundation)?
  9. Can advancements in science or our understanding of the human mind ever provide definitive answers to meta-ethical questions? Why or why not?
  10. How might the increasing interconnectedness of our globalized world influence the future of meta-ethical debates?
  11. Meta-ethics is often seen as a complex and abstract field. Can you think of any real-world examples that illustrate the importance of meta-ethical concepts?
  12. Ultimately, what do you see as the most valuable takeaway from studying meta-ethics?
