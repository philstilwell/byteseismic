Prompt 1: Assisted suicide has long been debated. Provide the common moral arguments on both sides of this issue.

Moral arguments on both sides

ChatGPT response · editorial edition

Moral Arguments For and Against Assisted Suicide

Assisted suicide and euthanasia are distinct: in the former the person performs the final act; in the latter another person does. Physician involvement is a further distinction. The World Medical Association declaration distinguishes these practices and separately discusses refusal of treatment. The moral arguments below concern voluntary assisted suicide, without assuming that every argument transfers unchanged to other end-of-life decisions.

Arguments For Assisted Suicide
  1. Autonomy and Personal Choice
    • Respect for Autonomy: Individuals have the right to make decisions about their own bodies and lives, including the choice to end their life to avoid suffering.
    • Personal Dignity: Allowing assisted suicide respects the individual’s desire to die with dignity, avoiding prolonged pain and loss of quality of life.
  2. Compassion and Relief from Suffering
    • Alleviating Suffering: Assisted suicide can be a compassionate response to alleviate extreme physical and emotional suffering when no other options provide relief.
    • Quality of Life: Supporters argue that a person’s considered assessment of intolerable suffering can support a choice about dying. This does not mean death improves a continuing life, or that disability makes a life less valuable.
  3. Practical Considerations
    • Regulation: Some supporters argue that a regulated system could reduce harms compared with unregulated practice. That is an empirical claim requiring evidence; neither inevitability nor improved safety follows from the existence of suicide.
    • Resource Allocation: Some raise opportunity costs in health care. Savings alone cannot establish permission to end a life, and financial pressures may undermine genuinely voluntary choice. The argument needs an explicit account of whose interests and rights constrain cost comparisons.
Arguments Against Assisted Suicide
  1. Sanctity of Life
    • Inviolability of Life: Many argue that life is inherently valuable and should not be ended intentionally, regardless of circumstances.
    • Slippery Slope: There is concern that legalizing assisted suicide could lead to a slippery slope where the value of life is undermined, potentially leading to non-voluntary or involuntary euthanasia.
  2. Moral and Ethical Concerns
    • Medical Ethics: Opponents see assistance in death as incompatible with the physician’s healing role. The World Medical Association opposes it; supporters dispute whether this role excludes every instance of assistance. An appeal to an oath identifies a professional commitment, not a self-validating moral conclusion.
    • Potential for Abuse: There are fears that vulnerable individuals, such as the elderly or disabled, could be pressured into assisted suicide due to financial, social, or familial pressures.
  3. Psychological and Social Implications
    • Impact on Families and Society: The decision for assisted suicide can have profound psychological impacts on families and can influence societal views on the value of life.
    • Alternative Care: Opponents argue that focus should be on improving palliative care and support systems to alleviate suffering rather than facilitating death.

These arguments illustrate the complex moral landscape surrounding assisted suicide, highlighting the deep ethical considerations and the need for careful deliberation in any discussion or legislation on this issue.

Moral Arguments for Assisted Suicide
  • Autonomy and Bodily Rights: Supporters of assisted suicide argue that competent adults have the right to make their own choices about their bodies, including the right to end their own life if they are terminally ill and suffering. This principle of bodily autonomy is considered a fundamental human right, allowing individuals to control their own medical care and make decisions based on their own values.
  • Compassion and Minimizing Suffering: For terminally ill patients experiencing immense physical or emotional pain, assisted suicide can be seen as a compassionate act. It allows them to die with dignity and on their own terms, rather than lingering in prolonged suffering.
  • Justice and Consistency: Supporters ask why autonomy permits refusal of treatment but cannot permit assistance in dying. Opponents distinguish refusing bodily intervention from requiring another person to help cause death. Calling treatment refusal “passive euthanasia” conceals this contested distinction rather than resolving it.
Moral Arguments Against Assisted Suicide
  • Sanctity of Life: Some religious and secular approaches regard intentionally ending innocent human life as prohibited even with consent. Describing the act as murder expresses this judgment; it cannot serve as an independent argument for that judgment.
  • Slippery Slope and Abuse: Opponents worry that legalizing assisted suicide could lead to a “slippery slope” where vulnerable populations, such as the elderly or disabled, are pressured into ending their lives. Concerns also exist about potential for coercion or mistakes in judgement.
  • Focus on Palliative Care: Opponents argue that improvements in palliative care can effectively manage pain and suffering, offering terminally ill patients a dignified and comfortable end-of-life experience. Resources should be directed towards improving palliative care instead of legalizing assisted suicide.

These are some of the most common moral arguments surrounding assisted suicide. The debate is complex and multifaceted, with strong arguments on both sides.

Arguments In Favor:

Personal Autonomy – People should have sovereignty over their own lives and bodies. Mentally competent adults should be able to choose to end their suffering through assisted death.

There are valid arguments on both sides of this very complex ethical issue. The debate involves weighing principles like autonomy versus sanctity of life.

Some key points on the potential incommensurability implied:

Prompt 6: Can we, therefore, conclude that, on issues we see much convergence of moral “scores” among ethical theories, it cannot be said that such convergence is evidence of an objective morality?

Convergence, evidence and objective morality

ChatGPT response · editorial edition

Convergence and Objective Morality

The convergence of moral “scores” among different ethical theories on certain issues does not necessarily imply the existence of an objective morality. While convergence might indicate a shared human intuition or common ethical reasoning, it does not definitively establish objective moral truths. Here’s why:

  1. Shared Human Experience:
    • Common intuitions: agreement about clearly specified harms may reflect shared experience. Terms such as “murder” already contain condemnation, so agreement that murder is wrong is less informative than agreement about which killings are wrongful.
    • Cultural and Social Conditioning: Societal norms and cultural values can shape ethical perspectives, leading to convergence on certain moral issues. This agreement might reflect common social conditioning rather than an objective moral truth.
  2. Different Justifications:
    • Divergent Rationales: Even when ethical theories converge on a moral conclusion, their underlying justifications can be vastly different. For instance, utilitarians might condemn murder because it decreases overall happiness, while deontologists oppose it due to the violation of a moral duty.
    • Plurality of Ethical Foundations: The same moral outcome can be derived from different foundational principles, such as duty, consequences, virtues, or divine commands. This plurality suggests that convergence does not necessarily point to a single objective moral standard.
  3. Subjective Interpretations:
    • Interpretive flexibility: scholars can emphasize different aspects of a theory. This complicates claims of independent convergence; interpretation alone does not establish that moral truth is subjective.
    • Context: different circumstances can justify different judgments even on an objective account. Context-sensitivity is therefore not, by itself, evidence against objectivity.
  4. Philosophical Debates:
    • Ongoing Philosophical Discourse: The existence of ongoing debates and disagreements among philosophers about fundamental ethical questions indicates that convergence on some issues does not resolve deeper philosophical disagreements. Objective morality remains a contentious and unresolved concept in philosophical discourse.
Conclusion

Your caution is warranted against treating convergence as proof. The stronger claim that convergence can never be evidence does not follow. Its evidential force depends on whether the agreement is more expected under an objective-truth explanation than under shared upbringing, common needs or other alternatives. The invented scores supply no independent test of that comparison.

Gemini response · editorial edition

We can conclude that convergence alone does not establish objective morality. We cannot infer that it has no possible evidential relevance: evidence can favor a hypothesis without proving it.

  • Different Priorities Can Lead to Similar Scores: As we saw with assisted suicide, theories with different core values might arrive at similar scores on a specific issue. For example, a Utilitarian score might be close to a Virtue Ethics score because both prioritize reducing suffering in that situation. This doesn’t mean they agree for the same reasons – one focuses on maximizing overall well-being, the other on compassion.
  • Varying Weights on Arguments: Even within the same theory, there can be disagreements about how much weight to give different arguments. This can lead to variations in scores even when the core principles are similar.
  • Focus on Specific Issues: Theories might converge on a particular issue but diverge on others. This doesn’t tell us if there’s an objective morality that applies universally.

However, convergence doesn’t necessarily negate the possibility of objective morality either. Here’s another way to look at it:

  • Common ground and evidence: independent reasoning that survives criticism might be offered as evidence by a realist. The anti-realist can ask whether common human needs or inherited assumptions explain the same agreement. Neither account wins merely because several theories share a conclusion.

So, what can we conclude?

Convergence suggests areas of potential agreement on moral principles. It doesn’t definitively prove or disprove objective morality. Instead, it can be a valuable tool in ethical discussions:

  • Identifying Shared Values: Convergence can help us identify moral values that hold weight across different frameworks. This can provide a foundation for building stronger ethical arguments.
  • Promoting Open Dialogue: Recognizing that convergence doesn’t equal certainty can encourage open discussions about moral reasoning and the justifications for our ethical positions.

In conclusion, convergence of moral scores is an interesting phenomenon in ethical discussions. It doesn’t definitively prove or disprove objective morality, but it can be a valuable tool for identifying areas of agreement and promoting open dialogue.

Claude response · editorial edition

The cautious conclusion is that convergence is not sufficient to establish moral objectivity. The broader claim that it cannot be evidence needs an additional argument.

Objective truth, if it exists, need not produce immediate agreement among fallible investigators. A disagreement argument must explain why the observed persistence or pattern is less expected on realism than on alternatives. The invented −9 to +5 scores cannot supply that evidence.

Agreement can arise for reasons other than access to objective moral facts. The following possibilities challenge an inference from agreement to certainty, though listing alternatives does not show that they are equally supported:

1) Coincidental overlap on a specific issue despite differing core premises
2) Conforming to common moral intuitions already embedded across theories
3) General principles being applied consistently by multiple theories
4) Localized agreement within the same broader ethical cluster (e.g. deontological theories)

Agreement may reflect common assumptions or parallel reasoning. Whether it supports realism depends on a comparison of explanations, not on the mere existence of disagreement elsewhere. A shared answer is neither an automatic discovery of moral fact nor automatically evidentially worthless.

The exchange leaves the curator’s central challenge intact: moral convergence needs an explanation before it can bear the weight placed on it. That challenge is stronger when separated from an unsupported claim that all ethical theories are intrinsically incomparable.
