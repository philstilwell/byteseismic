Prompt 1: In philosophy of mind, functionalism holds that subjectivity necessarily arises when material circuitry is configured in a particular way. Provide a rigorous description of this view.

Functional roles and the claim about subjectivity

ChatGPT response · editorial edition

Overview of Functionalism

Functionalism is a prominent theory in the philosophy of mind, which argues that mental states are defined by their functional roles in a system, rather than by their internal constitution or by the material from which they are made. This view suggests that mental states are like parts in a machine, each with a specific function or role in relation to other parts and to the system as a whole.

Material Circuitry and Subjectivity

The prompt describes a materialist application of functionalism to consciousness. On this view, subjectivity—the first-person character of experience—is constituted by the right causal organization of a physical system. Functionalism in general defines mental states by roles; extending that account to felt experience is a substantive commitment, not an established result about circuitry. The claim concerns an operating system in the relevant state, not a wiring diagram by itself.

Functional Equivalence

Functional equivalence must preserve the relevant internal causal relations, including what states would do under different inputs. A recording that says “ouch” and a system that learns, redirects attention and revises expectations after injury need not be equivalent. The strong consciousness claim is that preserving the full relevant organization and corresponding states preserves experience. Chalmers’s organizational-invariance argument distinguishes dependence under the laws of nature from logical or metaphysical necessity; the prompt’s “necessarily” therefore needs that qualification.

Multiple Realizability

Functionalism is closely associated with the concept of multiple realizability, which suggests that mental states can be realized in multiple ways across different physical substrates. This idea supports the notion that minds could potentially be built out of alternative materials (like computer hardware), provided these materials are arranged to perform the same functions as the neurons in a human brain.

Criticism and Challenges

Despite its influence, functionalism faces several criticisms and challenges. Critics argue that it might oversimplify the complexities of mental states, especially how subjective experiences (qualia) emerge from physical processes. Moreover, some philosophers contend that functionalism cannot adequately explain the phenomenological aspects of consciousness—how it feels to experience mental states.

This debate remains a lively area of inquiry in the philosophy of mind, highlighting the ongoing quest to understand the nexus between mind, body, and function.

Gemini response · editorial edition

Functionalism and the Mind-Body Problem

Functionalism is a prominent theory in the philosophy of mind that tackles the mind-body problem – the question of how mental states (beliefs, desires, pains) relate to physical states (brain activity). Functionalism offers a unique perspective on subjectivity and its emergence from material properties.

The Core Tenet: Functional Roles Define Mental States

Functionalism emphasizes the functional role of mental states over their underlying physical composition. A mental state, according to functionalism, is defined by its causal relations:

  • Inputs: How it receives information from the senses and other mental states.
  • Internal processing: How it manipulates information within the system.
  • Outputs: How it influences behavior and other mental states.

Think of a thermostat as an analogy for a functional state, not as an established example of a mind. Its temperature-control state has a role:

  • Input: It receives temperature readings from the environment.
  • Internal processing: It compares the reading to a set point.
  • Output: It triggers heating or cooling mechanisms based on the comparison.

A belief that it is raining has a much richer role: it interacts with the desire to stay dry, memories of forecasts and plans to take an umbrella. Functionalism identifies the belief through that network of relations, not through one behavior or one fixed group of neurons.

Subjectivity from Functional Organization

The materialist version under discussion identifies subjective states with states of an appropriately organized physical system. The general role-based account does not by itself establish that all realizers must be physical or settle whether phenomenal consciousness is fully functional.

Here’s how functionalism explains subjectivity:

  • The proposed roles must be sufficient for experience, not merely necessary conditions that also occur without it.
  • On the strong functionalist account of consciousness, a system with the same relevant causal organization in corresponding states would share the experience; this is the claim to be defended, not an empirical test already passed by computers.
  • The specific material makeup (neurons vs. silicon chips) wouldn’t matter as long as the functional organization replicated the human mind.

The proposal locates subjectivity in what an organized system does internally and in relation to its environment. Adding complexity without specifying those roles would leave the central claim unexplained.

However, it’s important to note that functionalism is not a monolithic theory. There are different varieties that debate the nature of these functional roles and how specific they need to be to generate subjectivity.

Claude response · editorial edition

Functionalism is a prominent theory in the philosophy of mind that attempts to provide an account of mental states and consciousness. The central tenet of this view is that mental states are defined by their functional roles rather than their underlying physical or material composition.

Functional Role

According to functionalism, what matters for a mental state is not the specific material substrate that realizes it, but rather the causal relations it bears to sensory inputs, other mental states, and behavioral outputs. In other words, a mental state is characterized by its functional role within the cognitive system, which is the way it interacts with and is influenced by other states and inputs, as well as the way it influences behavior and other states.

Multiple Realizability

One of the key motivations for functionalism is the idea of multiple realizability. This principle states that the same mental state or cognitive process can be realized or implemented by different physical systems or substrates. For example, the mental state of “believing that it is raining” could be realized in the neural circuitry of a human brain, the silicon circuitry of a computer, or even in a hypothetical system made of entirely different materials, as long as the relevant functional roles are preserved.

The Chinese Room Argument

Searle’s Chinese Room challenges the inference from rule-governed symbol manipulation to understanding: a person following instructions can produce Chinese responses without understanding Chinese. A functionalist can reply that the person is only one component and that the relevant candidate is the whole organized system. Matching input and output alone does not establish matching internal causal organization. The dispute tests what would count as understanding; it is not a demonstration that every functional account fails.

Machine Consciousness

Functionalism has significant implications for the question of machine consciousness. If mental states are defined solely by their functional roles, then it is conceivable that a sufficiently complex and appropriately configured computer system could instantiate the functional roles necessary for consciousness, even though it is made of silicon rather than biological neurons.

Criticism and Challenges

While functionalism has been influential, it has also faced numerous criticisms and challenges. Some philosophers argue that it fails to adequately account for the qualitative, subjective aspects of conscious experience, known as qualia. Others question whether functional roles can be specified with sufficient precision to capture the richness of mental states. Additionally, the problem of absent qualia and inverted qualia have been raised as potential counterexamples to functionalism.

Overall, functionalism offers a compelling and influential perspective on the nature of mental states, but it remains a subject of ongoing debate and refinement within the philosophy of mind.

Responses to the explanatory gap

ChatGPT response · editorial edition

Functionalists and the Explanatory Gap

Functionalism addresses the concern that subjective experiences (or qualia) seem explanatorily distinct from purely material explanations through several key arguments:

1. The Role of Functional Organization

Functionalists argue that mental states, including subjective experiences, are defined by their causal roles within a system. This means that any state that fulfills a particular functional role in the system is that mental state, regardless of the physical medium that constitutes it. This approach aims to bridge the gap by explaining that what matters for the emergence of subjectivity is not the material substrate itself, but rather the dynamic organization and interaction patterns between components.

2. The Argument from Multiple Realizability

Multiple realizability says that a mental role may have different physical implementations. It helps explain why one neural material need not be privileged. It does not explain why any implementation feels like anything: a critic can accept diverse implementations of cognition while continuing to question the link to experience.

3. The Systematic Explanation of Mental Phenomena

Functionalists often argue that subjective experiences, while phenomenologically distinct, are not beyond the reach of scientific explanation. By understanding mental states in terms of their functions within the larger system (e.g., how pain leads to withdrawal behaviors or certain thoughts lead to problem-solving actions), functionalism provides a framework for systematically relating subjective experiences to observable and describable physical processes.

4. Appeals to Future Scientific Advancements

Some functionalists treat the gap as a limitation of present knowledge rather than proof of a separate mental substance. That is a research position, not a guarantee of future success. A stronger account must specify the roles involved and explain why the apparent remainder is either included in them or based on a mistaken conception of experience.

Criticism and Continuing Debate

The hard problem asks why the relevant processes are accompanied by experience at all. It does not establish in advance that a physical explanation is impossible. Critics argue that predicting reports and behavior still leaves out what pain feels like; functionalists dispute whether a complete functional account could leave that out.

Functionalism continues to adapt and respond to these criticisms, illustrating the dynamic and evolving nature of debates in the philosophy of mind.

Gemini response · editorial edition

The Explanatory Gap and Functionalist Response

Functionalism faces a challenge from the subjective nature of our experience. We feel our thoughts and experiences in a way that physical descriptions often seem inadequate to capture. This feeling of an explanatory gap between subjective experience and physical explanations raises doubts about functionalism’s ability to fully account for the mind.

Here’s how functionalists address this concern:

  • Acknowledging the Feeling: Functionalists recognize the intuitive appeal of the explanatory gap. It seems that a purely physical account might never fully capture the richness of subjective experience, like the redness of a rose.
  • Limited understanding: One reply is that introspection gives access to experience without revealing its implementation. Feeling no neurons at work does not show that neurons are absent. This blocks one inference to non-materiality, but does not itself explain why the activity has a felt character.

Analogy: An ant colony illustrates how organization can explain capacities absent from isolated components. It does not establish that the colony has a collective experience. The analogy supports studying relations among parts, while leaving the question of feeling open.

Similarly, functionalism suggests that our subjective experience might be an emergent property arising from the complex interplay of functional states in the brain. Even if we don’t have a complete physical explanation yet, it doesn’t mean one isn’t possible in principle.

  • Focus on Function, Not Material: Functionalists emphasize that the key to understanding the mind lies in its functional roles, not the specific physical material it’s made of. As long as a system can replicate the functional organization of a human brain, it could potentially have subjective experiences even if it’s made of entirely different material (like silicon chips in a computer).

The shift to function offers a way to investigate the gap: identify how perception, memory, attention and self-reports interact, then ask whether anything about experience remains unexplained. A critic may accept the resulting account of access to information while rejecting its sufficiency for felt experience.

Claude response · editorial edition

Functionalists have several ways of addressing the argument that subjective minds seem to be explanatorily distinct from material explanations:

Functional Definition of Subjectivity

One approach is to provide a functional definition of subjectivity or conscious experience itself. Functionalists could argue that what we refer to as subjective experience is simply the functional role played by certain higher-order mental states or processes within the cognitive system. Under this view, the feeling of subjective experience arises from the specific causal interactions and functional roles of these states, rather than being an ontologically distinct phenomenon.

Multiple Realizability and Substrate Neutrality

Multiple realizability concerns what can implement a mental role, not why subjectivity seems different from an objective description. Substrate neutrality means that material differences do not matter when the relevant causal organization is preserved. That conditional claim still needs a defense when extended from cognitive abilities to qualia.

Explanatory Gap

Some functionalists acknowledge the existence of an explanatory gap between functional descriptions and the subjective character of experience. However, they may argue that this gap does not necessarily imply that subjective experience is ontologically distinct or irreducible to functional roles. The explanatory gap could be due to limitations in our current understanding or our inability to fully capture the complexity of the functional roles involved in subjective experience.

Phenomenal Consciousness as a Functional Role

A proposed functional account identifies consciousness with information being integrated and available for reasoning, memory and action. That directly addresses access consciousness. Whether it also captures phenomenal consciousness—the felt character of experience—is precisely the disputed step; using the same word for both would conceal it.

Elimination of the Subjective-Objective Distinction

A more radical reply rejects a fundamental division between subjective and objective reality. It can still preserve a difference between first-person acquaintance and third-person description. Denying two kinds of substance need not deny the experience being described or imply that every functionalist rejects the distinction.

While the problem of subjective experience remains a significant challenge for functionalism, these strategies demonstrate how functionalists can attempt to accommodate or redefine the notion of subjectivity within their theoretical framework.

Understanding the Material Substrate and Subjective Experiences

The argument that subjective experiences or qualia—the internal, personal sensations like the taste of chocolate or the pain of a burn—can be fully explained through the connectivity and interaction of material substrates in the brain hinges on several foundational concepts in neuroscience and philosophy of mind.

1. Neural Correlates of Consciousness

Neural correlates are neural conditions systematically associated with conscious states. Correlation alone does not establish which activity generates an experience, enables it, or follows from reporting it. Interventions and carefully controlled comparisons help separate these possibilities; even causal evidence would need interpretation before it establishes a complete theory of qualia.

2. Integration of Information

Integrated Information Theory (IIT) offers a distinct proposal about consciousness and a system’s intrinsic causal structure. It is not simply the claim that more connections mean more consciousness, nor a generic confirmation of computational functionalism. IIT 4.0 explicitly distinguishes systems with the same input–output function but different internal causal structures. Its account remains a theoretical proposal to evaluate, rather than a settled explanation of experience.

3. Emergence Theory

Emergence names a family of ways to relate whole-system properties to interacting parts. In a modest sense, a property can depend on organization even though no isolated component has it. Stronger claims that consciousness has irreducible powers go further. Calling experience “emergent” identifies the explanatory task; it does not supply the missing mechanism or settle which sense applies.

4. Functionalism and Systemic Organization

For the materialist functionalist, the substrate subsumes experience because the experienced state is the organized physical process, not a second product floating above it. For pain, the account must connect bodily information with aversion, attention, memory, expectations and possible actions. Withdrawal alone is too thin: a reflex can occur without establishing the whole proposed conscious state.

5. Addressing Non-Material Intuitions

The subject has access to the hurt or color without inspecting the machinery that realizes it. The functionalist can therefore explain why an experience does not present itself as a neural description. But explaining that impression and explaining the experience itself are separate burdens. The former weakens an intuition-based argument for non-materiality; it does not automatically discharge the latter.

These approaches converge on the importance of organization while making different commitments. The curator’s proposal is strongest when stated as an identity claim to be explained and tested: this organized activity is the experiencing. Correlation, integration and emergence cannot simply be added together as though each independently proved that identity.

The Mystery of Qualia and Functionalism’s Materialist Response

Qualia, the “what-it-is-like” nature of subjective experience – the redness of a rose, the sharp pang of pain – pose a significant challenge to functionalism. Here, we delve deeper into how functionalism attempts to explain qualia through the material substrate of the brain.

The Challenge of Subjectivity:

Imagine biting into a lemon. Its felt tartness is a quale; “qualia” is the plural. Neural processing can explain discrimination, salivation, memory and avoidance, but the disputed question is whether specifying the relevant organization also explains the felt sourness.

Here, functionalism proposes that the richness of our subjective experience arises from the complex connectivity within the brain.

Material Substrate and the Dance of Neurons:

The brain is a vast network of interconnected neurons. These neurons communicate through electrical signals and chemical messengers, creating intricate patterns of activity. Functionalism suggests that it’s these patterns, the specific ways neurons fire and connect, that underlie qualia.

  • Orchestra analogy: A symphony’s coordinated sound depends on relations among instruments, not on one note in isolation. This illustrates a whole-system pattern. The listener’s experience introduces a further conscious system, so it would be circular to use that experience as proof that coordinated sound itself explains subjectivity.

Similarly, the subjective feeling of sourness might arise from a specific pattern of neuronal activity in the brain’s taste processing areas. The intricate connections between these neurons – how they amplify, inhibit, and integrate signals – might be what generates the qualia of sourness.

Subjectivity from Complexity:

What matters is the kind of organization, not simply its size. A large network could be arranged so that signals never support memory, integrated discrimination or flexible control. A functionalist account must identify the relevant relations and show why they suffice for the kind of experience being explained.

  • Kaleidoscope analogy: Rearranging mirrors changes a visible pattern without adding a new kind of material. This helps illustrate dependence on arrangement, but the device’s patterns do not establish that it experiences them.

Likewise, changing neural connections may change the capacities and experiences of a brain. The analogy motivates investigating which arrangements matter; it cannot bridge the explanatory gap merely by multiplying connections.

An Ongoing Discussion:

While functionalism offers a materialist explanation for qualia, it doesn’t eliminate the feeling of subjectivity. The feeling of sourness still feels inherently subjective, even if it’s ultimately generated by the brain’s physical processes.

This is an ongoing debate in philosophy of mind. Some argue that functionalism might never fully capture the subjective nature of experience. Others believe that with a deeper understanding of the brain’s functional architecture, we might one day be able to fully explain qualia in physical terms.

Dr. Emily: But that’s exactly where functionalism falls short, Mark. It explains functioning, yes, but not experiencing. You speak of roles and systems, but how do these lead to a personal, inner experience? It seems like an explanatory leap.

Dr. Mark: It’s a common critique. Yet, consider the concept of multiple realizability. It suggests that different physical substrates can give rise to the same mental states if they perform the same functions. Doesn’t this support the idea that subjective experiences are indeed tied to functions rather than specific materials?

Dr. Emily: Multiple realizability supports the flexibility of realization, not the genesis of consciousness. Just because something can be realized in multiple ways doesn’t inherently explain the qualitative aspects of how it feels to be in those states.

Dr. Mark: I accept that objection. An AI saying “I’m in pain” would not be enough. My claim concerns a system with the full relevant internal relations—aversion, attention, learning, memory and their effects under different circumstances. Dr. Emily: That’s a tricky analogy. Just because an AI claims to feel pain doesn’t mean it truly experiences it. It could be merely simulating the responses associated with pain—a performance, not an experience. Philosophical zombies in thought experiments serve this exact argument.

Dr. Mark: Philosophical zombies are indeed a powerful thought experiment but let’s not forget, they are just that—thought experiments. They help us question and probe, yet they don’t prove that material configurations cannot give rise to experiences.

Dr. Emily: Consider the familiar textbook identity “pain is C-fiber stimulation.” It is an illustrative proposal, not a complete neuroscience of pain. If different organisms realize pain differently, how can one physical type identify every case?

Dr. Mark: That’s where functionalism is more robust than identity theory. It doesn’t tie the experience of pain to specific physical structures but to the functional equivalents across different organisms, which could indeed include AI or other non-human entities.

Dr. Emily: Still, tying back to our original point, functionalism adequately addresses how systems operate but seems to skirt around why certain functions should give rise to subjective experience at all. Isn’t there a qualitative difference between describing functions and describing experiences?

Dr. Mark: The distinction matters, but I question whether a complete causal description could really omit the experience while preserving every disposition to attend to, remember and judge it. Prediction alone is weaker than that identity claim.

Dr. Emily: It matters philosophically, and that’s crucial. The qualitative aspect might not alter the system’s operations, but it changes how we interpret and relate to these systems—be they human or artificial.

Dr. Mark: Then practical success would support the framework without settling the identity claim. I would also ask why first-person acquaintance should reveal the material nature of its own implementation. Its failure to do so need not indicate a separate substance.

Dr. Emily: Perhaps. Or perhaps it points to a deeper reality beyond the material—one that materialist and functionalist frameworks can approach but, at their limits, fail to grasp fully.

Dr. Mark: It’s a fascinating debate, Emily. One that pushes the boundaries of how we understand mind and consciousness. Let’s keep this dialogue open and continue exploring these profound questions.

Dr. Emily: Agreed, Mark. Exploring these questions is essential, as it shapes not only our philosophical landscape but also our approach to future technologies and the ethical considerations surrounding them.

A Functionalist and a Dualist Walk into a Bar.

Setting: Two philosophers, Anya (a dualist) and Ben (a functionalist), are seated at a table in a quiet corner of a university pub. Their conversation deepens as they debate the nature of consciousness.

Anya: So, Ben, you’re telling me a machine, made of wires and circuits, could experience the world in the same way I do? It seems absurd.

Ben: Not necessarily, Anya. It depends on how the machine is wired. If it can perform the same functional roles as the human brain – receiving sensory input, processing information, and generating outputs – then it’s conceivable that it could have subjective experiences like us.

Anya: But surely there’s more to it than just function. There’s a qualitative aspect to consciousness, a “what-it-is-like” to experience the redness of a rose or the sting of a wasp. How can physical processes replicate that?

Ben: That’s a fair point. The subjective quality of experience is a challenge. But perhaps it emerges from the complexity of the functional organization. Like an intricate dance giving rise to a beautiful ballet. The specific patterns of neuronal firing might generate the qualitative character of our experiences.

Anya: I find that analogy unconvincing, Ben. A dance is still a physical phenomenon, governed by the laws of physics. Consciousness seems to be something fundamentally different, a non-material substance that interacts with the brain. Perhaps something like Descartes’ res cogitans, a thinking thing separate from the physical body.

Ben: I understand the appeal of dualism, Anya. But where do you draw the line between the mental and the physical? How does this non-material substance interact with the brain to produce conscious experience? It seems like an unnecessary mystery.

Anya: The mind-body interaction problem is a challenge, I won’t deny that. But the subjective nature of consciousness is a powerful argument for dualism. We have direct access to our own thoughts and feelings in a way we don’t to physical processes.

Ben: Direct acquaintance with an experience does not necessarily reveal what realizes it. I can grant that the pain is real while questioning your inference from how it is known to what kind of thing it is.

Anya: Even if that’s true, it doesn’t negate the fact that there’s a qualitative difference between mental and physical states. Think about pain. Can you imagine explaining the searing agony of a migraine to someone who has never experienced it? Physical descriptions of neurons firing seem utterly inadequate.

Ben: A description need not reproduce what it describes. Someone who has never felt a migraine might learn its causal organization without acquiring the experience. Whether that leaves out a fact about the world or a form of acquaintance is the point we need to argue, not assume.

Anya: Maybe. But functionalism seems to downplay the richness of subjective experience. There’s a unity to consciousness, a sense of “me” that goes beyond the firing of neurons. How can functionalism account for that?

Ben: That sense of self might emerge from the integrated information processing within the brain. Different parts working together to create a unified whole. The functional roles might be not just about computation but also about integration and self-representation.

Anya: Even if functionalism can explain the mechanics of selfhood, doesn’t it leave something out? The raw feeling of existence itself? There’s a certain “je ne sais quoi” to consciousness that feels irreducible to physical processes.

Ben: Perhaps that feeling is itself a product of the brain’s function. The feeling of existing might be a complex mental state arising from the constant stream of information processing and self-referential thoughts. We might not have a complete understanding yet, but that doesn’t mean a full explanation isn’t possible in principle.

Anya: You may be right, Ben. Perhaps consciousness is more emergent than we realize. But even if functionalism is on the right track, I still have a feeling there’s more to the story. What about near-death experiences? People often report a sense of heightened awareness or even detachment from their bodies during such events. How can functionalism explain these experiences if consciousness is solely dependent on the brain?

Ben: A report of detachment does not by itself establish experience independent of brain activity. We would need evidence about the timing of the experience, the relevant physiological conditions and how the report was formed. The materialist account must explain the report; uncertainty about its mechanism does not establish a non-material mind.

Anya: Then I should distinguish degrees of awareness from degrees of independence from the brain. The reports raise questions, but our disagreement remains: would explaining their mechanism also explain what the experience felt like?

Dualist: But that seems like a convenient way to dismiss counterexamples. Shouldn’t we take seriously the possibility that subjective experience may be irreducible to functional roles, rather than simply assuming that our current theories are incomplete?

Functionalist: The challenge is to specify the supposed difference while holding all relevant relations fixed. If the subject can notice an inversion and remember the contrast, something functional has changed. A wholly unnoticed inversion avoids that reply, but its possibility needs argument on both sides.

Dualist: Perhaps, but I fear that in doing so, you risk losing the very thing you’re trying to explain – the richness and irreducibility of subjective experience itself.

Functionalist: Or perhaps by pushing past our initial intuitions and preconceptions, we may arrive at a deeper understanding of the true nature of consciousness, one that seamlessly integrates the subjective and objective realms.

Dualist: I must admit, your arguments give me pause. Perhaps my intuitions about the irreducibility of subjective experience are shaped by my own limited viewpoint. And yet, I cannot shake the sense that there is something profoundly distinct about the first-person, qualitative character of consciousness.

Functionalist: I understand that sense of conviction. The subjective nature of experience seems so fundamental to our being. But I would argue that this is precisely why we must interrogate it rigorously. What if our felt sense of subjectivity is itself part of a deeper functional process we don’t yet fully grasp?

Dualist: Even if that were true, it seems there would still be an irreducible qualitative residue left over – the raw feeling of what it is like to be that functional process. A functional role cannot fully capture the intrinsic nature of experiential states.

Functionalist: I would question whether “intrinsic” has been defined to exclude causal roles from the start. If so, the conclusion is built into the vocabulary. If you instead identify a feature of experience that the account misses, I must address that feature directly.

Dualist: You’re asking me to relinquish one of the most basic features of human experience – the sense of inwardness, of subjective awareness. Can we really theorize that away?

Functionalist: I want to preserve inwardness as a feature of the organized subject, not eliminate it. Whether that account succeeds is still contested; the mind–body dispute has not already been resolved in my favor.

Dualist: A bold vision, to be sure. And one that, I must admit, holds a certain appeal in its potential for unification and explanatory power. But can it truly capture the full majesty and mystery of human consciousness?

Functionalist: That is the challenge we face. Any truly comprehensive theory must do justice to the depth and complexity of subjective experience while also providing a coherent, empirically grounded account of its origins and mechanisms. It’s a delicate balance, but I believe functionalism provides a promising framework.

Dualist: Then our task is a daunting one – to expand and refine our theories until they can fully accommodate and explain the subjective character of consciousness without diminishing or discarding it. A worthy goal, though the journey will be long and arduous.

Functionalist: We can make that task concrete by separating three questions: what organization is present, what experience is reported, and why we think the two are identical or distinct. Agreement about the first two need not settle the third.

Dualist: You make a compelling case. While I may never fully relinquish my sense of subjective distinctness, I am persuaded that the functionalist approach holds great promise. Perhaps by combining our distinct perspectives and relentlessly pursuing the truth, we may one day achieve a unified theory of consciousness that honors both the subjective and objective domains.

Functionalist: Let us continue on that basis: shared investigation, with the disagreement about subjective character still explicit. Neither my explanatory ambition nor your sense of distinctness is a substitute for the argument.
