Practicality The analogy effectively highlights a practical approach to testing the reliability of tools (whether a compass or our cognition) through direct experience and testing rather than through seeking external validation from a creator or manufacturer. This emphasizes an empirical approach to understanding functionality and reliability.

Acknowledgment of Variability The argument recognizes the variability in cognitive reliability across different people and across different times in a person’s life. This is an important consideration, underscoring that cognitive capabilities are not static or universally consistent, which challenges any simplistic assessment of human cognition.

Simplification of Cognitive Assessment The analogy oversimplifies the process of evaluating cognition compared to assessing a compass. Human cognition is vastly more complex and influenced by numerous factors—biological, environmental, psychological—that a simple tool like a compass does not encapsulate. The methods suggested (e.g., crossword puzzles, frequency of losing things) may not comprehensively reflect the nuances of cognitive reliability or its underlying mechanisms.

False Equivalence While a compass has a singular function (to point north), human cognition encompasses a wide range of functions and processes, including memory, reasoning, emotion regulation, and more. Testing a compass against a known standard (like magnetic north) is straightforward, whereas assessing the accuracy and reliability of cognitive processes involves multifaceted and often subjective criteria, which can vary by context and by what cognitive function is being tested.

Underestimation of Theoretical Underpinnings By dismissing the exploration of the origins of cognition (whether evolutionary or divine), the argument potentially overlooks the importance of understanding these underlying mechanisms in enhancing our understanding of cognition. Theoretical insights can provide critical perspectives that inform and refine practical testing methods and interpretations of what constitutes “reliability” in human cognition.

The argument presented draws an analogy between testing the reliability of a found compass by using it directly, versus consulting its manufacturer, and assessing the reliability of our cognitive faculties through empirical self-testing rather than appealing to evolutionary or divine explanations of the origin of our minds. Here’s an assessment of the key points made 1. The analogy of testing a found compass by using it, rather than contacting the manufacturer, is apt and highlights the practical value of direct empirical validation over theoretical or external assurances. 2. The point about the variability of cognitive reliability across individuals and within an individual’s lifespan is well-taken. It rightly suggests that cognitive reliability is not a binary state but exists on a continuum, which aligns with real-world observations. 3. The suggestion of self-testing cognitive abilities through activities like puzzles, memory tests, or professional assessments is a reasonable approach to directly evaluate one’s cognitive faculties empirically. 4. The critique of invoking evolutionary or religious explanations as a “confirmation” of cognitive reliability is valid. Such explanations may provide a theoretical account of the origin of cognition but do not necessarily speak to its current functional state or degree of reliability in a given individual. Overall, the argument makes a compelling case for the merits of directly testing and empirically validating cognitive abilities, rather than relying on external assurances or theoretical accounts of their origins. It recognizes the inherent variability and dynamic nature of cognitive reliability, which necessitates ongoing self-assessment and measurement. However, a potential counterargument could be that while direct self-testing is pragmatic and valuable, it may not capture the full scope or depth of cognitive capabilities, which could benefit from a more comprehensive understanding of the underlying neurological, evolutionary, or philosophical underpinnings of human cognition. Ultimately, the argument presents a well-reasoned perspective favoring a pragmatic, empirical approach to assessing cognitive reliability, without dismissing the potential value of theoretical frameworks in providing a broader context for understanding the nature and origins of human cognition.

Key Argument from Lewis Lewis posits that if our brains are not designed with a purpose (i.e., for thinking), then our thoughts are merely accidental by-products of material processes. He suggests that without a deliberate design, trusting our cognition to reliably produce true thoughts is as random as expecting spilled milk to form a map. This argument is often used to support a theistic view where a belief in a divine intelligence is seen as foundational to trusting human cognition.

Source of Cognition Lewis argues that without a divine or intelligent source, there is no basis to trust the cognitive processes since they are not aimed at producing truth but are merely accidental. This contrasts with the argument that the source of cognition need not confirm its reliability, as practical tests of cognition can still be applied to assess its effectiveness regardless of its origin.

Testing Cognition Independently Following the analogy of the compass, just as one can test a compass’s reliability through practical means regardless of its origin, one could argue that we can test the reliability of our cognitive processes through empirical and practical means (like scientific methods and logical reasoning). This counters Lewis’s point by suggesting that the practical utility and consistent success of cognitive processes in navigating the world and producing effective results can serve as evidence of their reliability.

Evolutionary Perspective From an evolutionary perspective, one could argue that while no specific “intelligence” designed the brain for thinking, the processes of natural selection have shaped the brain to produce generally reliable outcomes for survival and reproduction. Thus, while the brain’s development is guided by natural processes rather than a conscious designer, these processes nonetheless confer a kind of reliability to our thoughts, aimed at practical and survival-oriented ends.

Circular Reasoning Lewis’s argument can also be critiqued for potentially being circular: one must first assume the existence of God to trust cognition, which one would use to justify the belief in God. This raises complex questions about the foundations of belief and knowledge.

Empirical Validation of Cognitive Processes Lewis argues from a premise that assumes cognitive processes require a divine designer to be reliable. Yet, empirical evidence and scientific understanding show that cognitive processes can be tested and validated within their own frameworks. For instance, through logical consistency checks and evidence evaluation, we can verify the reliability of our reasoning. Such methods are routinely used in scientific inquiry and critical thinking, suggesting that cognition can indeed form reliable conclusions, even if it evolved without explicit purposeful design.

Evolutionary Adaptation From an evolutionary perspective, while there might not be an intelligent designer, natural selection has shaped cognitive faculties that are generally effective at navigating the world. The reliability of our cognition is not a mere accident but a result of evolutionary pressures that favored individuals who could accurately interpret their environments and make beneficial decisions. Hence, cognition geared towards survival and reproduction tends to be reliable in these contexts, providing a non-theistic foundation for trusting our mental processes.

Circular Reasoning in Divine Trust Lewis’s argument requires one to presuppose the existence of God to validate cognition, which in itself is used to argue for God’s existence. This appears to be circular reasoning: using cognition to prove the reliability of cognition based on the existence of God, which cognition itself is meant to establish. A more robust approach would be to demonstrate the reliability of cognition through independent means that do not presuppose the conclusion they are meant to support.

Alternative Methods of Validation As discussed, the reliability of cognition can be assessed through various independent tests, such as consistency with other knowledge, empirical verification, and the ability to predict and control outcomes in our environment. These methods provide grounds for trusting our cognitive processes that do not rely on the assumption of divine intervention.
