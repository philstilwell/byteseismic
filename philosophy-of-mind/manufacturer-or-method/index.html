Assess the following argument on ascertaining the reliability of our mental faculties:

If you find a compass in the woods, should you a) contact the manufacturer to determine its accuracy, or b) simply test it to see if it gets you home?

It would be foolish to request of the manufacturer a promise that a found compass is accurate. You might instead simply test the compass against the sun and stars, or simply see whether it gets you out of the woods to home.

Likewise, we would be foolish to explore evolution or some proposed God to determine whether our cognition is currently reliable for two reasons.

1: The reliability of human cognition varies across humans and within the experience of a single human at different stages of their life. Reliability is not a single number but is measured on a gradient. Some people can remember the names of nearly everyone they meet while others can barely remember their own name. And the degree of reliability of everyone diminishes as the reach older age. Invoking the source of our cognition to know whether it is “accurate” is to ignore differing degrees of cognition among humans and the ebb and flow of our cognition during our lifetimes.

2: We can simply test our cognition. We can do this ourselves by tracking our success on crossword puzzles or the frequency we lose things, or by consulting professionals with experience assessing cognition.

To conclude, just as we would be foolish to call up the manufacturer of the compass we find in the woods to ascertain its degree of reliability, we are also foolish if we invoke some material process or some God as a “confirmation” our mind is reliable.

ChatGPT response · editorial edition

The argument presented attempts to draw an analogy between testing the reliability of a compass found in the woods and assessing the reliability of human cognition. Here’s a breakdown of the argument’s strengths and weaknesses:

Strengths:

  1. Practicality: The analogy effectively highlights a practical approach to testing the reliability of tools (whether a compass or our cognition) through direct experience and testing rather than through seeking external validation from a creator or manufacturer. This emphasizes an empirical approach to understanding functionality and reliability.
  2. Variability: Performance differs across people, tasks, and circumstances. The prompt’s claim that everyone’s cognition simply diminishes with age is too broad: different abilities follow different paths. Neither a person’s age nor an origin story establishes their present reliability.

Weaknesses:

  1. Simplification of Cognitive Assessment: The analogy oversimplifies the process of evaluating cognition compared to assessing a compass. Human cognition is vastly more complex and influenced by numerous factors—biological, environmental, psychological—that a simple tool like a compass does not encapsulate. The methods suggested (e.g., crossword puzzles, frequency of losing things) may not comprehensively reflect the nuances of cognitive reliability or its underlying mechanisms.
  2. False Equivalence: While a compass has a singular function (to point north), human cognition encompasses a wide range of functions and processes, including memory, reasoning, emotion regulation, and more. Testing a compass against a known standard (like magnetic north) is straightforward, whereas assessing the accuracy and reliability of cognitive processes involves multifaceted and often subjective criteria, which can vary by context and by what cognitive function is being tested.
  3. Underestimation of Theoretical Underpinnings: By dismissing the exploration of the origins of cognition (whether evolutionary or divine), the argument potentially overlooks the importance of understanding these underlying mechanisms in enhancing our understanding of cognition. Theoretical insights can provide critical perspectives that inform and refine practical testing methods and interpretations of what constitutes “reliability” in human cognition.

Conclusion:

Practical tests supply evidence about specified abilities under specified conditions. Information from a manufacturer can also be useful—calibration records are not merely a promise—just as knowledge of cognitive mechanisms can inform testing. The strongest conclusion is that origin alone does not certify present performance; self-testing is neither comprehensive nor independent of all cognition.

Claude response · editorial edition

The compass analogy supports testing present performance rather than treating an origin story as a certificate of accuracy. Its four useful points are direct comparison with a standard, variation across people and occasions, the possibility of assessment, and the insufficiency of origin alone. But manufacturer specifications can inform a compass test; cognition likewise benefits from knowledge of mechanisms and limitations. Crossword scores and misplaced objects are informal observations, not comprehensive assessments. Age-related change is not uniform across every ability or person. A test supplies evidence within its measured domain and can itself be mistaken; it does not certify every conclusion its subject reaches.

Key Argument from Lewis:

Philosophical Counterpoint:

  • Evolutionary reply: Selection is not equivalent to a single random spill. It can favor capacities useful in recurring environments, including some accurate discriminations. This offers a possible explanation of domain-specific reliability, not a guarantee that every adaptive belief is true or that abstract reasoning is universally reliable.

The practical reply challenges the inference from an undesigned origin to untrustworthy thought. Lewis argues that if our minds and thoughts are merely byproducts of physical processes, without any intelligent design or creative intent behind them, then we would have no grounds to trust the validity of our thinking itself. In essence, while the source of our cognition (whether divine, evolutionary, or otherwise) may offer different explanatory frameworks, it does not inherently guarantee or undermine the reliability of our mental faculties. The pragmatic approach advocated in the previous argument – to directly test and validate our cognitive capabilities through empirical means – remains a viable and necessary path, irrespective of the metaphysical or theoretical underpinnings one ascribes to the origin of human cognition.

The positions therefore do not converge on empirical validation. Lewis questions the grounds for trusting reason on a naturalistic account; the practical reply offers fallible assessment without first resolving that foundational dispute.

Claude response · editorial edition

Here is a potential rebuttal to the C.S. Lewis quote based on the points raised in this discussion:

While Lewis raises a valid concern about the implications of human cognition being merely a byproduct of unguided physical processes, his conclusion that this would inherently undermine the trustworthiness of our reasoning is problematic for a few key reasons:

First, Lewis frames the issue as an all-or-nothing dilemma – either our minds are designed by God as a source of truth, or they are random accidents unworthy of trust. However, this is a false dichotomy. Even if our cognitive faculties arose through non-designed evolutionary processes, we can still potentially validate their functional reliability through empirical cross-modal testing without appealing to their origins.

A task integrating several abilities provides evidence of joint performance, not proof that every neural component is sound. Repeated tests, different methods, and external checks can reduce some errors, while shared assumptions remain a limitation.

Secondly, the rebuttal contests the claimed dependence of rational warrant on a divine source. Simply labeling that dependence circular does not refute it; the disputed premise needs an argument.

The relevant standard should be made explicit: fallible practical reliability differs from certainty about the foundations of reason. The quotation does not by itself show that Lewis required every human judgment to be infallible.

In contrast, the empirical approach acknowledges the inescapable human limitations and variability in cognitive performance, while still enabling us to gauge and strive to improve our rational capacities through ongoing self-assessment and cross-validation, irrespective of any particular stance on the origins or cosmic underwriting of our mental faculties.

While Lewis’s perspective highlights important philosophical considerations, a pragmatic program of rigorously testing and evaluating human reasoning through multi-modal performance benchmarks allows us to navigate this conundrum through empiricism and perpetual self-calibration, rather than insisting on uncheckable external validations of our cognitive integrity.
