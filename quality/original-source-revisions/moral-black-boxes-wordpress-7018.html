P1: If you cannot question the moral goodness of a deity, you cannot rationally claim that deity is the legitimate arbiter of morality.

Conclusion: You cannot rational claim that Deity X is the legitimate arbiter of morality.

The argument presented follows a logical structure, specifically a form of deductive reasoning called a syllogism, where it attempts to establish a conclusion based on the logical relationship between premises. Here’s a step-by-step analysis of each premise and the conclusion:

Premise 1 (P1): If you cannot question the moral goodness of a deity, you cannot rationally claim that deity is the legitimate arbiter of morality.

  • Assessment: This premise suggests that the ability to question and critically evaluate the moral goodness of a being is necessary to rationally affirm its role as a legitimate moral arbiter. The underlying assumption here is that rational affirmation requires the possibility of doubt and critique. This seems reasonable because it aligns with principles of open inquiry and rational scrutiny.

Premise 2 (P2): If there is no imaginable action of Deity X that would constitute evidence against the moral goodness of Deity X, you then cannot question the moral goodness of Deity X.

  • Assessment: This premise asserts that the ability to question the moral goodness of Deity X is contingent upon the possibility of imagining actions that could count as evidence against their moral goodness. If every possible action by Deity X is compatible with Deity X being morally good, then there is no basis for questioning Deity X’s moral goodness. This premise logically follows the criteria for questioning set forth in P1.

Premise 3 (P3): There is no imaginable action of Deity X that would constitute evidence against the moral goodness of Deity X.

  • Assessment: This premise states a condition about Deity X—that no action can be conceived that would challenge the deity’s moral goodness. The truth of this statement depends heavily on the characteristics attributed to Deity X and how broadly or narrowly “imaginable action” is defined. It requires accepting that Deity X is such that all actions are inherently good, which might not be universally agreed upon but is internally consistent within the framework of the argument.

Conclusion: You cannot rationally claim that Deity X is the legitimate arbiter of morality.

  • Assessment: The conclusion follows logically from the premises. If P1 and P2 are accepted, and if P3 is true (as stipulated), then it logically follows that one cannot rationally claim that Deity X is the legitimate arbiter of morality. This conclusion is dependent on accepting the truth and validity of all preceding premises.

Logical Structure:

  1. P1 establishes a general principle about questioning and rational claims regarding moral arbiters.
  2. P2 and P3 set up a specific scenario involving Deity X, applying the principle from P1 to this specific case.
  3. The conclusion derives naturally from the premises, assuming the premises are true.

Conclusion of Assessment:
The argument is logically valid, meaning that if the premises are true, the conclusion necessarily follows. However, the soundness (truth of the premises) is subject to debate, particularly concerning P3, which assumes a particular theological or philosophical view of Deity X that not all might share. If one rejects P3, the argument’s persuasive power diminishes significantly.

I have seen theists of different sorts attempt to make their God off-limits to questions of moral goodness by claiming their God cannot be morally judged by humans. Comment on any logical flaws in this in light of our discussion above.

The stance that a deity, such as God, cannot be morally judged by humans presents an interesting philosophical and theological claim, and there are several points to consider in light of the discussion above:

  1. Implications for Rational Inquiry: If it is claimed that God is beyond human moral judgment, this aligns with Premise 3 (P3) from the original argument, which states that there is no imaginable action by the deity that would constitute evidence against their moral goodness. In such a scenario, if humans cannot imagine or assess actions that could count as morally good or bad, this limits their ability to rationally evaluate the deity’s moral nature. This essentially places the deity’s morality outside the realm of human understanding and scrutiny.
  2. Contradiction with Premise 1 (P1): The original argument’s Premise 1 suggests that the ability to question the moral goodness of a deity is necessary for rationally affirming that the deity is a legitimate moral arbiter. If a theistic claim posits that God cannot be judged morally by humans, it implicitly suggests that humans cannot question God’s moral goodness. According to P1, this would imply that humans cannot rationally affirm God as a legitimate arbiter of morality.
  3. Issue of Moral Relevance: If God’s actions and motives are completely incomprehensible or beyond human moral judgment, then on what basis can humans understand or accept moral commands purportedly issued by God? The relevance and applicability of a moral system depend significantly on the ability to understand the moral authority from which the commands derive. If God’s morality is inscrutable, it may challenge the coherence or relevance of His moral directives to human life and ethics.
  4. Autonomy and Moral Agency: A claim that God cannot be morally judged by humans might also undermine the notion of human moral agency and autonomy. It suggests a scenario where moral truth is dictated without the possibility of interrogation or understanding, which can conflict with views that see moral agency as involving understanding, questioning, and possibly even dissenting with proposed moral truths.
  5. Logical Coherence: Philosophically, if God is posited as perfectly good and this goodness is beyond human questioning or understanding, it might raise issues about the logical coherence of asserting any knowledge about God’s moral nature. It could lead to a paradox where believers claim knowledge of God’s perfect goodness while simultaneously holding that this goodness cannot be questioned or understood.

The logical flaws or challenges presented by such theistic claims center around the tension between asserting a deity’s moral perfection and the necessary conditions for rational discourse and moral agency. If God’s moral goodness is beyond question, it challenges the ability to rationally engage with and affirm the deity’s moral authority, as highlighted by the premises of the original argument discussed.

The claim by some theists that their God cannot be morally judged or questioned by humans is logically flawed in light of the argument we discussed, and it highlights an inherent tension in their position.

By asserting that their God is off-limits to moral scrutiny or questioning by humans, these theists are effectively embracing premise P3 of the argument: “There is no imaginable action of Deity X that would constitute evidence against the moral goodness of Deity X.”

However, as we established through the logical reasoning of the argument, if one accepts P3 as true, then one must also accept the conclusion: “You cannot rationally claim that Deity X is the legitimate arbiter of morality.”

In other words, by making their God immune to any moral questioning or evidence against its goodness, these theists are undermining their own ability to rationally justify or defend their God as the legitimate source of morality.

The flaw in their position is the attempt to have it both ways – to declare their God as the ultimate moral authority while simultaneously placing it beyond the realm of moral evaluation or criticism. This is logically incoherent because, as premise P1 states, “If you cannot question the moral goodness of a deity, you cannot rationally claim that deity is the legitimate arbiter of morality.”

Any attempt to make a deity’s moral status unquestionable or unfalsifiable inevitably leads to the conclusion that one cannot rationally ground that deity’s status as the arbiter of morality. It becomes a matter of blind faith or dogma rather than a reasoned, defensible position.

For a deity to be rationally accepted as the legitimate source of morality, there must be some conceivable way to evaluate its moral standing, even if that evaluation ultimately affirms the deity’s goodness. Closing off all avenues of moral scrutiny, as these theists aim to do, is self-defeating from a rational and logical standpoint.
