Can someone committed to the absence of objective free will also claim there is subjective free will?

Consider the following elaboration: The notion of personhood, in the mind of the determinist, is simply an subjectively perceived inner loop of causation that truly makes decisions through its determined mechanisms and input in a way that feels free to the subject. Can the determinist not say that free will exists subjectively in the same way they acknowledge that taking offense or getting your hopes up exist subjectively?

Yes, someone committed to the absence of objective free will can indeed claim there is subjective free will. This position typically involves distinguishing between free will as it is understood in an objective, universal sense and free will as it is experienced subjectively by individuals.

From a determinist perspective, objective free will—defined as the capacity to have acted differently under identical conditions—does not exist because all actions are determined by prior states and laws of nature. However, subjective free will refers to the individual’s personal experience of making choices and decisions. Despite the underlying deterministic nature of these processes, from the individual’s internal perspective, it often feels as though they are exercising free choice. This subjective experience doesn’t require the actual metaphysical existence of free will but is about the perception or feeling of being free.

Determinists might argue that subjective experiences, including the feeling of making a free choice, are themselves determined by prior states (such as brain chemistry, environmental factors, and past experiences). However, these experiences are real to the person undergoing them, just as emotions like happiness or sadness are real experiences, though they too are determined by underlying causes.

Therefore, a determinist can acknowledge the subjective existence of free will in much the same way they might recognize other subjective experiences. These are real as psychological phenomena, even if they do not correspond to an objective, metaphysical reality. This acknowledgment doesn’t conflict with determinism but rather fits within its framework by attributing the phenomenon of free will to internal, subjective perceptions rather than to an external, independent capability.

The question of whether someone committed to the absence of objective free will can also claim there is subjective free will is a fascinating and contentious issue in the free will debate. Let me address this without invoking compatibilism directly.

If we accept that the universe operates according to deterministic laws of cause and effect, and that our choices and actions are ultimately the product of prior causes beyond our control (e.g., genes, environment, brain chemistry), then it would seem that objective or metaphysical free will is an incoherent concept. Our decisions and behaviors would be the inevitable result of these causal factors, not the product of some uncaused, contra-causal free will.

However, even in a deterministic universe, we undeniably have a subjective experience of making choices and having a sense of agency. This phenomenological feeling of willing our actions is what could be described as “subjective free will.” The determinist could argue that this inner experience, while illusory from a objective standpoint, is nevertheless a real subjective phenomenon that emerges from the brain’s complex information processing.

Just as a determinist would acknowledge the subjective reality of emotions like anger or hope, despite viewing them as ultimately caused by physical factors, they could similarly accept the felt experience of choice and volition as a subjective reality. The fact that it arises from deterministic brain mechanisms doesn’t negate the reality of that experience itself.

So in essence, the determinist perspective could be that while objective, contra-causal free will is incoherent, the subjective experience of willing our actions is a very real psychological phenomenon that emerges from our brain’s causal dynamics. It is simply an inner model of agency constructed by deterministic neural processes, rather than a miracle of non-physical willing.

So, the determinist need not give up terms that reflect personal agency such as “choose” and “decide” if they are willing to make clear in relevant contexts that they are referring to subjective free will, right?

Exactly! A determinist can continue to use terms like “choose” and “decide” without contradicting their philosophical stance, as long as it’s clear that these terms are understood within the framework of subjective experience. By making it explicit that these words refer to the subjective sense of making choices and decisions, the determinist maintains consistency with their belief in determinism.

In everyday conversation and practical contexts, it’s natural and often necessary to speak about choosing and deciding, because these terms accurately describe how we experience our actions and decision-making processes from the first-person perspective. The determinist can acknowledge that while these experiences are determined by prior causes, the phenomenological aspect—how these actions feel to the individual—is genuinely experienced as choice and decision.

Thus, the use of such terms by a determinist can be seen as a pragmatic concession to the way humans subjectively perceive and navigate the world, rather than a philosophical endorsement of objective free will. This approach allows for a nuanced understanding that respects the complexities of human psychology and social communication within a deterministic framework.

The determinist simply needs to be clear about the subjective/experiential nature of what such language is capturing.

Ultimately, most of our lived experience takes place at the level of this subjective perception of agency. So retaining language implying willing and choice, properly contextualized, can be a pragmatic way for the determinist to communicate while avoiding the philosophical confusion of reifying an incoherent notion of objective contra-causal free will.

Given this conclusion, is there any field other than metaphysics in which a strong determinist stance from a determinist would be productive or required?

A strong determinist stance can be productive and indeed crucial in several fields beyond metaphysics. Here are a few examples:

  1. Neuroscience and Psychology: In these fields, a determinist perspective can be particularly useful in studying how biological, environmental, and psychological factors determine behavior. This viewpoint helps in understanding the causal mechanisms behind mental processes and behaviors, facilitating the development of treatments for mental health issues and improving therapeutic techniques.
  2. Criminal Justice: Determinism can influence approaches to criminal behavior and justice. A determinist might argue that criminal actions are the result of predetermined factors such as genetics, upbringing, and social environment, rather than personal moral failings. This perspective can shift the focus from retribution to rehabilitation, aiming to change the conditions that lead to criminal behavior rather than merely punishing individuals.
  3. Education: Determinism can also play a role in shaping educational methods and philosophies. Recognizing that student behavior and learning are influenced by prior conditions (like background, prior knowledge, and social context) can lead educators to tailor teaching methods and interventions more effectively to meet individual needs.
  4. Ethics and Philosophy of Action: While directly related to metaphysics, these areas benefit from a deterministic perspective by reevaluating notions of moral responsibility and ethical behavior. If actions are determined, it prompts rethinking how to assign moral responsibility and how ethics should guide behavior when true choice is limited.
  5. Artificial Intelligence and Robotics: In AI development, understanding determinism is essential, as it is fundamentally about creating systems that operate according to programmed algorithms and learned data patterns. Acknowledging the deterministic nature of these systems can guide more ethical and responsible AI development, particularly in understanding and predicting AI behavior.
  6. Social and Political Theory: Determinism can help analyze social behaviors and structures, understanding that societal conditions and historical contexts significantly shape individual actions and political systems. This can lead to more effective policy-making that addresses root causes of social issues rather than only their symptoms.

In each of these fields, adopting a determinist perspective enables a deeper understanding of causality and the factors that influence outcomes, which can lead to more effective interventions and policies.

Yes, there are several fields outside of metaphysics where adopting a strong deterministic stance could be productive or even required for a determinist:

  1. Neuroscience and Psychology: A deterministic view aligns well with the scientific understanding that human thoughts, behaviors, and decisions ultimately arise from brain activity, which follows the laws of biology, chemistry, and physics. Embracing determinism grounds psychological research in a naturalistic framework.
  2. Criminal Justice and Jurisprudence: Some argue that a thoroughgoing determinism undermines certain rationales for punishment centered on retribution and just deserts. It may shift focus toward prevention, deterrence, and rehabilitation based on the deterministic causes of criminal behavior.
  3. Public Policy and Social Engineering: Accepting a deterministic model of human behavior could inform more effective policy interventions and “choice architecture” that shapes outcomes by altering the deterministic factors and incentive structures.
  4. Artificial Intelligence and Decision Theory: AI systems are deterministic in nature. Embracing determinism is essential for developing coherent frameworks for artificial decision-making and intelligence aligned with human values and goals.
  5. Philosophy of Science: Determinism is a core premise of many interpretations of quantum mechanics and physical laws. Maintaining this view is important for exploring its implications across science.
  6. Certain Approaches to Ethics: Some ethical frameworks like consequentialism and virtue ethics may be more naturally aligned with determinism than theories hinging on libertarian free will.

In essence, anytime human behavior, decision-making processes, or the nature of agency and causation are deeply relevant, a deterministic stance can offer an important theoretical grounding and set of assumptions to work from, as opposed to libertarian free will. It constrains the conceptual options in potentially productive ways across many domains.
