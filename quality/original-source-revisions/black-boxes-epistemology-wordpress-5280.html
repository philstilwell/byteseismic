Imagine a new ideology proposes an entity that conveys testable truths about the universe. The entity constantly outputs predictions that turn out to be true. However, the inner workings of that entity are inscrutable. Are we rational in placing as much confidence in the future predictions of that entity as its track record of predictive successes warrants. Or does ignorance of its inner workings prevent this?

Your question touches on a profound issue at the intersection of epistemology (the study of knowledge) and philosophy of science, particularly around the themes of reliability, understanding, and trust in epistemic authorities or mechanisms.

On one hand, the reliability of the entity’s predictions, as evidenced by a track record of success, seems to provide strong grounds for confidence in its future predictions. This is in line with the principle of induction: if something has consistently happened under certain conditions in the past, we might expect it to happen again under similar conditions in the future. In many scientific and practical contexts, predictive success is a key criterion for accepting and relying on a model or theory, even when its inner workings are not fully understood. For example, quantum mechanics is famously counterintuitive and its foundations are still debated, yet its predictive successes make it one of the most reliable theories in physics.

On the other hand, understanding the mechanism behind predictions is often considered crucial for trust and reliability. This is because understanding can provide insight into the limits of applicability of a predictive model, situations where it might fail, and potential reasons for its failures. Without understanding the inner workings of the entity, one might argue that we are unable to judge the reliability of its predictions across different contexts, especially in situations that diverge significantly from those of its past successes. This concern echoes the problem of underdetermination in the philosophy of science: for any set of empirical data, there might be multiple underlying theories or explanations that could account for that data, and without understanding, we cannot choose between them on empirical grounds alone.

The balance between these perspectives might depend on several factors:

  1. Nature of Predictions: Are the predictions about well-bounded and well-understood phenomena, or do they extend into areas where our current theories and models are inadequate?
  2. Consequences of Errors: What are the potential consequences of acting on a false prediction? In high-stakes situations, we might demand a higher standard of understanding.
  3. Alternative Sources of Knowledge: Are there alternative, perhaps more understandable, sources that make similar predictions? If so, how do their track records compare?
  4. Opportunity for Validation and Falsification: Is there an opportunity to test and potentially falsify the entity’s predictions in a controlled manner, thereby gaining more insight into its reliability?

In summary, whether it is rational to place confidence in the future predictions of the entity, as much as its past success warrants, can depend on a nuanced assessment of these factors. While predictive success is a powerful indicator of reliability, a deeper understanding of the underlying mechanisms can significantly enhance trust and confidence, particularly in changing or unprecedented circumstances.

This shift can be more formally understood through the lens of Bayesian probability, which provides a mathematical framework for updating beliefs in light of new evidence.

Bayesian Updating in Brief

Bayesian updating allows us to adjust our beliefs (probabilities) about hypotheses based on new evidence. The formula for Bayesian updating is:

where:

  • (P(H|E)) is the posterior probability of the hypothesis (H) given the evidence (E).
  • (P(E|H)) is the likelihood of observing the evidence (E) if the hypothesis (H) is true.
  • (P(H)) is the prior probability of the hypothesis (H) before observing the evidence.
  • (P(E)) is the probability of observing the evidence under all possible hypotheses.

Applying Bayesian Reasoning to the Black Box Scenario

  1. Prior Belief ((P(H))): Initially, you might have a low prior belief in the hypothesis that the black box can predict die rolls with 90% accuracy, given the theoretical expectation that each roll of a fair die is random with a 1/6 chance for any specific outcome.
  2. Evidence ((E)): The evidence is the observed track record of the black box’s predictions—first, over 5 years, and then, more compellingly, over 1,000 years.
  3. Likelihood ((P(E|H))): The likelihood of observing this track record if the black box truly has a predictive accuracy of 90% is high. Conversely, the likelihood of observing this track record if the black box does not have this ability (i.e., if die rolls are purely random) is extremely low.
  4. Posterior Belief ((P(H|E))): Given the strong evidence (the black box’s track record), Bayesian updating would significantly increase the posterior probability of the hypothesis that the black box can reliably predict die rolls.

Impact of a 1,000-Year Track Record

The extension from a 5-year to a 1,000-year track record drastically reduces the probability that the black box’s success is due to chance. This is because the likelihood of consistently observing such predictive accuracy over an extended period, if the underlying process were truly random, diminishes exponentially with time.

Calculus Offered by Bayesian Probability

Bayesian probability calculus allows us to quantify the shift in belief about the black box’s predictive ability. It formalizes the intuition that as we accumulate more evidence (in this case, the extended track record of accuracy), our confidence in the hypothesis that the black box has predictive power should increase. This increased confidence is not merely a factor of time but of the compounded improbability of the evidence occurring under the null hypothesis of random chance.

In practical terms, while exact numbers would require specific priors and a detailed model of the evidence’s likelihood, the principle is clear: the epistemic weight of a 1,000-year track record of 90% accuracy in a context presumed to be governed by randomness is such that it would compel a rational observer to significantly revise their beliefs about the predictability of die rolls, notwithstanding any initial skepticism based on classical probability theory. This shift underscores the dynamic interplay between empirical evidence and theoretical frameworks in shaping our understanding of phenomena, particularly in scenarios where empirical outcomes challenge theoretical expectations.

1. Initial Belief (Prior Probability):

  • We start with a prior probability, which is our initial belief about the chance of rolling a 6. In this case, for a fair die, it’s 1/6 (approximately 16.7%).

2. New Evidence (Likelihood):

  • The black box’s successful predictions over time constitute the new evidence (likelihood). Each successful prediction updates our belief in the black box’s accuracy.

3. Posterior Probability:

  • Using Bayes’ theorem, we combine the prior probability with the likelihood to get the posterior probability, which is our updated belief about the chance of rolling a 6 considering the black box’s predictions.

The Strength of Evidence (Inductive Density):

  • 5 Years (Moderate Inductive Density): The black box’s 90% accuracy over 5 years provides significant evidence that deviates from the expected 1/6 probability. This evidence is strong enough to shift the posterior probability towards the black box’s predictions, making it more rational to trust the black box over pure chance.
  • 1,000 Years (High Inductive Density): The incredibly long time frame with consistent success (90% accuracy) makes the black box’s performance even more remarkable. The likelihood of this happening by chance becomes minuscule. Consequently, the posterior probability will heavily weigh towards the black box’s predictions. In this case, trusting the black box becomes overwhelmingly rational from a probabilistic perspective.
