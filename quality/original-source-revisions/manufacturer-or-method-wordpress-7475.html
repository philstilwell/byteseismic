Underestimation of Theoretical Underpinnings: By dismissing the exploration of the origins of cognition (whether evolutionary or divine), the argument potentially overlooks the importance of understanding these underlying mechanisms in enhancing our understanding of cognition. Theoretical insights can provide critical perspectives that inform and refine practical testing methods and interpretations of what constitutes “reliability” in human cognition.

Conclusion:

The argument provides a compelling, if somewhat simplified, view that practical testing is crucial for understanding the reliability of cognition, akin to testing a compass. However, it underestimates the complexity of human cognition and the value of theoretical insights into its origins and mechanisms. A more robust assessment would integrate both practical testing and theoretical exploration to fully appreciate and understand the nuances of cognitive reliability.

Here is a potential rebuttal to the C.S. Lewis quote based on the points raised in this discussion:

While Lewis raises a valid concern about the implications of human cognition being merely a byproduct of unguided physical processes, his conclusion that this would inherently undermine the trustworthiness of our reasoning is problematic for a few key reasons:

First, Lewis frames the issue as an all-or-nothing dilemma – either our minds are designed by God as a source of truth, or they are random accidents unworthy of trust. However, this is a false dichotomy. Even if our cognitive faculties arose through non-designed evolutionary processes, we can still potentially validate their functional reliability through empirical cross-modal testing without appealing to their origins.

The cross-modal approach demonstrates that by evaluating performance on tasks integrating multiple cognitive systems (memory, language, visuospatial skills, etc.), we can implicitly assess the proper functioning of the underlying neural components. Succeeding on such tests provides evidence for the sound operation of our cognitive machinery in the present, regardless of how it arose originally.

Secondly, Lewis’s argument appears to set up an inherent paradox where the very reasoning we use to conclude God doesn’t exist is itself undermined by that conclusion. But this is circular – we can bracket conclusions about God’s existence and still pragmatically evaluate the reliability of our cognitive processes through empirical self-testing, without presupposing any cosmic guarantor of rationality.

Furthermore, asserting that reliable reasoning requires an infallible, transcendent source of truth raises troubling philosophical questions about the standard of certainty expected. If human cognition can never achieve perfect reliability by Lewis’s criteria, then even the divinely-gifted reason he advocates for remains perpetually suspect and untrustworthy to some degree.

In contrast, the empirical approach acknowledges the inescapable human limitations and variability in cognitive performance, while still enabling us to gauge and strive to improve our rational capacities through ongoing self-assessment and cross-validation, irrespective of any particular stance on the origins or cosmic underwriting of our mental faculties.

While Lewis’s perspective highlights important philosophical considerations, a pragmatic program of rigorously testing and evaluating human reasoning through multi-modal performance benchmarks allows us to navigate this conundrum through empiricism and perpetual self-calibration, rather than insisting on uncheckable external validations of our cognitive integrity.
