Provide three scenarios in which the cost of belief would be high, epistemically justifying disbelief.

  1. Medical Diagnosis:
    When a medical professional is diagnosing a patient, the cost of incorrect belief can be extremely high. For instance, if a set of symptoms could be attributed to either a common illness or a rare but serious condition, acting on the belief that the patient has the serious condition without substantial evidence could lead to unnecessary, invasive procedures, causing harm to the patient. Therefore, it would be epistemically justified for the medical professional to maintain disbelief in the serious condition until all requisite tests are conducted and the evidence conclusively points to that diagnosis.
  2. Criminal Justice:
    In a criminal justice context, particularly when determining guilt, the standard is ‘beyond a reasonable doubt’ for good reason. The cost of a wrongful conviction based on an incorrect belief about a person’s guilt is very high — leading to loss of freedom for the individual, and a failure of justice. As such, a juror should maintain disbelief in the defendant’s guilt until the evidence presented removes all reasonable doubt.
  3. Scientific Research:
    In scientific research, particularly in fields dealing with public health, such as epidemiology or environmental science, the cost of belief based on insufficient evidence can have wide-ranging consequences. For example, prematurely believing in a causal link between a specific environmental factor and a health outcome without strong evidence could lead to costly policy changes, public panic, and possibly the diversion of resources from more pertinent issues. Scientists should therefore remain skeptical and maintain disbelief in the causal link until multiple high-quality studies converge on the same conclusion.

In your three secnarios, what would be the actual danger of mapping one’s belief to the degree of the evidence?

In the three scenarios I provided, mapping one’s belief too closely to the degree of evidence without considering the quality, reliability, or completeness of that evidence can lead to significant dangers:

  1. Medical Diagnosis:
    If a healthcare professional were to map their belief too closely to preliminary or non-conclusive evidence, they might initiate a treatment plan based on an incorrect diagnosis. This could lead to administering unnecessary medication, performing unwarranted surgeries, or overlooking the actual condition that requires treatment, thereby causing harm to the patient and potentially resulting in medical complications or death.
  2. Criminal Justice:
    For jurors and law enforcement officials, mapping belief too closely to evidence without a critical assessment could result in confirmation bias, where they interpret all subsequent evidence in light of their initial belief. This could lead to ignoring or undervaluing exculpatory evidence, resulting in a wrongful conviction or the real perpetrator remaining at large. The dangers here are miscarriages of justice, the destruction of an innocent person’s life, and a loss of faith in the judicial system.
  3. Scientific Research:
    In scientific research, closely aligning belief to the degree of evidence without rigorous scrutiny can lead to the dissemination of false or premature conclusions. The dangers include undermining public trust in scientific findings, misguiding public policy, wasting resources on ineffective or harmful interventions, and potentially causing public health crises if, for example, the research pertains to incorrect medical guidance or environmental hazards.

But a credence is not a decision. Wouldn’t more accurate credences better inform the decisions?

Indeed, a credence is not a decision; it’s a degree of belief or subjective probability one assigns to a proposition being true. More accurate credences, when properly informed by evidence, do contribute to better decision-making. The key is in the word “accurate”—credences should be based on a careful and critical evaluation of the available evidence.

The danger arises when the process of forming these credences is not sufficiently critical or discerning, particularly in situations where the evidence is complex, incomplete, or can be interpreted in various ways. If the evidence is not thoroughly vetted or if its reliability is overestimated, the resulting credences may be misguided, and thus the decisions based on these credences could be flawed.

For instance, in the scenarios I described:

  1. Medical Diagnosis:
    More accurate credences would indeed help a doctor make better decisions regarding treatment. However, if a doctor updates their credences with every new piece of evidence without considering the quality of that evidence, they might make a diagnosis or treatment decision based on misleading or incomplete information.
  2. Criminal Justice:
    Jurors should update their belief in the guilt or innocence of a defendant as new evidence is presented, but they also need to critically assess the quality and relevance of each piece of evidence. Overconfidence in weak evidence might lead to a wrongful conviction, while undue skepticism might result in a guilty person being acquitted.
  3. Scientific Research:
    For scientists, having accurate credences based on solid evidence is crucial for advancing knowledge. However, if researchers adjust their beliefs too readily based on preliminary or non-peer-reviewed evidence, they might pursue fruitless lines of inquiry, overlook better hypotheses, or publicly endorse findings that don’t hold up under scrutiny.

In summary, while more accurate credences informed by a critical evaluation of the evidence can indeed lead to better decisions, there’s a risk when those credences are formed on an uncritical acceptance of evidence. Therefore, it is essential to weigh the evidence, considering its reliability and the potential consequences of being wrong before updating one’s beliefs and making decisions based on those beliefs.

All things being equal, including the credibility of the evidence, is it not better to update your priors as new confirming and disconfirming evidence arrives?

Absolutely, under the assumption that all things are equal, including the credibility of the evidence, it is better to update your priors as new confirming and disconfirming evidence arrives. This principle is grounded in Bayesian reasoning, which suggests that individuals should adjust their beliefs proportionally to the weight of the new evidence, while also taking into account their prior beliefs.

By continuously updating one’s priors with new evidence, one maintains a belief system that is responsive and adaptive to new information, which is essential for rational decision-making. This process allows for a more nuanced and informed understanding of the situation at hand, and typically leads to better outcomes because decisions can be made based on the most current and comprehensive assessment of the evidence available.

This methodology promotes a dynamic approach to knowledge and decision-making, one that is self-correcting and always seeking alignment with the best representation of reality as evidenced by the latest, most credible data.

Here is a salient reformulation of the notion that rational belief is a degree of belief that maps to the degree of the relevant evidence:

Rational belief calibrates conviction proportionally to justification.

In other words, the strength of one’s belief in a proposition should align with the weight of the evidence supporting or contradicting that proposition. Belief that outstrips the evidence is irrational – whether that takes the form of excessive skepticism in the face of ample proof or unfounded certainty despite scant or ambiguous evidence.

Rationality demands that as new evidence arises, one updates their degree of confidence, tempering belief if exculpatory data emerges or reinforcing belief if augmented by additional confirmation. The ideal is a credence that maps precisely to the cumulative evidential balance, avoiding arbitrary fixity.

Ultimately, rational belief reflects reasoned judgment seamlessly tuned to the substantiating grounds, rather than manifesting rigid conviction disconnected from reality’s actual epistemic signals. It is belief calibrated as a finely measured mirror of the philosophical motto – proportion belief to the evidence.
