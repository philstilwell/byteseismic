Rational belief is a degree of belief that maps to the degree of the relevant evidence. What makes humans inclined to quickly reach for dogmatic conclusions that do not reflect the degree of the evidence?

Humans are complex beings influenced by a multitude of factors, including cognitive biases, social influences, emotional responses, and evolutionary tendencies. These factors can sometimes lead individuals to reach dogmatic conclusions that don’t align with the degree of evidence available. Here are a few reasons why this happens:

  1. Cognitive Biases: Our brains often take mental shortcuts to process information quickly. These shortcuts, known as cognitive biases, can lead us to make judgments and decisions based on irrational factors rather than evidence. For example, confirmation bias causes people to seek out information that confirms their existing beliefs while ignoring contradictory evidence.
  2. Emotional Influences: Emotions can cloud judgment and lead to dogmatic thinking. Fear, for example, can make people cling to familiar beliefs, even in the face of contradictory evidence. Similarly, tribalism and the desire for social belonging can lead individuals to adopt the beliefs of their group without critically evaluating the evidence.
  3. Cultural and Social Conditioning: People are often raised in environments where certain beliefs are deeply ingrained. This cultural and social conditioning can make it difficult for individuals to question or reconsider these beliefs, even when presented with contradictory evidence.
  4. Cognitive Dissonance: When individuals encounter evidence that challenges their beliefs, it can create cognitive dissonance—a state of mental discomfort. To alleviate this discomfort, people may reject the new evidence or double down on their existing beliefs, even if they are irrational.
  5. Complexity and Uncertainty: Some issues are inherently complex, and the available evidence may be uncertain or ambiguous. In such cases, people may be more likely to rely on intuition, heuristics, or preconceived notions rather than engaging in thorough, rational analysis.
  6. Desire for Certainty: Humans have a natural desire for certainty and security. Dogmatic beliefs can provide a sense of stability and certainty in an uncertain world, even if they are not supported by evidence.
  7. Lack of Critical Thinking Skills: Finally, many people lack the critical thinking skills necessary to evaluate evidence objectively and critically. Without these skills, individuals may be more susceptible to dogmatic thinking.

Overall, dogmatic thinking is a complex phenomenon influenced by a variety of psychological, social, and cognitive factors. Overcoming it requires self-awareness, critical thinking skills, openness to new evidence, and a willingness to challenge one’s own beliefs.

Rational belief is a degree of belief that maps to the degree of the relevant evidence. What make humans inclined to quickly reach for dogmatic conclusions that do not reflect the degree of the evidence?

Humans tend to form dogmatic conclusions that do not reflect the degree of evidence due to several cognitive biases and heuristics that have evolved as mental shortcuts for efficient decision-making. Some of the key factors that contribute to this tendency include:

  1. Confirmation bias: People tend to seek out, interpret, and remember information in a way that confirms their existing beliefs or hypotheses, while ignoring or discounting contradictory evidence.
  2. Motivated reasoning: People often have emotional or psychological motivations that lead them to arrive at particular conclusions, regardless of the evidence. These motivations can include desires for certainty, identity protection, or maintaining a positive self-image.
  3. Availability heuristic: People tend to judge the likelihood of events based on how easily examples come to mind, which can lead to overestimating the probability of vivid or recent events, and underestimating the probability of less salient occurrences.
  4. Anchoring bias: People can become overly attached to initial impressions or pieces of information, even when subsequent evidence contradicts or outweighs those initial anchors.
  5. Overconfidence bias: People generally have an inflated sense of confidence in their own judgments, knowledge, and abilities, leading them to underestimate the uncertainty or complexity of a situation.
  6. Tribalism and in-group bias: People have a strong tendency to favor and identify with their own social groups, which can lead to dogmatism and dismissal of evidence that challenges the group’s beliefs or interests.
  7. Cognitive laziness: Evaluating evidence objectively and revising beliefs can be mentally taxing, so people often resort to heuristics or dogmatic thinking as a cognitive shortcut.

While these biases and heuristics can be adaptive in certain situations, they can also lead to dogmatic thinking that is disconnected from the available evidence. Overcoming these tendencies requires active effort to cultivate critical thinking, intellectual humility, and a willingness to update beliefs in light of new evidence.

Ensuring that our degree of confidence in a proposition aligns with the available evidence is essential for rational thinking and decision-making. Here are some best practices to achieve this:

  2. Seek Diverse Sources of Information: Avoid relying solely on sources that confirm your existing beliefs. Actively seek out diverse perspectives and sources of information, including those that challenge your assumptions. This can help you gain a more comprehensive understanding of the evidence for and against a proposition.
  3. Evaluate the Quality of Evidence: Assess the quality and reliability of the evidence supporting a proposition. Consider factors such as the source’s credibility, the methodology used to gather the evidence, and whether the evidence has been peer-reviewed or subject to rigorous scrutiny.
  4. Consider Alternative Explanations: When evaluating evidence, consider alternative explanations or hypotheses that could account for the observed data. Assess the strength of each explanation based on its explanatory power, coherence, and consistency with other evidence.
  5. Update Your Beliefs in Response to New Evidence: Be open to revising your beliefs in light of new evidence. Avoid clinging to dogmatic positions and be willing to adjust your degree of confidence in a proposition as new information becomes available.
  6. Quantify Uncertainty: Acknowledge and quantify the uncertainty surrounding a proposition. Recognize that few propositions can be known with absolute certainty and assign degrees of confidence or probabilities based on the available evidence.
  7. Engage in Critical Thinking: Develop and apply critical thinking skills to evaluate arguments, assess evidence, and identify logical fallacies. Learn to distinguish between sound reasoning and flawed arguments, and be willing to question your own assumptions and beliefs.
  8. Seek Feedback and Discussion: Engage in discussions with others who hold different perspectives or expertise. Solicit feedback on your reasoning and conclusions, and be open to constructive criticism that challenges your beliefs.

Evaluate Evidence Critically:

  • Understand limitations of studies: Research often has limitations like sample size, methodology, or potential biases. Be aware of these limitations when interpreting results.
  • Look for replicability: If a claim is based on a single study, see if other studies support the findings. Replicability strengthens the evidence.
  • Consider alternative explanations: Are there other factors that could explain the observed phenomenon? Don’t jump to conclusions based on limited evidence.

  • Don’t be afraid to say “I don’t know”: It’s perfectly okay to admit when the evidence isn’t clear enough to form a strong opinion.

Here are some best practices to help ensure our degree of confidence in a proposition does not exceed the degree of evidence for that proposition:

  1. Actively seek disconfirming evidence: Instead of just looking for evidence that confirms our existing beliefs, we should actively seek out contradictory information and counterarguments. This can help counter confirmation bias.
  2. Consider alternative explanations: Rather than fixating on one explanation, we should intentionally consider multiple possible explanations for the evidence and weigh their relative merits. This can mitigate anchoring bias.
  3. Be aware of motivated reasoning: We should reflect on our own potential biases, emotional attachments, or vested interests that could be influencing our interpretation of the evidence. Maintaining intellectual humility is key.
  4. Quantify uncertainties: Instead of making binary true/false judgments, we can express our confidence levels as probabilities or ranges to better capture the uncertainty inherent in many propositions.
  5. Integrate new evidence objectively: As new evidence emerges, we should update our confidence levels accordingly, even if it means changing our position. Holding beliefs too rigidly can lead to dogmatism.
  6. Seek informed opinions: Consulting subject matter experts and authoritative sources can provide additional evidence and perspective beyond our own limited knowledge.
  7. Be wary of vivid anecdotes: While compelling stories and examples can shape our intuitions, we should balance them against robust statistical evidence to avoid falling prey to the availability heuristic.
  8. Embrace tentativeness: We should cultivate a willingness to change our minds and hold beliefs tentatively, recognizing that new evidence may emerge to revise our understanding.

Implementing these practices can help us maintain calibrated confidence levels that accurately reflect the strength of the evidence, rather than succumbing to overconfidence or belief perseverance in the face of contradictory information.

  3. The prevailing belief was that any attack would occur in Southeast Asia, not at Pearl Harbor. Despite warnings from intelligence agencies and concerns about the difficulties of occupying Afghanistan, Soviet leaders underestimated the resolve of the Afghan resistance and the long-term costs of the conflict, leading to a costly and protracted war.

Despite evidence of Cuban military preparedness and lack of popular support for the invasion among the Cuban people, U.S. decision-makers were overly confident in the plan’s success. This overconfidence, combined with inadequate planning and intelligence failures, led to a humiliating defeat for the United States and strengthened Castro’s regime.
