Introduction

Christian apologists often assert that non-Christian worldviews cannot “account for” the existence of logic or the laws of nature, implying that without the Christian God, these foundational aspects of reality lack a sufficient explanation. To critically evaluate this claim, we need to:

  1. Define what it means to “give an account for x” in a meaningful way.
  2. Analyze the apologists’ argument using formal logic.
  3. Provide counter-arguments that refute the necessity of invoking the Christian God to account for logic and the laws of nature.

1. Defining “Giving an Account for x”

In philosophy, to “give an account for x” typically means to provide a coherent explanation or justification for the existence or nature of x, grounded in a set of premises or a worldview. A meaningful definition would involve:

  • Coherence: The explanation should be logically consistent.
  • Sufficiency: The explanation should adequately address the question without significant gaps.
  • Non-circularity: The explanation should not assume what it attempts to prove.

2. Formalizing the Apologists’ Argument

Let’s represent the Christian apologists’ argument in logical form:

Premise 1 (P1): If logical laws and natural laws exist, then there must be an absolute, unchanging foundation (God) that accounts for them.

Premise 2 (P2): Logical laws and natural laws exist.

Conclusion (C1): Therefore, there must be an absolute, unchanging foundation (God) that accounts for them.

  3. (Therefore, God exists.)

3. Critical Examination and Refutation

Refuting Premise 1 (P1):

The key to refuting the apologists’ argument lies in challenging P1, which asserts a necessary connection between the existence of logical/natural laws and the existence of God as their foundation.

Counter-Argument to P1:

Premise 1A (P1A): If logical laws are necessary truths, they do not require an external foundation to account for them.

Premise 1B (P1B): Logical laws are necessary truths.

Conclusion 1A (C1A): Therefore, logical laws do not require an external foundation to account for them.

Symbolically:


  1. (For all x, if x is a necessary truth, then there does not exist y such that y accounts for x.)

  2. (For all x, if x is a law of logic, then x is a necessary truth.)

  3. (Therefore, for all x, if x is a law of logic, then there does not exist y such that y accounts for x.)

Explanation:

  • Necessary truths are propositions that could not be false in any possible world (e.g., “A is A”).
  • Logical laws are by nature necessary truths; their validity doesn’t depend on external factors.
  • Therefore, they do not require an external foundation (such as God) to “account for” them.

Refuting the Need for an External Foundation for Natural Laws:

Premise 2A (P2A): The uniformity of nature is an observed regularity, not necessarily requiring an absolute foundation.

Premise 2B (P2B): Scientific explanations account for natural laws through descriptive models and theories based on empirical observation.

Conclusion 2A (C2A): Therefore, natural laws can be accounted for without invoking an absolute, unchanging foundation.

  • Conclusion 3A (C3A): Therefore, logical laws can be accounted for through human rationality and the inherent structure of reality.

  • Premise 4B (P4B): Their existence is a brute fact about the nature of reality.

  • Premise 5B (P5B): Logic is a formalization of these evolved cognitive processes.
  • Conclusion 5A (C5A): Therefore, logic can be accounted for by naturalistic processes without invoking the divine.

Meaninglessness of the Demand in Certain Contexts:

  • Premise 6A (P6A): Demanding an “account” for necessary truths or brute facts leads to an infinite regress of explanations.
  • Premise 6B (P6B): At some foundational level, explanations must stop at self-evident or axiomatic truths.

Analysis of Each Candidate Definition

1. Provide a Causal Explanation for X

  • Definition: To identify the cause or origin of X; explain how X came into existence through a series of events or processes.
  • Coherence and Semantic Substance:
    • Coherence: Partially coherent when X is a contingent entity.
    • Semantic Substance: Lacks substance when applied to necessary, abstract entities like logic.
  • Detailed Explanation:
    • Logic and the laws of nature are often considered abstract and necessary truths, not contingent entities that arise from causes.
    • Causality typically applies to temporal, physical phenomena, not to atemporal, abstract principles.
    • Attempting to provide a causal explanation for logic may lead to category errors, conflating metaphysical categories.
  • Conclusion: This definition is not coherent and lacks semantic substance in the context of logic and the laws of nature.

2. Justify the Existence or Nature of X within a Worldview

  • Definition: To show that X is compatible with and adequately explained by the principles of a particular worldview.

    • Semantic Substance: Highly substantial.
  • Detailed Explanation:
    • This involves demonstrating that a worldview has the resources to explain why logic and the laws of nature exist and function as they do.
    • It assesses the explanatory power and internal consistency of the worldview concerning these foundational concepts.
    • For example, naturalism might justify logic as arising from consistent patterns in nature, while Christianity might attribute it to the nature of God.
  • Conclusion: This definition is coherent and semantically substantial.

  • Definition: To establish that X logically follows from fundamental principles or axioms within a given framework.
  • Coherence and Semantic Substance:
    • Coherence: Coherent, though potentially challenging.
    • Semantic Substance: Substantial.
  • Detailed Explanation:
    • Providing a foundation for logic involves meta-logical analysis, which can be complex due to potential circularity.
    • For the laws of nature, this might involve grounding them in more fundamental physical laws or principles.
    • It requires careful navigation to avoid logical fallacies, particularly when logic is both the tool and the subject of analysis.
  • Conclusion: This definition is coherent and semantically substantial, but requires rigorous handling to avoid circular reasoning.

Detailed and Rigorous Explanations

Definition 1 assumes causality applies to abstract, necessary entities, which is a category error. Logic and natural laws are not contingent beings that arise from prior causes; they are foundational aspects of reality (in most philosophical views). Therefore, seeking a causal explanation for them is incoherent.

Definition 2 is central to the debate. It challenges worldviews to demonstrate that they have the philosophical resources to explain or justify the existence of logic and natural laws. This is a meaningful endeavor that can be rigorously analyzed.

Definition 3 requires careful handling to avoid circular reasoning. When providing a logical foundation for logic itself, one must engage in meta-logic or foundational logical theories. Despite the complexity, this is a coherent and meaningful pursuit.

Definition 4 addresses the very nature of existence for logic and natural laws. It involves deep metaphysical inquiry into whether these entities are abstract objects, emergent properties, or grounded in a divine mind. This is a coherent and substantial line of inquiry.

Definition 5 is less applicable because it presupposes that logic and natural laws have purposes, which is not a universally accepted notion. Unless one adopts a teleological worldview, this definition lacks coherence in this context.

Definition 6 focuses on how we come to know and justify our belief in logic and natural laws. This is an essential aspect of epistemology and directly relates to whether a worldview can “account for” our knowledge of these concepts.

Definition 7 is problematic when applied to logic, as it is often considered irreducible. Attempting to reduce logic to more fundamental components risks circularity or infinite regress, thus lacking substantial value in this context.

Definition 8 involves demonstrating that logic (and possibly natural laws) must be the way they are due to necessity. This is a profound philosophical task that can provide a robust account of these concepts within a worldview.

Definition 9 is about clarifying terms and concepts to ensure meaningful dialogue. It is a foundational philosophical practice that adds significant value to the discussion.

Definition 10 emphasizes the importance of internal consistency within a worldview. If logic and natural laws fit coherently within a belief system, the worldview can be said to “account for” them in a meaningful way.

In the context of whether non-Christian worldviews can “account for” logic and the laws of nature, the most coherent and semantically substantial definitions of “account for” involve:
