1. Intrinsic Value

Intrinsic value posits that human lives are valuable in themselves, irrespective of external factors like potential or past actions. This concept is often grounded in human rights theory, which argues that all humans inherently possess rights and dignity simply by virtue of being human. A coherent argument for intrinsic value might also draw from Kantian ethics, which posits that humans, being rational agents capable of making moral choices, must always be treated as ends in themselves, not merely as means to other ends.

2. Future Potential

The notion that beings with greater future potential should be valued more highly can be seen in both utilitarian and consequentialist ethics. These frameworks might suggest prioritizing the saving of younger individuals or those likely to contribute significantly to society. However, this approach is contentious because it can lead to the devaluation of those who might not appear to have as much potential, such as the elderly or those with certain disabilities, thus clashing with principles of equality and fairness.

3. Future Burden

Diminishing the value of lives based on anticipated future burdens—whether due to health, legal status, or dependency—raises ethical concerns about discrimination and justice. Such views might risk violating the principle of equal respect by basing the worth of a person on their utility or cost to society rather than their inherent dignity. Ethical theories like care ethics, which emphasize interdependence and the moral significance of caring for the vulnerable, would argue against this perspective.

4. Past Achievements

Valuing individuals based on past achievements aligns with meritocratic principles, suggesting that contributions to society increase a person’s value. While this can incentivize positive contributions, it might also unjustly disadvantage those who have had fewer opportunities to achieve recognizable successes, thereby challenging the fairness of this criterion.

5. Past Harms

Reducing the value of lives based on past harms they have caused, such as criminal behavior, confronts legal and moral philosophy. Justice systems often grapple with this issue, balancing retributive justice (punishment for wrongs) with rehabilitative aims. From a moral standpoint, a purely retributive approach could conflict with the notion of intrinsic value and redemption, suggesting that even those who have caused harm retain certain rights and dignity.

Sentience is typically defined as the capacity to have subjective experiences, which includes the ability to feel pain, pleasure, emotions, and other conscious states. Sentience implies a level of awareness necessary to experience well-being or suffering.

Step 2: Establish Moral Significance of Sentience

The moral significance of sentience arises from the premise that the ability to experience suffering and well-being gives an individual interests—specifically, an interest in avoiding pain and experiencing pleasure or happiness. Ethical theories argue that beings with interests deserve moral consideration, which means their interests should be taken into account in moral deliberations.

Step 3: Moral Consideration Necessitates Rights

If a being’s interests are morally significant, there arises a moral duty for others (both individuals and societies) to respect these interests. This respect is effectively afforded through the recognition and protection of rights. Rights serve as moral guarantees that individuals’ interests are not arbitrarily disregarded. They create moral and often legal boundaries around how individuals can be treated.

Step 4: From Rights to Intrinsic Rights

Rights become intrinsic when they are recognized as inherent to the being itself, independent of external factors like social status, role, or utility. The notion that rights are intrinsic is supported by the argument that all sentient beings, by virtue of their capacity for subjective experiences, have a fundamental interest in being free from undue harm and in having the freedom to pursue their well-being.

Step 5: Generalization to Human Rights

The recognition of intrinsic rights in all sentient beings logically extends to humans, given that humans are sentient. Therefore, human rights—such as the right to life, liberty, and the pursuit of happiness—are viewed as intrinsic. They are not bestowed by any authority, but are derived from the inherent capacities and needs of humans as sentient beings.

Conclusion

The logical flow from sentience to intrinsic human rights involves recognizing that the capacity for subjective experiences engenders specific fundamental interests, which necessitate moral consideration, typically articulated through rights. These rights are termed intrinsic because they are not contingent upon any qualities other than the basic nature of the individual as a sentient being. This philosophical view underpins many modern human rights frameworks and is a cornerstone of ethical theories that prioritize the moral value of conscious experience.

David Hume’s Perspective

David Hume, as an empiricist and skeptic, was wary of philosophical concepts that could not be directly derived from empirical observation. He famously criticized the rationalist approach of deriving ought from is—meaning one cannot directly derive moral imperatives (what ought to be) from factual statements (what is).

  1. Skepticism of Abstract Concepts: Hume might argue that the concept of “intrinsic human rights” is not empirically observable and thus lacks a foundation in human experience, which is central to his philosophy.
  2. Moral Sentiments: He believed that morals are derived from feelings or sentiments rather than reason alone. Therefore, he might suggest that what we call “intrinsic human rights” are actually expressions of deeply felt communal sentiments about how we ought to treat one another.
  3. Convention and Utility: Hume might assert that rights are societal constructs developed out of utility and human conventions. According to this view, rights are not intrinsic but are agreements made within societies to promote order and well-being.

Yuval Noah Harari’s Perspective

Yuval Noah Harari, as a historian and author of “Sapiens: A Brief History of Humankind,” takes a broad view of human societies and their constructs through time. He often explores how human beliefs and structures are shaped more by shared myths than by immutable truths.

  1. Human Rights as a Modern Construct: Harari would likely point out that the concept of human rights is a relatively modern invention, reflecting a specific cultural narrative rather than a universal, intrinsic truth.
  2. Narrative Myths: Harari argues that much of what societies hold as sacred or inherent, including laws, money, and even human rights, are “inter-subjective realities” — constructs that exist because people collectively agree to believe in them.
  3. Functional Myths: He might also suggest that the myth of intrinsic human rights serves important functions, such as fostering cooperation and reducing violence in complex societies, rather than stemming from any objective human nature.

Synthesis

Both Hume and Harari provide frameworks that challenge the notion of intrinsic qualities by emphasizing the empirical and constructed bases of human concepts. Hume would dissect the logical and empirical basis of claiming any intrinsic property for rights, leaning on sentiment and utility rather than inherent moral truths. Harari would frame human rights within the context of human history as a powerful but contingent narrative that serves to uphold modern social orders.

Their insights suggest that while intrinsic human rights may serve as a useful and perhaps even necessary fiction, they are not grounded in the empirical or historical realities in the way proponents of these rights might claim. Instead, these rights are powerful societal tools that reflect collective human agreements and serve specific societal functions.

  1. In the scenario with the burning building, why was the newborn infant chosen for rescue first?
  2. Briefly explain the argument for the intrinsic value of human life. What is a challenge to this argument?
  3. Do we generally value lives with greater potential for future contributions more highly? Why or why not?
  4. Should the fact that someone might be a burden on society in the future diminish the value of their life in the present? Explain your answer.
  5. How does the past achievements of a person influence the value we place on their life?
  6. Should someone who has committed harmful acts in the past have less value placed on their life? Explain your reasoning.
  7. Besides the factors mentioned, are there any other reasons we might consider when assigning value to a human life? Briefly list two.
  8. Why is context important when considering the value of a life?
  9. Is there a universally agreed-upon way to assign value to human life? Briefly explain your answer.
  10. What is the logical foundation for the syllogistic argument for intrinsic human rights presented in this conversation?

Answers:

  1. Newborn infants are most vulnerable and have their whole life ahead of them.
  2. Intrinsic value suggests all life forms have inherent value simply for existing. A challenge is defining “life” and the relative value between different life forms.
  3. We often value young lives more due to potential contributions, but predicting potential is difficult and focusing solely on the future undervalues the present experience of life.
  4. No, valuing life based on potential burden is ethically problematic and discriminatory.
  5. Past achievements can make us admire someone, but it shouldn’t exclude those with potential to contribute or disregard unrecognized contributions.
  6. This is a complex issue. While some argue for less protection for those who’ve caused harm, rehabilitation and redemption are possible.
  7. Relationships and sentience are other potential factors.
  8. The factors influencing value will vary depending on the situation (e.g., triage vs. organ allocation).
  9. No, cultural and philosophical beliefs influence how we value life.
  10. The right of all sentient beings to avoid unnecessary suffering, grounded in the inherent negativity of suffering itself.
