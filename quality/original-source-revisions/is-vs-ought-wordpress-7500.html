Present a rigorous formulation of the is/ought problem.

The is/ought problem, also known as Hume’s guillotine, is a philosophical question that concerns the relationship between descriptive statements (what is) and prescriptive or normative statements (what ought to be). This problem is central to meta-ethics and was prominently articulated by the Scottish philosopher David Hume. Here is a rigorous formulation of the is/ought problem:

Background

In his 1739 work “A Treatise of Human Nature,” David Hume discusses how people often make claims about what ought to be done based on statements about what is the case. He observes that there seems to be a logical leap from descriptive statements (is) to normative statements (ought).

Hume’s Observation

Hume points out that many writers make claims about what ought to be on the basis of what is, but he notes a significant gap in their reasoning. According to Hume, the premises in their arguments typically consist entirely of ‘is’ statements, but their conclusions suddenly switch to ‘ought’ statements without justification.

Quote from Hume

In “A Treatise of Human Nature,” Hume writes:

“In every system of morality, which I have hitherto met with, I have always remarked, that the author proceeds for some time in the ordinary ways of reasoning, and establishes the being of a God, or makes observations concerning human affairs; when all of a sudden I am surprised to find, that instead of the usual copulations of propositions, is, and is not, I meet with no proposition that is not connected with an ought, or an ought not. This change is imperceptible; but is however, of the last consequence. For as this ought, or ought not, expresses some new relation or affirmation, ’tis necessary that it should be observed and explained; and at the same time that a reason should be given; for what seems altogether inconceivable, how this new relation can be a deduction from others, which are entirely different from it.”

Philosophical Implications

  1. Logical Gap: The core of the is/ought problem is the logical gap between descriptive and normative statements. No amount of factual information about the state of the world can, on its own, lead to a moral judgment without some form of normative premise.
  2. Need for Normative Premises: To validly derive an ‘ought’ from an ‘is’, one must introduce at least one normative premise. Without such a premise, the conclusion remains unsupported by the descriptive premises alone.
  3. Meta-Ethical Consequences: This issue raises significant questions about the objectivity and grounding of moral statements. It challenges the view that moral truths can be derived solely from empirical facts.
  4. Ethical Theorizing: The is/ought problem implies that ethical theories must justify their normative claims independently of empirical observations, leading to debates about moral realism, emotivism, and constructivism among others.

Conclusion

Hume’s guillotine suggests that ethical reasoning requires a careful examination of the premises and their logical connectivity. It challenges philosophers, ethicists, and indeed anyone engaged in moral reasoning, to clearly distinguish between how things are and how they believe things should be, thereby underlining the need for robust normative justifications in ethical arguments.

The Is/Ought Problem: A Rigorous Formulation

The is/ought problem, championed by Scottish philosopher David Hume, highlights the difficulty of deriving normative statements (“oughts”) from purely factual statements (“is”). Here’s a rigorous breakdown:

1. Types of Statements:

  • Is-Statements (descriptive): These statements describe the world as it is. They are objective and verifiable through observation or logic. Examples:
    • The grass is green. (Observable fact)
    • All bachelors are unmarried men. (Logical definition)
  • Ought-Statements (prescriptive): These statements express moral values, preferences, or obligations. They are subjective and not directly verifiable. Examples:
    • You ought to help those in need. (Moral value)
    • We ought to eat healthy foods. (Preference)
    • Stealing is wrong. (Obligation)

2. The Gap:

The crux of the problem lies in the apparent disconnect between these two categories. Is-statements tell us how things are, while ought-statements tell us how things should be. The question becomes:

  • Can we logically bridge this gap and derive “oughts” solely from “is”?

3. Hume’s Fork:

Hume further complicates the issue with his “fork.” It proposes that all knowledge falls into two categories:

  • Relations of Ideas: These are truths based on logic and definitions (e.g., all bachelors are unmarried). They hold true regardless of the world’s state.
  • Matters of Fact: These are truths based on observation and experience (e.g., the grass is green). They depend on the world being a certain way.

4. The Challenge:

Hume argues that “ought” statements don’t fit neatly into either category. They aren’t logical truths like relations of ideas, nor are they directly observable facts like matters of fact.

  • If “oughts” aren’t derived logically or empirically, how can we justify them?

5. The Problem:

The is/ought problem raises concerns about the validity of moral reasoning. If “oughts” can’t be logically derived from “is,” then:

  • Are moral judgments subjective and arbitrary?
  • Is there any objective basis for morality?

Conclusion:

The is/ought problem remains a central debate in ethics. While there’s no universally accepted solution, philosophers continue to explore how to bridge the gap between facts and values.

Provide rigorous formulation of the best arguments against the proposed irreconcilable is/ought divide.

The debate around Hume’s is/ought problem has generated several counterarguments that challenge the idea of an irreconcilable divide between descriptive and normative statements. Here are some of the best-formulated arguments against this proposed divide:

  1. Naturalistic Ethics:
    • Argument: Proponents of naturalistic ethics argue that moral properties are reducible to natural properties. For example, what is ‘good’ can be understood in terms of beneficial natural properties like promoting well-being or survival.
    • Philosophical Basis: This view often aligns with ethical naturalism, which posits that moral truths are derived from and consistent with empirical facts about the world.
    • Example: A naturalist might argue that “Pain is bad” can be derived from empirical observations about pain causing suffering and harm, which are naturally undesirable states.
  2. Internalism in Meta-Ethics:
    • Argument: According to ethical internalism, the recognition of moral facts intrinsically motivates one to act accordingly. If understanding a fact about the world necessarily entails a moral reaction, then the distinction between ‘is’ and ‘ought’ may not be as clear.
    • Philosophical Basis: Internalism links moral judgment with motivation, suggesting that to know what is right is to feel compelled to act on it.
    • Example: Discovering that someone is in pain inherently involves the motivation to alleviate their pain, thereby bridging the is/ought gap.
  3. Pragmatic Integration:
    • Argument: Some philosophers argue that the practices of explanation, justification, and understanding in human life inherently blend descriptive and normative elements, making the is/ought distinction less relevant in practical reasoning.
    • Philosophical Basis: This approach often draws on pragmatic theories of truth and meaning, suggesting that the usefulness of bridging ‘is’ and ‘ought’ in everyday decision-making undermines the strict separation.
    • Example: In deciding how to treat others, the factual understanding of their experiences and the normative commitments to fairness and empathy are inseparably linked.
  4. Constructivism in Ethics:
    • Argument: Constructivists hold that ethical truths are constructed from a set of rational procedures or practices rather than discovered in the empirical world. Here, ‘ought’ statements are derived from rational consistency with agreed-upon principles rather than empirical observation alone.
    • Philosophical Basis: Ethical constructivism focuses on the procedures of rational agreement and coherence among rational agents.
    • Example: If all rational beings agree that causing harm is wrong, this consensus can construct a normative truth that “One ought not harm others” from the factual understanding of what harm entails.
  5. Rule-Based Consequentialism:
    • Argument: This theory suggests that normative principles can be derived from the outcomes they produce, which are empirically observable. Thus, ‘ought’ statements are supported by ‘is’ statements about the consequences of following certain rules.
    • Philosophical Basis: Rule-based consequentialism links the moral worth of actions to the outcomes of following general rules rather than individual acts.
    • Example: If empirical evidence shows that societies flourish when they uphold the rule “Do not lie,” then one can argue that one ought to follow this rule based on the observed consequences.

Each of these arguments against the strict is/ought divide brings a unique perspective, suggesting that the transition from descriptive to normative is not only possible but sometimes inherent in understanding human values and rational decision-making.

Here’s a list of rigorous formulations against the irreconcilable is/ought divide:

1. Natural Law Arguments:

  • Moral Facts as Discoverable Facts: This perspective argues that moral truths exist objectively in the natural world, just like scientific facts. We can discover these truths through reason, similar to how we discover scientific laws. (Thinkers like Aquinas)
  • Values Embedded in Facts: This approach suggests that certain facts inherently carry value judgements. For example, the fact that humans are social creatures implies a value on cooperation. (Thinkers like Philippa Foot)

2. Error Theory:

  • Confusing “Is” and “Ought”: This theory argues that the is/ought problem arises from a misunderstanding. Moral statements (“oughts”) are actually disguised factual statements about human flourishing or well-being. We just haven’t fully understood the causal relationships between actions and their consequences. (Thinkers like G.E. Moore)

3. Emotivism:

  • Moral Statements Express Feelings: Emotivists suggest that “ought” statements don’t describe the world; they express emotions and attitudes towards certain actions. They function more like exclamations (“Boo to stealing!”) than factual claims. (Thinkers like A.J. Ayer)

4. Pragmatist Arguments:

  • “Oughts” Derived from Consequences: Pragmatists argue that moral worth arises from the practical consequences of actions. Actions are “good” if they lead to desirable outcomes (e.g., happiness, well-being) for individuals or society. (Thinkers like John Dewey)

5. Moral Psychology:

  • Emotions and Reasoning in Morality: This approach highlights the interplay between emotions and reason in moral judgements. Moral judgments involve both rational evaluation of facts and emotional responses to situations. (Thinkers like Jonathan Haidt)

6. Value-laden Descriptions:

  • Fact-laden Descriptions Can Imply Values: Some argue that seemingly neutral descriptions often carry implicit values. For example, describing someone as “wealthy” implies a positive value judgment compared to “poor.” These descriptive statements can then inform moral judgements. (Thinkers like Charles Stevenson)

These arguments challenge the idea of an absolute gap between facts and values. They explore different ways “oughts” might connect to “is,” even if the connection isn’t always straightforward.

1. Reliance on Unsubstantiated Axioms or Intersubjective Facts

Many of the arguments against the is/ought divide do indeed seem to rely on some form of normative assumption or axiom that itself may not be justifiable purely through empirical or descriptive means. This challenge can be seen as follows:

  • Naturalistic Ethics: While it aims to derive ethical statements from natural facts, critics argue that it presupposes a normative standard (e.g., that well-being or survival is good) which itself is not a natural fact but a normative claim needing justification.
  • Internalism in Meta-Ethics: This approach hinges on the idea that recognizing certain facts inherently involves moral inclinations. Critics argue that this still does not explain why the moral reaction is the correct one, nor does it address cases where different individuals have divergent moral intuitions about the same facts.
  • Constructivism in Ethics: While it sidesteps the need for empirical grounding by focusing on rational agreement, the normative force of these agreements relies on the acceptance of rationality as inherently normative. Critics question why rational agreement should carry moral weight in itself.

Each of these approaches attempts to bridge the gap by introducing a normative component that is somewhat external to the empirical facts themselves, thus not fully closing the is/ought divide without presupposing some moral commitments.

2. Reduction of Moral Statements to Expressions of Emotions

The criticism that some ethical theories reduce moral statements to mere expressions of emotion refers primarily to non-cognitivist approaches like emotivism, which are indeed distinct from the arguments listed but share a similar foundational challenge:

  • Emotivism and Similar Theories: These theories suggest that moral statements do not assert truths but express emotional states or commands. Here, the is/ought distinction is bypassed by denying that ‘ought’ statements are truth-apt in the first place.
  • Pragmatic Integration and Rule-Based Consequentialism: While not reducing moral statements to emotions per se, these approaches can be critiqued for treating ethical reasoning as a matter of practical coherence or social utility rather than as statements about objective moral truths.

Critics of these views argue that such reductions fail to capture the normative force and objectivity that many believe are essential to morality. They suggest that turning moral judgments into expressions of emotion or social constructs undermines their authority and universality.

Conclusion

These critiques underscore a fundamental tension in meta-ethics: the challenge of grounding normative claims in a way that is both objective and substantively informative. Each approach to bridging the is/ought gap attempts to address this by either redefining the nature of normative statements, linking them to some form of naturalistic or rational foundation, or questioning the distinction’s relevance. However, each solution brings its own philosophical challenges, highlighting the depth and complexity of the is/ought problem in moral philosophy.
