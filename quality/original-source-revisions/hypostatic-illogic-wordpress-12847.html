Ways a Believer in Christianity Might Address the Apparent Logical Contradiction

Believers in Christianity often encounter theological questions that seem logically contradictory. The statement that Jesus was 100% human and 100% God while humans can sin and God cannot sin presents one such challenge. Here are several ways they might explain or resolve this apparent contradiction:

1. Hypostatic Union

The doctrine of the Hypostatic Union asserts that Jesus Christ is one person with two distinct natures: divine and human. Believers argue that these natures coexist without mixing, changing, dividing, or separating. Therefore, Jesus’ divine nature, which cannot sin, remains intact and unaffected by His human nature’s susceptibility to sin.

2. Sinlessness of Jesus

Christian doctrine holds that while Jesus was fully human, He was without sin. This belief is supported by scriptures such as Hebrews 4:15, which states that Jesus was “tempted in every way, just as we are—yet he did not sin.” This explanation posits that Jesus’ humanity does not imply He sinned, aligning with the view that His divine nature was sinless.

3. Kenosis Theory

The Kenosis Theory suggests that Jesus voluntarily relinquished some of His divine attributes to fully experience human life. Based on Philippians 2:7, which says He “emptied himself, by taking the form of a servant,” this theory implies that Jesus limited His divine powers, including the ability to avoid sin, to relate fully to humanity’s struggles. However, His divine nature ensured He did not actually sin.

4. The Role of the Holy Spirit

Believers might argue that the Holy Spirit played a crucial role in maintaining Jesus’ sinlessness. According to Christian belief, the Holy Spirit guided and empowered Jesus, enabling Him to resist sin despite His human nature. This perspective emphasizes the cooperative work of the Trinity in Jesus’ life and ministry.

5. Mystery of Faith

Some Christians might embrace the mystery of faith, accepting that certain aspects of divine nature and the incarnation are beyond human comprehension. They might argue that finite human logic cannot fully grasp the infinite nature of God, and therefore, the coexistence of Jesus’ humanity and divinity, including His sinlessness, is a divine mystery to be accepted by faith.

6. Distinct Roles of Natures

Another explanation is the concept of distinct roles of natures. This view holds that Jesus’ divine nature and human nature operated in different domains. His divine nature remained omniscient, omnipotent, and sinless, while His human nature experienced hunger, thirst, and temptation. This separation of roles allowed Jesus to fulfill His mission without compromising His sinless divine nature.

In summary, these explanations demonstrate how believers in Christianity might reconcile the apparent logical contradiction surrounding Jesus’ dual nature and sinlessness. Each approach emphasizes different theological principles and scriptural interpretations to maintain the coherence of Christian doctrine.

  4. If Jesus was 100% human, he could sin. (From 1 and 3)
  5. If Jesus was 100% God, he could not sin. (From 2 and 3)
  6. Jesus could sin and could not sin. (From 4 and 5)
  7. This is a contradiction. (From 6)
  8. Therefore, our assumption (3) must be false.
  9. Jesus could not be both 100% human and 100% God. (Negation of 3)

It is logically impossible for Jesus to be both 100% human and 100% God.

  2. We assume that Jesus is both 100% human and 100% God.
  3. This assumption leads to a logical contradiction: Jesus would simultaneously have the ability to sin (from his human nature) and the inability to sin (from his divine nature).
  4. Since this results in a contradiction, we must conclude that the initial assumption is false.
  5. Therefore, the only logically consistent position is that Jesus could not be both 100% human and 100% God.

This revision highlights the logical incompatibility of the concept of Jesus being both fully human and fully divine, given the premises about the nature of humanity and divinity in relation to sin. Appendix: Rigorous Examination of the Hypostatic Union and Logical Consistency

This appendix addresses a rigorous examination of the hypostatic union doctrine—the claim that Jesus is simultaneously fully human and fully divine. 1. Core Contradiction: The Incompatibility of Human and Divine Attributes

The hypostatic union posits that Jesus possesses two complete, fully embodied natures—human and divine—unified in one person. However, inherent to these natures are mutually exclusive attributes: humans have the capacity to sin (), while Gods have the incapacity to sin (). This creates a logical tension: if Jesus is fully human, he must fully possess the capacity to sin (). If he is fully divine, he must fully embody the incapacity to sin (). 2. Analysis of “Switching” or “Suppressing” Natures

To address this contradiction, some theologians propose that Jesus “switches” between his human and divine natures, selectively “suppressing” one nature while embodying the other. This view suggests that Jesus, while on earth, can embody his human limitations (such as hunger, pain, and limited knowledge) by suppressing his divine attributes, then “switch” to his divine nature when necessary, thereby avoiding contradiction.

However, selective suppression or “switching” undermines the claim that Jesus fully possesses both natures simultaneously. It implies a form of selective embodiment rather than the unity of two fully embodied natures. To clarify, if Jesus can simply switch from human to divine attributes as needed, he does not fully embody both natures at all times; rather, he embodies aspects of each situationally, thereby compartmentalizing his identity. This selective embodiment implies a division, contradicting the hypostatic union, which asserts that Jesus is unified in one person who is fully human and fully divine at every moment.

3. Illustrative Analogies and Their Limitations

Several analogies are often used to explain the hypostatic union, yet they fail to resolve the underlying logical inconsistency:

  • Emergency Responder Analogy: Some suggest that Jesus, like an emergency responder who can speed while on duty but obeys speed limits off-duty, adopts human limitations when acting within his human nature and divine attributes when acting within his divine nature. This analogy, however, falls short because it only addresses situational permissions rather than fully embodied, inherent attributes. The emergency responder does not embody both lawful and unlawful behaviors simultaneously; he merely shifts behavior based on context, which does not reflect the hypostatic union’s claim of unified possession of incompatible attributes.
  • Avatar or Body Occupation Analogy: Another analogy likens Jesus’ two natures to a character occupying two forms, such as an avatar, with the human form dormant while in the divine state and vice versa. This analogy implies that Jesus’ two natures are distinct entities or modes, essentially alternating between roles. This perspective, however, risks fragmenting Jesus’ identity into two compartmentalized entities, contradicting the hypostatic union’s assertion that Jesus embodies both natures as a single, unified person.

4. Critical Analogy: The Fully Male and Fully Female Example

To illustrate the inherent logical tension, consider a hypothetical person who claims to be fully male and fully female. Certain attributes, such as the biological ability to bear children, are intrinsic to femaleness but incompatible with maleness. If this individual claimed to suppress or switch between these characteristics at will, it would not resolve the contradiction but merely indicate selective expression rather than unified identity. In other words, the individual would not fully embody both genders at all times.

Similarly, if Jesus is both fully human and fully divine, he must embody the inherent attributes of each nature simultaneously. Being human means having the capacity to sin, while being divine means having the incapacity to sin. Selective suppression or “switching” fails to unify these natures, as it implies compartmentalization rather than true union.
