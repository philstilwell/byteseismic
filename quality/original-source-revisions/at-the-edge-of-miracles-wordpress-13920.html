David Hume, in his essay “Of Miracles,” argues that it is always more rational to disbelieve in miracles because the probability of human error—such as misinterpretation, faulty memory, or intentional deceit—is always greater than the probability of a violation of the laws of nature. Hume’s skepticism is rooted in the principle that extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence, and that human testimony is often unreliable.

However, the possibility arises that a sufficiently large and well-verified collection of miracle accounts could alter this balance of probabilities. With advances in investigative methodologies and technologies that reduce reliance on subjective human testimony, the cumulative weight of evidence might justify revisiting the skepticism toward miracles. To rigorously explore this dynamic, we can employ principles from probability theory and epistemology.

Belief is not typically a binary switch from disbelief to belief but rather a continuum where confidence levels adjust incrementally with new evidence. Degrees of Belief and Bayesian Updating

In Bayesian epistemology, beliefs are represented as probabilities on a continuous scale between 0 and 1. As new evidence is obtained, we update our prior beliefs to form new posterior beliefs using Bayes’ Theorem. This process reflects a gradual adjustment rather than a sudden change from disbelief to belief.

Revisiting the Earlier Statement

Instead of suggesting that we should “accept the miracle” once a certain threshold is crossed, it’s more accurate to say that our confidence in the occurrence of the miracle increases progressively with each new piece of credible evidence. This approach aligns with the concept of a “rational needle move,” where our belief adjusts in proportion to the strength and amount of evidence.

The Continuum of Belief

  • Incremental Updates: Each new, reliable piece of evidence increases by a certain amount, depending on its strength and reliability.
  • No Binary Threshold: There’s no specific point at which we must “accept” the miracle; instead, our confidence grows progressively.
  • Decision Thresholds: While belief itself is continuous, practical decisions might require setting thresholds (e.g., acting upon a belief when confidence exceeds 0.9). However, this threshold is subjective and varies by individual or context.

Implications for Evaluating Miracles

  • Evidence Quality: High-quality, objective evidence has a more significant impact on increasing our degree of belief than anecdotal or subjective reports.
  • Cumulative Effect: The accumulation of multiple independent pieces of strong evidence can collectively raise our degree of belief to a high level, even if each piece alone wouldn’t suffice.

Conclusion

Belief in miracles, or any extraordinary claim, should be viewed as a matter of degrees rather than an all-or-nothing stance. The rational approach involves continuously updating our beliefs in light of new evidence, allowing our confidence to adjust incrementally. This perspective acknowledges the nuanced nature of belief and aligns with a more accurate interpretation of Bayesian reasoning in epistemology.

Was Hume’s depth of skepticism perhaps justified given the unavailability of the objective tools of scrutiny at his time?

David Hume’s skepticism regarding miracles, as articulated in his 1748 essay “Of Miracles,” was profoundly influenced by the epistemological and technological limitations of his time. Hume argued that miracles are violations of the laws of nature, and as such, the probability of their occurrence is exceedingly low. He maintained that human testimony, which is prone to error, deception, and bias, is insufficient to establish the authenticity of miraculous events.

Justification of Hume’s Skepticism in His Historical Context

  1. Limited Investigative Tools: In the 18th century, the scientific method was still in its developmental stages, and the instruments available for empirical investigation were rudimentary. There were no advanced technologies like spectroscopy, radiometric dating, or electron microscopy to analyze phenomena with precision. This lack of sophisticated tools made it difficult to objectively verify extraordinary claims.
  2. Reliance on Human Testimony: Without technological means to record or measure events, people had to rely heavily on eyewitness accounts and personal testimonies. Hume recognized that such accounts are susceptible to errors arising from misperception, faulty memory, exaggeration, or intentional deceit. The absence of objective recording devices like cameras or audio recorders meant that there was no way to cross-verify individual claims independently.
  3. Prevalence of Superstition and Lack of Scientific Literacy: The general population during Hume’s time had limited access to scientific education. Superstitions and unfounded beliefs were widespread, often filling gaps in understanding natural phenomena. This environment fostered a readiness to accept miraculous explanations without demanding rigorous evidence.
  4. Challenges in Communication and Verification: Information dissemination was slow, and cross-cultural or international verification of events was cumbersome. News of miracles could spread without being subjected to scrutiny by experts from different regions or disciplines, allowing unverified claims to gain credibility.

Contrast with Modern Tools of Scrutiny

Today, we have access to a plethora of advanced technologies and methodologies that enhance our ability to investigate and verify claims:

  • Scientific Instrumentation: Devices like high-powered telescopes, particle accelerators, and genomic sequencers allow us to explore and understand phenomena at cosmic and microscopic levels.
  • Digital Recording and Surveillance: Cameras, smartphones, and other recording devices can capture events in real-time, providing objective evidence that can be analyzed and authenticated.
  • Data Analysis and Computational Power: Advanced software and algorithms enable us to detect patterns, analyze large datasets, and simulate complex systems, reducing reliance on subjective interpretation.
  • Global Communication Networks: The internet and satellite communications allow for instant sharing of information, enabling peer review and collaborative verification across the world.

Implications for Hume’s Argument

Given the limitations of his era, Hume’s insistence on skepticism was a rational stance. He advocated for a high evidentiary standard, which was appropriate considering that most miracle claims could not be substantiated beyond anecdotal reports. His approach encouraged critical thinking and demanded that claims be proportionate to the evidence available.

In modern times, while we have better tools to investigate and potentially verify extraordinary events, Hume’s underlying principles remain relevant:

  • Extraordinary Claims Require Extraordinary Evidence: Even with advanced technology, the burden of proof lies heavily on demonstrating that an event genuinely violates natural laws.
  • Fallibility of Human Perception: Despite technological aids, human interpretation of data can still be influenced by cognitive biases and errors.
  • Necessity of Peer Review and Replication: Objective scrutiny by the scientific community is essential to validate any claim, miraculous or otherwise.

Conclusion

Hume’s depth of skepticism was justified within the context of his time due to the unavailability of objective and reliable tools for scrutiny. His arguments highlighted the need for critical examination and empirical evidence when assessing extraordinary claims. While modern technology provides us with better means to investigate such claims, the foundational principles of skepticism and rigorous inquiry that Hume championed continue to be essential in distinguishing between credible evidence and unfounded assertions. His work laid important groundwork for the development of scientific methodology and the emphasis on evidence-based reasoning that persists in contemporary discourse.
