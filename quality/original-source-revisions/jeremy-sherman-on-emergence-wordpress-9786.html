Key Terms and Definitions

  1. Autogen: A minimal model for a self-replicating system struggling for its own existence. It involves autocatalysis and the formation of protective shells.
  2. Autocatalysis: A process where a chemical reaction is catalyzed by one of its own products, leading to a self-sustaining reaction.
  3. Capsid Molecule: Molecules that form shells around genetic material, often seen in viruses.
  4. Constraint: A limitation or restriction that governs the behavior and interactions of a system.
  5. Dynamical Depth: A measure of complexity introduced by Terrence Deacon, focusing on the depth of interactions within a system.
  6. Emergent Constraint: A form of self-organization that arises spontaneously and governs the behavior of a system.
  7. Information First Model: A theory of the origin of life that posits information-bearing molecules like RNA as the initial step.
  8. Metabolism First Model: A theory that suggests life began with self-sustaining chemical reactions.
  9. Membrane First Model: A theory that life started with the formation of cell-like enclosures.
  10. Psychoproctology: A term coined by Jeremy Sherman to study how humans can behave destructively, individually or in groups.
  11. Selective Autogen: An advanced form of autogen that can selectively open or close in response to environmental conditions.
  12. Templated Autogen: An autogen that uses template molecules to store information and guide replication.

Premise 2: Autogen theory proposes a model where life emerges from autocatalysis and the formation of protective shells (capsids).

  2. Autogen theory addresses these limitations by proposing autocatalysis and protective shells as mechanisms.
  3. Therefore, autogen theory is a plausible alternative for explaining the emergence of life.

Argument 2: Autogen as a Self-Maintaining System

Premise 1: Autocatalysis involves chemical reactions where products catalyze their own formation.
Premise 2: In the presence of capsid molecules, these reactions can be encapsulated, forming dormant states that can regenerate in favorable conditions.
Conclusion: The autogen model, through autocatalysis and encapsulation, describes a self-maintaining system that can survive and replicate in varying environments.

Syllogism:

  1. If a system can catalyze its own formation and form protective states, it can maintain and regenerate itself.
  2. Autogen theory describes such a system through autocatalysis and encapsulation.
  3. Therefore, autogen theory explains a self-maintaining and regenerative system.

Argument 3: The Role of Emergent Constraints

Premise 1: Life must maintain regularity and order against the second law of thermodynamics, which drives systems towards disorder.
Premise 2: Emergent constraints, such as the formation of traffic congestion or whirlpools, demonstrate how systems can maintain order and function.
Conclusion: Emergent constraints are crucial in explaining how life maintains order and regularity, making them fundamental to understanding biological systems.

Syllogism:

  1. If emergent constraints can maintain order in physical systems, they can explain how life maintains regularity.
  2. Autogen theory relies on emergent constraints to describe the maintenance of order in living systems.
  3. Therefore, emergent constraints are fundamental in explaining how life maintains order and regularity.

Argument 4: The Importance of Selective Interaction

Premise 1: Life requires the selective intake of energy and resources to sustain itself.
Premise 2: The selective autogen can open or close in response to environmental conditions, allowing it to intake favorable substances and avoid harmful ones.
Conclusion: Selective interaction is a key mechanism for survival and self-maintenance in early life forms, as demonstrated by the selective autogen.

Syllogism:

  1. If a system needs to selectively intake resources to sustain itself, it must have mechanisms to differentiate between favorable and harmful substances.
  2. The selective autogen demonstrates such mechanisms by opening or closing based on environmental conditions.
  3. Therefore, selective interaction is crucial for the survival and self-maintenance of early life forms.

Augmented by Relevant Sources

  1. RNA World Hypothesis:
  • The RNA world hypothesis suggests that life began with self-replicating RNA molecules. While RNA can store genetic information and catalyze chemical reactions, it is considered unstable and unlikely to exist in the prebiotic conditions without proteins and membranes (Joyce, G. F. (2002). The antiquity of RNA-based evolution).
  1. Metabolism First Hypothesis:
  • The metabolism first hypothesis posits that life began with self-sustaining metabolic networks. However, these networks would require highly specific conditions and catalysts to form spontaneously (Morowitz, H. J. (1992). Beginnings of cellular life: Metabolism recapitulates biogenesis).
  1. Membrane First Hypothesis:
  • The membrane first hypothesis argues that life started with the formation of lipid membranes. These membranes would need to be selectively permeable to support metabolic processes, which is challenging to achieve without pre-existing proteins (Deamer, D. W. (1997). The first living systems: a bioenergetic perspective).

Assessment of Plausibility

Plausibility of the Arguments

  1. Autogen Theory as a Plausible Alternative:
    • Strengths: Autogen theory addresses some of the significant gaps in traditional theories by providing a more integrated approach. It combines the benefits of autocatalysis, encapsulation, and emergent constraints to explain the emergence of life. The concept of a self-sustaining system that can regenerate in favorable conditions is a compelling explanation for the origins of life.

  2. Self-Maintaining Systems:
    • Strengths: The idea that life can sustain itself through autocatalysis and encapsulation is supported by numerous natural examples, such as enzyme-catalyzed reactions and viral capsid formation. The dormant state concept effectively addresses the second law of thermodynamics by allowing systems to conserve energy and resources.
    • Support: This approach provides a robust mechanism for understanding how early life forms could maintain and propagate themselves in varying environmental conditions.
  3. Emergent Constraints:
    • Strengths: Emergent constraints offer a plausible mechanism for maintaining order and regularity in biological systems, countering the natural tendency towards disorder. This concept is well-supported by examples from both biological and physical systems, such as traffic congestion and whirlpools.
    • Support: The reliance on emergent constraints to maintain order in life forms aligns with observed phenomena in nature, making this argument highly plausible.
  4. Selective Interaction:
    • Strengths: The necessity for life to selectively intake favorable substances and avoid harmful ones is a well-established biological principle. The selective autogen model provides a realistic mechanism for how early life forms could achieve this through simple chemical interactions.
    • Support: This model is consistent with the behavior of current biological systems, where selective permeability and interaction are crucial for survival.

Potential Weaknesses

  1. Empirical Testing and Validation:
    • Weakness: One of the main criticisms of autogen theory is the current lack of empirical testing. While the theory is testable, it has not yet been validated through in vitro experiments or computational models.
    • Impact: Without empirical validation, the theory remains speculative, and its acceptance in the scientific community may be limited.
  2. Complexity of Chemical Systems:
    • Weakness: The transition from simple autocatalytic systems to more complex self-sustaining life forms involves numerous intermediate steps that are not fully understood. The theory may oversimplify these transitions, ignoring potential challenges in achieving each stage.
    • Impact: If the intermediate steps are more complex than the theory accounts for, the plausibility of autogen theory as a comprehensive explanation for the origin of life may be reduced.
  3. Environmental Variability:
    • Weakness: The theory assumes that early life forms could survive and regenerate in varying environmental conditions. However, the variability and harshness of early Earth environments might pose significant challenges for the survival and propagation of autogens.
    • Impact: If early Earth environments were more hostile than assumed, the likelihood of autogens forming and sustaining themselves decreases.
  4. Assumption of Self-Purging Mechanisms:
    • Weakness: The idea that autogens can self-purge to maintain functionality and prevent error catastrophe is speculative. This mechanism needs more detailed exploration and empirical support to be fully credible.
    • Impact: If self-purging is not as efficient as proposed, the stability and sustainability of autogens might be compromised.

Conclusion

Overall Plausibility: Autogen theory presents a compelling and plausible alternative to traditional theories of the origin of life, offering a cohesive framework that integrates chemical and physical principles. The arguments for self-maintaining systems, emergent constraints, and selective interaction are well-supported by observed natural phenomena, making the theory conceptually strong.

Potential Weaknesses: The main challenges for autogen theory lie in its empirical validation, the complexity of intermediate steps, environmental variability, and the assumption of self-purging mechanisms. Addressing these weaknesses through targeted experiments and detailed modeling will be crucial for strengthening the theory’s credibility and acceptance in the scientific community.
