    1. Discussion Prompts: Examining Explanations and Avoiding the Ontological Buffet

Once the rational demand for evidential substantiation has been removed or diminished, configuring and defending an internally logical ideology from whole cloth becomes much easier. Explain how this works using Carl Sagan’s The Dragon in my Garage.

Carl Sagan’s parable of “The Dragon in My Garage” from his book “The Demon-Haunted World: Science as a Candle in the Dark” illustrates the critical role of evidential substantiation in distinguishing between what is real and what is merely an unfounded belief. The scenario described helps explain how an internally logical ideology can be easily constructed and defended once the demand for rational evidence is removed or diminished.

The Process of Constructing an Internally Logical Ideology:

  1. Assertion of Existence:
  • The initial claim introduces the existence of something extraordinary—in this case, a fire-breathing dragon living in a garage. This claim sets the stage for building an ideology around the dragon.
  1. Removal of Empirical Checks:
  • When evidence (seeing the dragon, capturing its footprints, detecting its heat) is demanded, the claimant modifies the dragon’s properties (invisible, floats, emits heatless fire). This step crucially removes any empirical method of verification. By declaring that the dragon is undetectable by normal means, the claim shields itself against empirical scrutiny.
  1. Application of Special Explanations:
  • Every proposed physical test is countered with a special explanation why it won’t work (the dragon is incorporeal, the fire is heatless). These explanations are tailored specifically to negate any empirical testing methods. This further entrenches the belief by adapting it to overcome objections.
  1. Creating a Closed Ideological System:
  • The belief system becomes self-sustaining and internally consistent by continuously adjusting explanations to shield it from criticism. The lack of disproof (due to specially tailored explanations) is misconstrued as a form of weak validation.
  1. Invoking Wonder and Belief Without Evidence:
  • The narrative shifts focus from empirical validation to emotional and philosophical realms—areas less amenable to empirical testing. The ideology then appeals to wonder, mystery, or other emotional responses which do not require factual substantiation.
  1. Expanding the Ideology:
  • The claim might grow to suggest a broader phenomenon (invisible dragons in many garages) based on collective assertions from multiple individuals. This communal reinforcement can lend undue credence to the baseless claims.

Implications of Removing Rational Demand for Evidence:

  • Erosion of Empirical Foundations: Without the need for evidence, any claim can be adjusted ad hoc to resist falsification. This makes the ideology impervious to empirical challenge but also removes it from the realm of rational discourse.
  • Entrenchment of Beliefs: Beliefs can become deeply entrenched when they are not subject to empirical checks. People may begin to identify emotionally and culturally with these beliefs, making them part of personal or group identity.
  • Spread of Misinformation: In the absence of a requirement for proof, misinformation can spread unchecked. This can foster a culture where myths and superstitions overshadow scientific understanding.
  • Challenge in Debunking: Debunking such ideologies becomes increasingly difficult as they are designed to be unfalsifiable. The more flexible and tautological the explanations, the harder it is to effectively challenge them.

In summary, by removing the rational demand for evidence, constructing and defending an ideology becomes straightforward because the ideology is no longer anchored in reality but in the mutable and often esoteric explanations that support it. This detachment from empirical reality allows for a self-contained logical system that is immune to outside scrutiny but devoid of substantive truth.

Here’s how ideologies can expand with less need for evidence, using Carl Sagan’s “Dragon in My Garage” analogy:

The Freedom to Embellish: The Garage Grows

Carl Sagan’s “Dragon in My Garage” highlights how ideologies thrive when the pressure for evidence weakens. Here’s how this allows for the unconstrained addition of fantastical elements to a fabricated ideology:

  • The Expanding Explanations: Imagine the “invisible dragon” scenario. Every attempt to find the dragon is met with a new excuse – it floats, its fire is heatless, etc. This creates a pattern: the ideology can endlessly adapt to explain away any potential challenge.
  • Ontological Buffet: This lack of evidentiary constraints opens the door to an “ontological buffet.” Just like adding features to the invisible dragon (floating, heatless fire), new entities and qualities can be freely incorporated into the ideology.
  • Introducing the “Dragon Whisperer”: The believer might claim a special connection to the dragon, allowing them to explain its behavior in intricate detail. This “dragon whisperer” role is a new ontological entity – someone with unique abilities – that bolsters the ideology without needing verification.
  • The Expanding Lore: The ideology can develop a whole backstory for the dragons. Where do they come from? Why are they invisible? Entire narratives can be woven without needing evidence, enriching the ideology and making it more elaborate.
  • Immunity to Counterarguments: Because any counterargument can be explained away with new features or entities, the ideology becomes impervious to challenges. This creates a self-contained system, fostering a sense of internal consistency even in the absence of external validation.

The Peril of Embellishment:

While these embellishments might seem attractive, they create significant problems:

  • Loss of Grounding: The ideology becomes increasingly detached from reality. The more fantastical elements it incorporates, the less it reflects the actual world.
  • Echo Chamber Mentality: Believers become convinced of their ideology’s truth due to its internal consistency and elaborate explanations. This can lead to dismissal of any evidence that contradicts the embellished ideology.
  • Erosion of Trust: When ideologies prioritize self-preservation over factual accuracy, trust in the ideology itself erodes.

Sagan’s Message:

Sagan advocates for healthy skepticism. Even if something can’t be definitively disproven, a critical evaluation of its explanatory power and its reliance on unfalsifiable elements is essential. This helps us avoid ideologies that become elaborate castles built on sand.

What actual ideologies or mythologies employ this same ontological buffet modus operandi?

The ontological buffet modus operandi, where beliefs are adapted and shielded from empirical scrutiny through a self-sustaining framework of tailored explanations, is prevalent in various ideologies and mythologies across different cultures and historical contexts. This method can be particularly attractive in spiritual, religious, and conspiracy-theory circles, where empirical evidence is often either unavailable or actively dismissed in favor of more esoteric or subjective validations. Here are a few prominent examples:

1: Religious and Spiritual Claims:

    • Creationism and Intelligent Design: These ideologies often reject evolutionary biology, positing instead that life on Earth was created by a divine entity. Any scientific evidence supporting evolution can be dismissed by proponents as either misinterpretation or as tests of faith, thus circumventing empirical critique by framing the debate in untestable terms.
    • Spiritual and Paranormal Phenomena: Beliefs in ghosts, spirits, or other paranormal entities typically rely on subjective experiences and anecdotal evidence rather than empirical verification. Skeptical scrutiny is often countered with claims that the paranormal is beyond the physical realm and thus not subject to physical laws.

    2: Conspiracy Theories:

      • Moon Landing Hoax: Proponents of this theory claim that the moon landings were faked by the US government. Evidence provided by NASA and other scientific sources is dismissed as part of the conspiracy, creating a scenario where disproof is impossible because all contrary evidence can be claimed as fabricated.
      • Flat Earth Theory: Despite overwhelming scientific evidence to the contrary, flat Earth advocates believe that Earth is flat, and they dismiss all evidence of a spherical Earth as falsifications by a global conspiracy. This ideology adjusts explanations for various observable phenomena (e.g., gravity, horizon line) to fit their narrative.

      3: Historical Mythologies:

        • Ancient Myths and Legends: Many cultures have mythologies that include creatures and events (like dragons, the Phoenix, massive floods, etc.) that defy empirical validation. These stories often serve symbolic or moral purposes rather than factual recounting of events and are protected from scrutiny by their allegorical or supernatural framing.
        • Cultural Legends such as the Loch Ness Monster or Bigfoot: These creatures have become part of local lore in various regions. Alleged sightings and lack of concrete evidence keep these legends alive, with each claim of evidence dismissed by skeptics and explained away by believers in ways that prevent conclusive disproof.

        4: Alternative Medicine:

          • Homeopathy: This form of alternative medicine is based on principles not recognized by mainstream science, such as the law of similars and the idea that water has memory. Scientific criticism is often countered by suggesting that homeopathy works on subtle energies or planes that conventional science cannot yet detect or understand.

          5: Ideological Constructs in Politics and Culture:

            • Cultural Marxism: This is a theory used by some political groups to describe their perception that progressive academics are subtly undermining traditional values. The theory is structured such that any evidence of cultural change is seen as proof of this plot, whereas evidence to the contrary can be dismissed as part of the conspiracy or as misinformation.

            In each of these cases, the core strategy is similar to Carl Sagan’s hypothetical dragon: continually adapt the ideology to insulate it from criticism and redefine parameters to evade disproof. This ontological flexibility allows proponents to maintain their beliefs in the absence of supportive empirical evidence and even in the face of direct contradictory evidence.

            Several ideologies and mythologies throughout history have employed the “ontological buffet” modus operandi, where fantastical elements are freely added to explain phenomena and bolster the core beliefs:

            • Creation Myths: Many creation stories across cultures involve divine beings with extraordinary powers. These beings can perform feats that defy natural laws, like creating the universe from nothing or breathing life into clay figures. These explanations, while not falsifiable, fall into the category of ontological additions to explain the origin of the world.
            • Conspiracy Theories: Conspiracy theories often rely on a vast network of secret agents, hidden cabals, and technological marvels beyond public knowledge. These entities and qualities (e.g., mind control technology) are introduced to explain complex events without the need for verifiable evidence.
            • Cryptozoology: The search for legendary creatures like Bigfoot or the Loch Ness Monster hinges on the possibility of these creatures existing outside the realm of documented animal life. New characteristics or explanations (e.g., elusive nature, government cover-ups) are added to explain why these creatures haven’t been definitively proven or disproven.
            • Religious Eschatology: Beliefs about the afterlife, especially those involving paradises or hells, often include details about the nature of the soul, the structure of these realms, and the judging of human actions. The lack of ability to directly observe the afterlife allows for the addition of elaborate details and entities (e.g., angels, demons) to support these belief systems.
            • Pseudoscience: Fields like astrology or alchemy often incorporate invisible forces, unknown energies, or hypothesized connections between celestial bodies and human events. These elements function as ontological additions, allowing explanations for phenomena without relying on the scientific method or verifiable evidence.

            It’s important to note that not all aspects of these ideologies or mythologies rely solely on the ontological buffet approach. Some may have elements grounded in cultural traditions, philosophical thought, or even misinterpreted scientific observations. However, the core concept of adding fantastical elements to bolster beliefs without the need for evidence is a recurring theme across these examples.

            Explain the asymmetry between the rhetorical limits on those who employ the ontological buffet and those constrained by proper standards of evidence.

            The asymmetry between those who employ the “ontological buffet” approach—effectively crafting an unfalsifiable belief system by continuously adjusting its tenets to avoid empirical refutation—and those adhering to proper scientific standards of evidence, is substantial and significant in both philosophical and practical terms. This disparity profoundly affects discourse, debate, and the acceptance of ideas within society.

            Rhetorical Limits of the Ontological Buffet Approach

            1: Freedom from Empirical Constraints:

              • Flexibility in Argumentation: Proponents of ideologies that utilize the ontological buffet are not limited by the need to provide empirical evidence. They can adapt their arguments to any counter-evidence or inquiry, often resorting to claims that lie outside the realm of observational science (e.g., supernatural or metaphysical claims).
              • Infinite Adjustability: These belief systems can always introduce new, untestable elements to the ideology as needed (e.g., the dragon in the garage being invisible, incorporeal, and fireless). This adjustability makes the belief system immune to disproof.

              2: Strategic Ambiguity:

                • Vague Assertions: Claims are often made in sufficiently vague terms that they cannot be directly tested or refuted. This vagueness serves as a rhetorical shield against critical scrutiny.
                • Shifting Goalposts: Proponents can change the criteria or conditions required for proving the claim whenever their positions are challenged, a tactic known as shifting the goalposts.

                Rhetorical Limits of Those Constrained by Evidence

                1: Empirical Rigor:

                  • Requirement for Proof: Scientific standards demand reliable, replicable evidence for claims. Scientists and skeptics must base their conclusions on data that can be observed, measured, and confirmed by others.
                  • Falsifiability: A key tenet of scientific inquiry is that claims must be falsifiable—that is, there must be some potential observation or experiment that could prove the claim false. This requirement fundamentally constrains the type of claims that can be seriously considered in scientific discourse.

                  2: Consistency and Coherence:

                    • Logical Consistency: Claims must not only be supported by empirical evidence but also be logically coherent and integrated into the existing body of scientific knowledge without contradiction.
                    • Predictive Power: Scientific theories are expected to provide predictions that can be tested and potentially falsified. Lack of predictive power diminishes the credibility of a scientific theory.

                    Practical and Discursive Consequences

                    • Debate Dynamics: In debates, those employing the ontological buffet approach have a rhetorical advantage because they can always manufacture a way out of objections, while scientific skeptics are limited to arguing within the confines of empirical evidence. This can make scientific skepticism appear weaker or less imaginative, especially in public forums where intricate understanding of evidence and methodology is lacking.
                    • Public Perception and Policy Influence: The apparent certainty and simplicity offered by unfalsifiable ideologies can be more appealing to the public than the conditional, probabilistic statements typical of scientific findings. This can influence public opinion and policy in ways that favor unscientific views.
                    • Educational and Cultural Impact: The spread of ontologically unfalsifiable ideologies can undermine public understanding of science and the acceptance of scientific consensus on issues like climate change, vaccine efficacy, and evolution. This erosion of empirical standards can have broad societal consequences, affecting education, healthcare, and governance.

                    In conclusion, the asymmetry between these two approaches highlights a fundamental challenge in promoting rational discourse and evidence-based policy: the need to communicate the importance and strength of empirical constraints in understanding and interacting with the world, despite the rhetorical challenges posed by unfalsifiable ideologies.

                    There’s a significant asymmetry between those who employ the “ontological buffet” approach and those who rely on proper standards of evidence. Here’s why:

                    Freedom for the Buffet Builder:

                    • Effortless Explanations: Someone using the ontological buffet can effortlessly explain away any challenge to their ideology. New entities (like the “dragon whisperer” in Sagan’s analogy) or qualities (invisible, heatless fire) can be introduced to fit any situation. This flexibility makes their explanations seem comprehensive, even if they lack factual basis.
                    • Shifting the Burden of Proof: The burden of proof falls on those who seek evidence. The “buffet builder” doesn’t need to provide positive proof for their ever-evolving explanations. This can be a tiring and ultimately futile exercise for those seeking factual answers.
                    • No Need for Internal Consistency: The lack of reliance on evidence allows for inconsistencies within the ideology itself. As long as it provides a satisfying narrative for the believer, contradictions might be overlooked or explained away with additional embellishments.

                    Constraints on the Evidence-Based Approach:

                    • The Difficulty of Proving a Negative: Science often deals in probabilities and well-supported theories. Disproving something entirely (e.g., the existence of invisible dragons) can be very difficult, if not impossible. This gives the “buffet builder” an advantage
                    • The Need for Rigorous Testing: Evidence-based explanations must undergo rigorous testing and scrutiny. This can be a slow and meticulous process, often failing to produce quick and definitive answers. This can seem less compelling compared to the seemingly all-encompassing explanations offered by the ontological buffet.
                    • The Challenge of Public Understanding: Scientific concepts can be complex and nuanced. Communicating them effectively to the public can be challenging. This can leave space for the “buffet builder’s” simplistic explanations, even if they lack factual grounding, to gain traction.

                    The Resulting Imbalance:

                    This asymmetry can create an unfair situation. The “buffet builder” can make bold claims without the burden of rigorous proof, while those who demand evidence struggle to disprove the ever-shifting explanations. This can lead to the spread of misinformation and the erosion of trust in evidence-based approaches.

                    It’s important to note:

                    • Critical thinking skills and scientific literacy can help us recognize the limitations of the ontological buffet approach.
                    • Demanding evidence and evaluating the internal consistency of explanations are crucial tools for evaluating ideologies.

                    By understanding this asymmetry, we can be better equipped to challenge unfounded claims and advocate for the importance of evidence-based reasoning.

                    What clues can warn us that an ideology has been fabricated though the ontological buffet process?

                    Recognizing when an ideology has been constructed through the “ontological buffet” process involves identifying certain key characteristics and patterns that signal the use of unfalsifiable assertions, vague premises, and shifting goalposts. These clues help differentiate between genuinely empirical, evidence-based thinking and ideologies crafted to resist refutation regardless of the evidence against them. Here are some signs to look out for:

                    1: Unfalsifiability:

                      • Invisible Mechanisms: The ideology relies on mechanisms that are inherently unobservable and undetectable, such as invisible forces, energies, or entities that conveniently evade all attempts at empirical verification.
                      • Absence of Disproof Considered as Proof: The claim that there is no way to disprove the ideology is used as evidence of its validity, rather than recognizing the scientific principle that a claim should be falsifiable to be testable.

                      2: Shifting Goalposts:

                        • Ad Hoc Adjustments: Whenever the ideology is challenged by new evidence, its proponents modify the initial conditions or explanations to accommodate or dismiss the evidence. These adjustments are made post-hoc and are not based on any new empirical findings.
                        • Evolving Definitions: Key terms and definitions within the ideology change depending on the context or in response to criticism, making it difficult to pin down what exactly is being claimed.

                        3: Vagueness and Ambiguity:

                          • Lack of Specificity: The claims made are overly broad or vague, preventing clear understanding and testing. This vagueness acts as a rhetorical shield against criticism.
                          • Elastic Explanations: The explanations provided can be stretched to fit any observational data, or they are metaphorical and symbolic to the point of non-testability.

                          4: Appeal to Mystery and Complexity:

                            • Mystification: The ideology often appeals to the mysterious, invoking complexity or unknown phenomena as reasons why the ideology cannot be understood or examined by conventional science.
                            • Supernatural Explanations: Explanations resort to supernatural or metaphysical components that do not adhere to the established laws of nature, positioned in such a way that they cannot be empirically assessed.

                            5: Dismissal of Contradictory Evidence:

                              • Rejection of Mainstream Science: Proponents of the ideology systematically dismiss scientific evidence and consensus that contradicts their views, often accusing the scientific community of conspiracy or closed-mindedness.
                              • Selective Use of Data: Evidence that supports the ideology is exaggerated or taken out of context, while evidence against it is minimized, ignored, or discredited.

                              6: Circumstantial Evidence Touted as Conclusive:

                                • Anecdotal Emphasis: Reliance on anecdotal evidence and testimonies as the primary support for the ideology, while disregarding statistical or experimental data that provide a more comprehensive view.
                                • Correlation Equated with Causation: Drawing causal conclusions from mere correlations, often ignoring alternative explanations that are more plausible and better supported by evidence.

                                7: Resistance to Peer Review:

                                  • Insularity: The ideology is promoted within a closed community or echo chamber where critical review and open debate are absent. Proponents may avoid exposing their ideas to peer-reviewed contexts where they might be rigorously evaluated.

                                  8: Persecution Complex:

                                    • Martyrdom Narrative: Believers may portray themselves as victims of mainstream bias or suppression, suggesting that their ideas are too radical or truth-revealing to be accepted by the establishment.

                                    Recognizing these clues can be crucial in assessing the credibility of new theories and ideologies, especially in today’s complex landscape of information where distinguishing between scientifically valid concepts and pseudoscience can be challenging. Engaging critically and applying these criteria helps maintain a clear perspective on what constitutes a robust, evidence-based ideology versus one fabricated through the ontological buffet process.

                                    Here are some clues that can warn us an ideology might be a product of the “ontological buffet” process, where fantastical elements are freely added without the need for evidence:

                                    Red Flags in Explanations:

                                    • Overreliance on Unfalsifiable Claims: The ideology centers around explanations that cannot be definitively disproven through observation or experimentation. This makes it difficult to challenge the core tenets, even if they seem improbable. (e.g., Beliefs about a hidden government controlling everything).
                                    • Constant Introduction of New Entities: The ideology readily introduces new fantastical entities or qualities to explain away any challenges. These additions (e.g., psychic abilities, secret technologies) often lack grounding in established knowledge or evidence.
                                    • Internal Inconsistencies: Despite an overarching narrative, the ideology might contain contradictions or inconsistencies. These might be ignored or explained away with further embellishments, creating a system that prioritizes internal coherence over factual accuracy.

                                    Focus on Narrative over Evidence:

                                    • Emotional Appeals over Reason: The ideology relies heavily on emotional appeals, invoking fear, anger, or a sense of belonging, to win adherents. Critical thinking and the demand for evidence are discouraged.
                                    • Lack of Peer Review or Scrutiny: The core ideas are not subjected to rigorous peer review or scientific scrutiny. The ideology might exist in a closed loop, where information is primarily disseminated by believers, reinforcing existing biases.
                                    • Demonization of Skepticism: Critical thinking and questioning are seen as threats to the ideology. Skeptics might be ostracized or labeled as enemies, discouraging open discussion and examination of the core beliefs.

                                    Beware the Allure of Certainty:

                                    • Simple Answers to Complex Problems: The ideology offers seemingly simple explanations for complex issues. This can be appealing in a world with many unanswered questions, but true solutions are rarely black and white.
                                    • Us vs. Them Mentality: The ideology fosters a sense of in-group vs. out-group mentality. Believers see themselves as enlightened or chosen, while those who question the ideology are seen as ignorant or even malicious.
                                    • Resistance to Updating Beliefs: New evidence or contradictory information is dismissed or reinterpreted to fit the existing narrative. The ideology remains rigid and inflexible, even in the face of new discoveries.

                                    By recognizing these clues, we can become more critical consumers of information. If an ideology relies heavily on these tactics, it’s a good sign to investigate its sources, demand evidence for its claims, and be wary of explanations that seem too easy or rely on fantastical elements.
