The question of whether pragmatic considerations can legitimately lower epistemic standards of evidence touches on deep philosophical debates between pragmatism and more traditional epistemological views. The argument that you’ve outlined suggests that in certain contexts, the practical benefits of believing a claim might justify accepting it with less stringent evidence than would otherwise be required. This stance raises several key points for consideration:

  1. Pragmatic vs. Epistemic Justification: Pragmatic justification focuses on the practical consequences of holding a belief, whereas epistemic justification concerns the truth of the belief and the adequacy of evidence supporting it. The argument implies a possible conflict between these two types of justification, suggesting that in some cases, pragmatic considerations might outweigh purely epistemic ones.
  2. Contextual Relativism: Some philosophers argue that standards of evidence can be context-dependent. In matters of scientific inquiry, for example, rigorous evidence is paramount for advancing knowledge. In personal or existential matters, however, the standards might be viewed more flexibly, taking into account the consequences of belief or disbelief.
  3. William James and The Will to Believe: Philosopher William James famously argued that in cases where a decision is forced, momentous, and live, and where the evidence is inconclusive, it is permissible to decide based on passion rather than evidence alone. This stance suggests that in certain existential or moral contexts, pragmatic considerations could justify lowering epistemic standards.
  4. Risks of Lowering Epistemic Standards: Critics of lowering epistemic standards for pragmatic reasons point out the risks of endorsing beliefs without adequate evidence. These include the potential for self-deception, the propagation of false beliefs, and the undermining of rational inquiry. Furthermore, determining which pragmatic considerations are sufficient to justify lowering standards can be highly subjective and contentious.
  5. Consequences of Belief: The argument also hinges on the assumption that the consequences of belief justify the belief itself. This raises questions about the nature of belief, the ethics of belief, and the relationship between belief and action. It challenges the notion that beliefs should be held purely based on evidence and truth, suggesting instead that the utility of a belief can be a factor in its justification.
  6. Faith and Reason: The specific example of religious belief, such as belief in the resurrection of Jesus for redemption, highlights the tension between faith and reason. In religious contexts, faith often involves belief in the absence of empirical evidence or in the face of inconclusive evidence, with justification rooted in spiritual, moral, or existential grounds rather than empirical verification.

In summary, whether pragmatic considerations can legitimately lower epistemic standards of evidence is a complex and contested issue. The question of whether pragmatic considerations can justify lowering epistemic standards for evidence is a complex one with compelling arguments on both sides. Here’s a breakdown of the different perspectives:

Arguments for lowering standards based on pragmatism:

  • Pascal’s Wager: This classic argument suggests that if the potential benefits of believing a claim outweigh the costs of disbelief, even in the absence of strong evidence, then believing might be a rational choice. In the context of the resurrected Jesus, the potential benefit of eternal salvation could outweigh the cost of potentially being wrong.
  • Psychological benefits: Belief in certain propositions, even with limited evidence, can offer psychological comfort, hope, or motivation. Some argue that these benefits may justify a less rigorous approach to evidence evaluation in specific personal contexts.
  • Social cohesion: Shared beliefs, even without full epistemic justification, can be crucial for social order and cultural stability. This might be relevant in religious communities where belief in specific tenets forms the bedrock of their identity.

Arguments against lowering standards based on pragmatism:

  • Erosion of intellectual integrity: Sacrificing rigor and objectivity for desired outcomes can undermine the very foundation of knowledge and truth-seeking. Once we accept such an approach, it sets a dangerous precedent for accepting other unsubstantiated claims for purely pragmatic reasons.
  • Potential for harm: Belief in unverified claims, even with good intentions, can lead to harmful consequences in personal and societal spheres. For example, faith healing based on unfounded beliefs could discourage individuals from seeking proper medical care.
  • Slippery slope: Lowering standards in one specific case can pave the way for doing so in others, gradually eroding the importance of evidence and leading to a decline in overall critical thinking.

Ultimately, whether pragmatic considerations can legitimize lowering epistemic standards is a matter of individual value judgment and risk tolerance. Some might prioritize potential benefits and take a leap of faith based on limited evidence, while others might consider the risks of sacrificing intellectual integrity and potential harm too high.
