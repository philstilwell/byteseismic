Assess the following statement against the notion that rational belief is a degree of belief that maps to the degree of the relevant evidence.

It seems to me that, if you have more evidence for one view rather than for another view, then you are intellectually obligated to believe the one that is most reasonable, that is most likely, and not believe the one that is least likely.

Greg Koukl — STR Podcast — June 28, 2023

The statement in question approaches belief as if it were binary, rather than a gradient. The use of phrases like “believe the one that is most reasonable” and “not believe the one that is least likely” implies that belief is an either/or state—either one fully believes a proposition or does not believe it at all. This dichotomy overlooks the concept of gradational belief, where the degree of belief can vary in proportion to the evidence available.

In the context of rational belief as a degree that maps to the degree of the relevant evidence, belief should not be strictly binary but should adjust along a spectrum according to how much and what kind of evidence supports a given proposition. The statement does not clearly acknowledge this gradational nature; instead, it suggests a threshold of belief that one must either meet or not meet, which is contrary to the idea that belief should scale with evidence.

Moreover, the statement does not explicitly treat 50% as a special threshold on the epistemic gradient, but it does hint at a similar concept by suggesting a cutoff between what beliefs one should hold versus those one should not, based on their likelihood. This approach can be seen as implicitly aligning with a threshold model, where there is a specific point at which disbelief turns into belief. However, this is a simplification and can be seen as an illegitimate treatment of belief if one adheres strictly to the idea that rational belief should continuously vary with the strength of evidence, without arbitrary cutoffs.

In summary, the statement treats belief more as a binary condition than as a gradient aligned with varying degrees of evidence, and while it does not explicitly set 50% as a special threshold, it implies a threshold-like approach to belief that does not fully reflect the gradational nature proposed by the notion that rational belief should map directly to the degree of relevant evidence.

The statement you provided seems to treat belief as a binary, all-or-nothing proposition rather than viewing it as a gradient that maps to the degree of evidence. The phrasing “believe the one that is most reasonable” and “not believe the one that is least likely” implies a dichotomy where one either fully believes or does not believe a proposition, without acknowledging intermediate degrees of belief or uncertainty.

The notion of rational belief as a degree that maps to the degree of relevant evidence suggests a more nuanced and continuous spectrum, where the strength of one’s belief should align with the weight of the supporting evidence. As evidence accumulates or diminishes, the degree of belief should adjust accordingly, without any special thresholds or abrupt transitions.

The statement you provided does not explicitly treat 50% as a special threshold, but by framing belief as a binary choice between “most reasonable” and “least likely,” it implicitly suggests that once the evidence crosses some implicit threshold, one must fully commit to believing or disbelieving the proposition, rather than maintaining a graded level of uncertainty or confidence.

In summary, the statement seems to contradict the idea of rational belief as a gradient that maps to the degree of evidence, as it portrays belief as an all-or-nothing proposition and fails to acknowledge the possibility of holding intermediate degrees of belief or uncertainty based on the weight of evidence.

How might you respond to such a statement to demonstrate that belief is not binary and that there is no special threshold along the epistemic gradient that demands a binary flipping of our epistemic commitment from disbelief to belief?

Belief can vary in intensity and certainty based on the quantity and quality of evidence supporting a proposition. You can use an analogy, such as a dimmer switch on a light, which allows for various levels of brightness corresponding to the degree of evidence, rather than a simple on/off light switch.
  2. Discuss the Implications of a Gradient View: Elaborate on how viewing belief as a spectrum allows for a more nuanced understanding of our convictions. This perspective acknowledges that our confidence in different beliefs can and should vary in proportion to the supporting evidence. This approach is more reflective of real-world decision-making, where rarely anything is absolutely certain, and decisions are often made under varying degrees of uncertainty.
  3. Challenge the Idea of a Threshold: Address the notion of there being a specific threshold (such as 50%) that categorically shifts one from disbelief to belief. Argue that such thresholds are arbitrary and do not realistically reflect how we process information and form beliefs. Instead, emphasize that belief strength increases gradually as evidence accumulates, and there isn’t a definitive point where one suddenly flips from disbelief to belief.
  4. Provide Examples: Use practical examples to illustrate your point. For instance, in science, theories are not simply accepted or rejected outright but are believed with varying degrees of confidence based on the accumulation of evidence over time.   5. Cite Philosophical Precedents: Refer to philosophical approaches that support the gradient view of belief, such as Bayesian epistemology, which treats belief quantitatively and adjusts it incrementally as new evidence is introduced. By structuring your response this way, you not only refute the binary view of belief presented in the statement but also promote a richer, more accurate understanding of how beliefs realistically function in light of evidence.

As the evidence accumulates in favor of one view over another, our degree of belief should shift gradually along a continuous spectrum, without any abrupt transitions or special thresholds. There is no magical point at which the evidence becomes sufficient to demand a binary flip from disbelief to full belief. Rather, as the evidence grows stronger, our confidence in the claim should increase proportionately, while still acknowledging the potential for uncertainty or error.

Even with a preponderance of evidence favoring one view, it may be reasonable to maintain some degree of uncertainty or openness to revising our position if new, compelling evidence emerges. Treating belief as binary runs the risk of becoming entrenched in our views, ignoring or discounting contradictory evidence, and failing to update our beliefs as new information becomes available.

Instead of framing it as an intellectual obligation to ‘believe the one that is most reasonable,’ I would suggest that we have an obligation to calibrate our degree of belief to the degree of supporting evidence, while remaining open to revising our views as the evidence evolves. This allows for a more nuanced, flexible, and rational approach to belief formation and epistemology.”

What are other terms similar to “preponderance” that illegitimately suggest there is a threshold along the epistemic gradient at which disbelief should flip to belief?

The idea of a threshold along the epistemic gradient at which disbelief should flip to belief is often implicitly suggested through various terms that imply a binary transition. These terms can subtly reinforce the notion that belief is an all-or-nothing state, rather than a continuum. Here are some terms similar to “preponderance” that might be used in ways that suggest such a threshold:

  1. Assent to – This term implies a point at which one agrees to or approves a belief, suggesting a definite transition from non-acceptance to acceptance.
  2. Accept – Similar to “assent to,” “accept” indicates a moment of adopting or taking on a belief, implying a clear switch from rejection.
  3. Conclude – To conclude often means to arrive at a belief or a decision after consideration, indicating a finality or closure that suggests crossing a threshold.
  4. Affirm – This term indicates a strong agreement or confirmation of a belief, which can imply a solidified state of belief as opposed to a more tentative or probabilistic stance.
  5. Endorse – To endorse a belief is to officially support or approve it, suggesting a definitive shift from doubt to backing.
  6. Subscribe to – Implies a formal agreement or alignment with a belief, echoing the idea of signing up or committing, which can suggest a binary switch.
  7. Adopt – This suggests choosing or taking up a belief, implying a decision point at which one goes from not holding to holding that belief.
  8. Commit to – This term explicitly signals a decisive and firm decision to believe something, reinforcing the binary nature of belief at a specific threshold.

Each of these terms can be used in contexts that reinforce a binary view of belief, especially when they are not qualified with indications of the degree of belief or uncertainty that might still be present. Such language might simplify complex realities where beliefs actually exist along a continuum and are subject to change with new evidence or reinterpretation of existing evidence.

Here are some other terms that can illegitimately suggest a threshold along the epistemic gradient at which disbelief should flip to belief:

  1. “Assent to” or “assent to the truth of” – This phrasing implies giving full assent/agreement to a proposition as true, rather than holding a graded belief.
  2. “Accept” or “accept as true” – Similar to assent, this suggests fully accepting a claim as true rather than having a degree of belief proportional to the evidence.
  3. “Endorse” or “endorse the view” – Endorsing a view carries the connotation of fully getting behind it, rather than having a nuanced level of certainty.
  4. “Embrace” or “embrace the idea” – Embracing an idea implies wholly adopting it as true, not just partially believing it based on the evidence.
  5. “Conviction” or “firmly convinced” – Having a firm conviction or being firmly convinced suggests a binary, entrenched belief rather than a graded position.
  6. “Subscribe to” or “subscription to the theory” – Subscribing to a theory implies fully signing on to it, not just partially or tentatively believing it.
  7. “Allegiance to” or “allegiance to the hypothesis” – Allegiance suggests an unwavering commitment, rather than proportional uncertainty.
  8. “Advocate for” or “advocacy of the position” – Advocating for a position implies arguing for its truth, not just partially believing it based on evidence.
  9. “Uphold” or “upholding the claim” – Upholding a claim suggests defending it as wholly true, rather than believing it to a certain degree.
  10. “Championing” or “champion the cause” – Championing a cause implies full-throated support, not nuanced belief updating.

Many of these terms carry connotations of fully committing to, defending, or arguing for the truth of a proposition, rather than holding a belief that is carefully calibrated to the weight of the current evidence. They can suggest illegitimate thresholds where one must wholly flip to belief or disbelief.

Some of the terms in the section above appear appropriate when the degree of belief is fairly high. Provide guidelines to when more nuanced terms might be necessary to encourage the mapping of our degree of certainty to the degree of the evidence.

When considering how to appropriately map the language we use to the degree of certainty and the corresponding evidence, it’s beneficial to choose terms that accurately reflect the fluid and often tentative nature of belief. Here are some guidelines on when to use more nuanced terms to foster a clearer representation of how beliefs align with the evidence:

1. In Early Stages of Evidence Gathering

  • Use terms like:
    • “Consider”
    • “Explore”
    • “Investigate”
  • Rationale: These terms are useful when evidence is still being collected and an initial hypothesis is being examined. They suggest openness to different outcomes and a withholding of firm commitment until more evidence is available.

2. When Evidence is Mixed or Conflicting

  • Use terms like:
    • “Lean towards”
    • “Tend to favor”
    • “Hypothesize”
  • Rationale: In situations where evidence does not clearly support one conclusion over another, these expressions appropriately communicate a tentative leaning or bias without committing fully to a conclusion.

3. When Incremental Evidence Supports a Growing Trend

  • Use terms like:
    • “Increasingly convinced”
    • “Growing confidence in”
  • Rationale: These phrases are suitable when evidence accumulates in a particular direction, but the total body of evidence has not yet reached a level of completeness or certainty that justifies full belief.

4. High Degree of Evidence but Still Below Certainty

  • Use terms like:
    • “Strongly believe”
    • “Highly probable”
  • Rationale: When evidence strongly supports a particular conclusion but falls short of absolute certainty, these terms convey a high level of confidence while still acknowledging the possibility of alternative explanations.

5. Communicating Statistical or Probabilistic Evidence

  • Use terms like:
    • “Likely”
    • “Unlikely”
    • “Probable”
  • Rationale: These terms are particularly useful in contexts where decisions or beliefs are based on statistical models or probabilistic assessments. They precisely indicate the likelihood of outcomes, helping to map belief directly to quantifiable evidence.

6. Reflecting Revisions Based on New Information

  • Use terms like:
    • “Re-evaluating”
    • “Adjusting belief towards”
  • Rationale: When new evidence emerges that contradicts or modifies previous understandings, these terms effectively communicate the dynamic and revisable nature of belief.

By carefully selecting language that reflects the degree of certainty and the strength of evidence, communicators can more accurately represent their epistemic stance and encourage a more nuanced and evidence-based approach to forming beliefs. This practice not only enhances clarity and precision in discourse but also fosters a more thoughtful and analytical approach to evaluating information and making decisions.

You make a fair point. Some of those terms may be appropriate when the degree of belief is very high and closely aligned with an overwhelming amount of supporting evidence. However, in many cases, using more nuanced language can better encourage mapping our degree of certainty to the degree of evidence in a proportional way. Here are some guidelines for when more nuanced phrasing might be necessary:

1) When the evidence is mixed, contradictory or inconclusive: In such cases, words like “accept”, “endorse”, “conviction” etc. would be too strong. More nuanced terms like “tendency to believe”, “leaning towards”, “provisional belief” etc. better capture the uncertainty.

2) When new evidence is still emerging: If a body of evidence is still evolving and growing, nuanced language like “current assessment”, “present inclination”, “evidence so far suggests” etc. avoids overstating certainty prematurely.

3) For highly complex issues with many variables: On nuanced topics with many interacting factors, phrases like “weight of evidence tilts towards”, “balance of probabilities favors” etc. better capture the difficulty of certainty.

4) When beliefs are based heavily on inductive reasoning: Inductive arguments don’t guarantee truth, so nuanced terms like “inductive grounds to believe”, “inference to the best explanation” etc. qualify the belief.

5) For emotionally/ideologically charged issues: On topics prone to biases, nuance with “appears most supportable”, “currently most plausible” etc. acknowledges potential distortions.

6) When communicating with strong preexisting beliefs: Nuanced language like “evidence suggests revising view to…” is less confrontational than “accept/endorse…”

7) For beliefs with potential severe consequences: On issues with major impacts, reserving absolute certainty with “evidence compellingly indicates” leaves prudent room for revision.

In essence, more nuanced phrasing is warranted when the evidence is limited, conflicting, still evolving, difficult to evaluate objectively, or when beliefs are prone to human biases and shortcomings. Proportioning language to evidence improves calibration.
