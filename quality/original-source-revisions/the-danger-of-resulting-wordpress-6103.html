Annie Duke introduces a concept called “resulting”. Provide a definition for and examples of “resulting”.

“Resulting” is a term popularized by Annie Duke, a former professional poker player and author, in her discussions about decision-making theory. The concept revolves around the tendency to judge a decision by its outcome rather than by the quality of the decision at the time it was made.

Definition of Resulting

Resulting is the cognitive bias that occurs when people equate the quality of a decision with the quality of its outcome. This bias leads to a misunderstanding of the relationship between outcomes and decision-making, often causing people to overvalue their success or failure as a direct reflection of their decision-making abilities. Essentially, it suggests that good outcomes always stem from good decisions and bad outcomes from bad decisions, which isn’t necessarily true.

Examples of Resulting

  1. Poker Game Decisions:
  • In poker, a player might decide to go all in with a hand that statistically stands a good chance of winning (e.g., a 75% probability). If the player loses due to the opponent drawing a lucky card, resulting would be criticizing the “all in” decision purely based on the loss, rather than recognizing the decision was statistically sound.
  1. Sports Coaching Decisions:
  • A football coach decides to go for a two-point conversion in the final seconds of a game instead of a safer one-point kick to tie and extend the game. If the two-point attempt fails and the team loses, resulting would involve fans criticizing the coach’s decision as poor, even if the decision itself could be justified by certain strategic or game-specific factors.
  1. Business Decisions:
  • A CEO decides to invest heavily in a new technology. If the technology sector unexpectedly crashes due to external economic factors, resulting would occur if the board views the decision to invest as poor, despite it being a reasonable decision with the information available at the time.
  1. Medical Decisions:
  • A doctor chooses a treatment plan that is considered the best option based on clinical evidence and patient history. If the patient reacts poorly to the treatment due to an unforeseen complication, resulting would involve judging the doctor’s initial decision as wrong, ignoring the fact that the decision was appropriate with the given data.

In each of these examples, “resulting” showcases a flawed logic pathway where the outcome unfairly influences the perception of the decision’s quality. This bias can lead to overly conservative or risk-averse behaviors and a misunderstanding of randomness and risk in decision-making processes.

Annie Duke defines “resulting” as the tendency to judge the quality of a decision based solely on the outcome, rather than the process itself.

Here are some examples of “resulting”:

  • Investment decision: You invest in a company that does well and see it as a good decision, but you might have made the same decision based on bad information and just gotten lucky.
  • Sports game: A coach blames poor player performance after a loss, without considering if they made the best strategic decisions with the players they had.
  • Daily life: You choose to take an umbrella because the weather forecast predicted rain, but it ends up being sunny. You might think it was a bad decision to take the umbrella, even though it was based on the best information available at the time.

“Resulting” has often influenced historical evaluations, where the outcomes of decisions were used to judge their inherent quality without adequate consideration of the context or the decision-making process at the time. Here are three notable historical examples:

1. The Bay of Pigs Invasion (1961)

Event: In 1961, the U.S. government, under President John F. Kennedy, sponsored an invasion of Cuba by Cuban exiles intending to overthrow Fidel Castro. The plan failed disastrously, with most of the invaders captured or killed within days.

Resulting Example: The outcome was widely regarded as a massive blunder for U.S. foreign policy. Historically, critics have used the failure as evidence of poor decision-making by the Kennedy administration. However, the decision itself was based on the CIA’s assessments and the geopolitical strategies of the Cold War era, which were not necessarily flawed given the information available at the time. The judgment of this operation often hinges more on its embarrassing failure than on the rationale behind it.

2. The Charge of the Light Brigade (1854)

Event: During the Battle of Balaclava in the Crimean War, British cavalry, known as the Light Brigade, was mistakenly sent into a frontal assault against a well-entrenched Russian artillery. The charge resulted in high British casualties.

Resulting Example: This military action is frequently cited as an example of heroic but disastrous misjudgment. The decision to charge was immediately seen as folly because of the severe British losses. However, the decision was made under confusing and chaotic battle conditions, and it ignored the fact that the officers were acting on possibly misinterpreted orders. The critique often overlooks these critical nuances, focusing solely on the catastrophic outcome.

3. The Treaty of Versailles (1919)

Event: The Treaty of Versailles formally ended World War I, imposing heavy reparations and territorial losses on Germany. The harshness of the treaty is often credited with setting the stage for World War II.

Resulting Example: Historically, the treaty has been labeled as a poor decision because it led to economic devastation in Germany, which in turn facilitated the rise of Adolf Hitler and the Nazi Party. However, at the time, the decision to impose such terms was driven by a desire to cripple Germany militarily and economically to prevent future conflicts. The resulting view critiques the decision based on the eventual rise of the Nazi regime, rather than evaluating the context and objectives of the Allied powers post-World War I.

In each case, “resulting” leads to a retrospective judgment that may not accurately reflect the decision’s quality at the time it was made. These examples show how complex decisions can be oversimplified when outcomes are used as the primary measure of decision-making effectiveness.

  2. The American Civil War: The South’s decision to secede is often seen as a clear mistake in hindsight. However, through the lens of “resulting,” we can understand that the Southerners might have genuinely believed secession was the best course of action based on their economic interests and fear of federal overreach.
  3. The Voyages of Christopher Columbus: Columbus’ “discovery” of the Americas is often celebrated. However, focusing just on the outcome ignores the devastating consequences for the indigenous populations. “Resulting” reminds us to consider the full picture, not just the positive outcome for Europeans.

How can I apply Annie Duke’s notion of “resulting” to my dating life?

Applying Annie Duke’s concept of “resulting” to your dating life can provide valuable insights into how you evaluate experiences and make decisions in relationships. Understanding and mitigating the impact of this bias can help you make better choices and maintain a healthier perspective on dating. Here’s how to apply the notion of “resulting” to your dating life:

1. Evaluate Decisions Independently of Outcomes

Explanation: In dating, as in poker, not every good decision leads to a good outcome, and not every bad decision leads to a bad outcome. For instance, deciding to date someone because you share common interests and values is a sound decision, regardless of whether the relationship ultimately lasts.

Application: If a relationship ends, instead of concluding that your decision to enter the relationship was wrong, assess the decision based on the information you had at the time. Were there genuine reasons to believe it was a good match? If so, the decision was sound, even if the outcome was unfavorable.

2. Recognize the Role of Luck and Circumstance

Explanation: Many uncontrollable factors influence dating outcomes, including timing, external stressors (like job loss or family issues), and the other person’s emotional state or readiness for a relationship.

Application: When a date or relationship doesn’t work out, consider these external factors before judging your decision-making. Acknowledging that luck and circumstance play a role can prevent unfair self-criticism and help you maintain resilience in your dating efforts.

3. Avoid Overcorrection Based on Outcomes

Explanation: A common mistake in dating, as in other areas of life, is to make radical changes based on one or two negative outcomes. This can lead to overcorrection, where you change your approach drastically, potentially missing out on good matches in the future.

Application: If a seemingly perfect relationship ends unexpectedly, resist the urge to overhaul your “dating criteria” completely. Instead, analyze specific aspects that might need adjustment without abandoning what could still work well for you.

4. Develop a Process for Decision Making

Explanation: Creating a structured approach to making decisions can help you focus on the process rather than just the outcomes. This involves identifying what qualities and relationship goals are most important to you and consistently applying these criteria when considering potential partners.

Application: Define what you want in a partner and relationship, and use these criteria to guide your dating decisions. This method helps you stay true to your values and make decisions that are logically sound, irrespective of the outcomes.

5. Learn from Each Experience

Explanation: While you shouldn’t judge a decision solely by its outcome, every outcome still offers valuable data. Analyzing what went right and what went wrong can refine your decision-making process.

Application: After a relationship ends, take the time to reflect not just on what happened, but also on how and why you made your decisions. What can you learn about your preferences, your deal-breakers, and your approach to conflict? Use these insights to make more informed decisions in the future.

Conclusion

By applying the concept of “resulting” to your dating life, you can cultivate a more balanced and thoughtful approach to relationships. This strategy encourages you to focus on making well-considered decisions based on a clear set of criteria, rather than fluctuating wildly in response to the highs and lows of dating outcomes.

How can I apply Annie Duke’s notion of “resulting” to my investment portfolio?

Applying Annie Duke’s concept of “resulting” to investment portfolio management can significantly improve how you evaluate the success of your investments and refine your decision-making process. This concept is particularly useful in financial contexts because it helps separate the quality of decisions from the randomness of outcomes. Here’s how to integrate “resulting” into managing your investments:

1. Evaluate Investment Decisions on Their Own Merits

Explanation: Just as with poker decisions, investment decisions should be evaluated based on the information and rationale at the time the decision was made, not solely on the outcome.

Application: When an investment performs poorly, instead of immediately labeling the decision as bad, analyze the decision-making process. Was the investment decision based on sound research and aligned with your risk tolerance and investment goals? If yes, it may still have been a good decision, even if the market dynamics did not favor the outcome.

2. Understand the Role of Luck and External Factors

Explanation: The markets are influenced by numerous external factors such as economic changes, political events, and global incidents, which can affect investment outcomes.

Application: Recognize that even well-thought-out investments can fail due to unforeseen events. Separating these external factors from your decision-making process can prevent you from making rash decisions based on outcomes influenced by luck or factors beyond your control.

3. Avoid Outcome Bias in Future Decisions

Explanation: Outcome bias occurs when a decision is judged based on its result rather than how it was made. This can lead to overcorrection or inappropriate adjustments in strategy.

Application: If a particular stock or asset class underperforms, do not automatically exclude it from your portfolio. Analyze whether the fundamental reasons for your initial investment have changed. If they haven’t, it might still be a valid part of a diversified portfolio.

4. Develop a Robust Decision-Making Framework

Explanation: A structured decision-making process can provide consistency and reduce the emotional impact of market fluctuations on your decisions.

Application: Establish clear criteria for buying and selling investments, which might include financial metrics, company performance, economic indicators, or alignment with broader market trends. This framework should help guide your investment decisions, making them less susceptible to the influence of “resulting.”

5. Learn from Outcomes to Refine Your Process

Explanation: While it’s important not to confuse the quality of a decision with its outcome, outcomes still provide valuable information that can be used to refine your decision-making process.

Application: Regularly review your investment decisions and their outcomes. Consider what you can learn from the investments that did not work out as expected. Is there a pattern that suggests a flaw in your decision-making process? Use this analysis to make systematic improvements to how you evaluate potential investments.

6. Maintain Emotional Equanimity

Explanation: Emotional reactions to winning and losing can cloud judgment and lead to poor investment decisions.

Application: Develop habits and attitudes that maintain your emotional balance regardless of market conditions. This might involve setting rules for when and how you review your investment portfolio (e.g., not reacting to daily fluctuations but reviewing quarterly) to prevent emotional responses from dictating your decisions.

Conclusion

By applying Annie Duke’s concept of “resulting” to your investment strategy, you can create a more disciplined and rational approach to portfolio management. This will help you make decisions based on sound reasoning and established criteria, rather than reactive adjustments prompted by the sometimes deceptive nature of investment outcomes.

How might we identify when we have become susceptible to “resulting”?

Identifying when you are susceptible to “resulting” is crucial for maintaining rational decision-making across various aspects of life, including business, personal choices, and especially in environments with high uncertainty like investing or sports. Here are some key indicators and strategies to recognize and manage this cognitive bias:

1. Overemphasis on Recent Outcomes

  • Indicator: You might find yourself focusing excessively on the latest outcomes rather than the overall trends or the quality of the decisions leading up to those outcomes.
  • Management Strategy: Practice systematic reflection by reviewing decisions after significant time intervals. This helps to assess decisions based on the information available at the time, rather than their outcomes. Use a decision journal to track your rationale and expectations when making decisions to provide a clearer retrospective assessment.

2. Post-Outcome Rationalization

  • Indicator: Rationalizing decisions strictly based on how they turned out, rather than the validity of the decision-making process itself. This often manifests as “I was right because I won” or “I was wrong because I lost.”
  • Management Strategy: Encourage and practice thinking in probabilities. Acknowledge that a good decision can have a bad outcome and vice versa. This probability-based thinking helps mitigate the tendency to rationalize decisions post hoc.

3. Emotional Decision-Making

  • Indicator: Feeling particularly emotional about outcomes, which then influences future decisions. For example, feeling devastated by a loss might lead you to avoid similar decisions in the future, even when they would be rationally justified.
  • Management Strategy: Implement rules that govern your decision-making process, which can act as checks against emotional reactions. For instance, in investing, set predefined criteria for buying and selling stocks that are based on financial indicators rather than emotional responses to market movements.

4. Pattern Recognition Errors

  • Indicator: Misinterpreting random events as patterns (e.g., seeing patterns in losses or wins and basing decisions on these misconceptions).
  • Management Strategy: Enhance your statistical and analytical skills to better understand variance and randomness. Learning the basics of statistics can provide a more accurate interpretation of events and reduce the misidentification of patterns where none exist.

5. Short-Term Focus

  • Indicator: Concentrating on immediate results rather than long-term outcomes. This short-term focus can skew your perception of what constitutes a ‘successful’ decision.
  • Management Strategy: Set long-term goals and assess decisions based on how well they align with these objectives. Shifting focus from short-term outcomes to long-term results can provide a more balanced view and reduce the impact of “resulting.”

6. Lack of Robust Review Processes

  • Indicator: Not having a consistent method for reviewing and evaluating decisions.
  • Management Strategy: Develop a formal review process for decision analysis. This should include a critique of both the outcomes and the decision processes. Regularly scheduled reviews can help identify when resulting is influencing your evaluations.

7. Echo Chambers and Confirmation Bias

  • Indicator: Surrounding yourself with opinions or data that reinforce the outcomes rather than challenge the process.
  • Management Strategy: Actively seek diverse perspectives and constructive criticism. Engaging with a variety of viewpoints can help check your biases and enhance decision-making frameworks.

Conclusion

By recognizing these indicators and implementing strategic management practices, you can better identify when you are falling into the trap of “resulting.” This awareness will enable you to maintain a more balanced and rational approach to decision-making, focusing on process quality over outcome-based evaluations. This shift is essential for achieving sustained success in complex, uncertain environments.

Here are some signs that you might be susceptible to “resulting”:

Emotional Reactions:

  • Excessive focus on outcomes: Do you find yourself dwelling on the results of decisions, even if you made well-informed choices at the time?
  • Feeling discouraged by setbacks: Do setbacks in your personal or professional life lead to feelings of self-doubt or questioning your abilities?
  • Celebrating only success: Do you only feel good about your decisions when they lead to positive outcomes?

Decision-Making Biases:

  • Hindsight bias: Do you often look back on situations and think, “I should have known that”? This is a classic sign of focusing on the outcome rather than the decision-making process.
  • Confirmation bias: Do you tend to seek out information that confirms your existing beliefs about a decision, even if there’s contradictory evidence?
  • Regret aversion: Do you avoid making decisions out of fear of making the wrong choice, even if it leads to missed opportunities?

Focus on Control:

  • Feeling a need to justify past decisions: Do you find yourself constantly explaining or defending your choices, even if the outcome wasn’t ideal?
  • Obsessive analysis of past events: Do you spend excessive time replaying scenarios in your head, wishing you could have done things differently?
  • Difficulty letting go of mistakes: Do you hold onto past mistakes and let them impact your present decisions?

If you recognize some of these signs in yourself, here are some tips to overcome your susceptibility to “resulting”:

  • Focus on the process: Actively remind yourself to analyze the quality of your decisions based on the information available at the time, not just the outcome.
  • Practice self-compassion: Accept that mistakes are inevitable and view them as learning opportunities.
  • Celebrate good choices: Acknowledge the times you made well-informed decisions, even if the outcome wasn’t ideal.
  • Embrace uncertainty: Accept that many situations have unpredictable elements, and focus on what you can control – your decision-making process.
  • Seek external perspective: Discuss your decision-making with a trusted friend or mentor to gain a different perspective.
  • Practice mindfulness techniques: Mindfulness practices can help you stay present and reduce the tendency to dwell on the past or worry about the future.

By becoming aware of your susceptibility to “resulting” and adopting these strategies, you can make more objective decisions and navigate life’s uncertainties with greater confidence.
