Under these strictly defined conditions, there is ethical culpability in choosing universal death through inaction. This doesn’t make the alternative morally good, but rather the “least bad” option in an impossible situation. The culpability stems not from failing to kill, but from choosing the option that maximizes suffering and ensures complete extinction when an alternative, though terrible, exists.

This conclusion is specific to this thought experiment’s parameters and should not be generalized to real-world situations where uncertainty exists or other options are available.

Refugees and Natives: A Compassionate Response to Shared Survival

The plight of 100 refugees seeking asylum on a secluded island of 300 natives touches on profoundly human questions about coexistence, survival, and responsibility. While traditional ethical frameworks often invoke universal principles or moral absolutes, a compassionate approach grounded in moral non-realism provides an alternative viewpoint. This perspective focuses less on fixed “rights” and more on the potential for empathy and shared human experiences to foster coexistence. By engaging with the scenario through compassion and practicality, both populations can cultivate an environment of respect, mutual benefit, and survival without appealing to objective moral truths.

Compassion as a Practical Response

In moral non-realism, concepts like “rights” or “moral duties” are not intrinsic; rather, they are constructs that can be useful for organizing society. Here, a non-realist perspective underscores that “right” and “wrong” are not absolute but are subject to human interpretation, shaped by cultural contexts and the needs of survival. A compassionate non-realist sees the emotional landscape of suffering and relief as central to decision-making, acknowledging the subjective nature of value while striving to reduce harm for all involved. Compassion thus becomes not only an ethical choice but a practical one, uniting both groups in a quest for shared welfare.

Cooperative Survival over Individual Entitlements

The island, a space with finite resources, creates a situation where traditional entitlements—such as exclusive property rights or autonomy—may hinder survival rather than support it. For example, a moral non-realist might argue that prioritizing strict property rights would ultimately damage the potential for cooperation and endanger all lives on the island. Instead, cooperative arrangements allow both groups to focus on shared goals. Resources like food, water, and shelter could be pooled, with each group contributing labor and skills toward communal survival. By promoting collective care over individual entitlements, compassion-driven pragmatism becomes a guide for action, forging a path that values the welfare of the community as a whole.

Respect for Differences within Shared Spaces

Another challenge in this scenario involves navigating cultural and religious differences. The refugees fled their homeland due to persecution, likely valuing freedom of belief and security as central to their needs. At the same time, the natives might fear that cultural integration threatens their way of life. A moral non-realist would advocate for respecting these differences without needing to establish universal moral rules. In this light, cultural spaces could be shared with boundaries that acknowledge and respect each group’s unique customs, creating a system that balances the integration of new values without undermining the natives’ traditions.

By creating physical or temporal spaces for cultural expression—such as designated areas or times for religious practices—each group’s emotional and cultural needs can be honored. This approach does not imply objective cultural “rights” but rather an understanding that a respectful exchange minimizes conflict and contributes to communal harmony.

Communication as the Foundation of Cohesion

One of the greatest barriers to integration and cooperation is often misunderstanding. In a situation as precarious as this, maintaining open channels of communication is critical. A compassionate moral non-realist approach emphasizes practical empathy: understanding the other group’s fears, hopes, and needs without imposing rigid ethical standards. The practical benefits of transparent communication and conflict resolution processes offer a means to establish trust without reliance on moral absolutes.

For instance, a joint council comprising members from both groups could mediate disputes and oversee the distribution of resources, ensuring that all voices are heard. Here, practical compassion provides a functional solution, underscoring that while no objective moral duty exists to create such a council, the benefits of cooperative engagement are clear.

Sustaining Compassionate Reciprocity

In moral non-realism, reciprocity is valuable not as a moral duty but as an emotionally fulfilling and stabilizing behavior. A spirit of reciprocity—doing for others what one would wish in return—becomes a strategic pathway to long-term social cohesion. Acts of kindness, sharing, and respect are not “morally required” but help establish the necessary emotional ties for survival. In this context, the natives could offer hospitality to the refugees, who in turn contribute their labor and skills toward the community’s wellbeing.

Over time, this compassionate exchange can build a shared sense of purpose and alleviate any residual mistrust, demonstrating that reciprocal gestures foster a sustainable society without reliance on an objective moral code. This approach recognizes that compassion, empathy, and mutual support are human strengths, aligning individual welfare with communal stability.

Conclusion: Compassion Without Absolutes

A moral non-realist approach to this refugee scenario illustrates how compassion and empathy can serve as a practical foundation for cooperation, without the need for moral objectivism. Compassion-driven pragmatism supports a balanced approach to resource sharing, respect for cultural autonomy, and reciprocity, all rooted in the shared goal of survival rather than adherence to moral absolutes. In the end, this compassionate response prioritizes well-being, demonstrating that human resilience lies not in objective moral claims but in our ability to connect, cooperate, and find value in one another’s existence. This perspective encourages both natives and refugees to seek peace and survival through shared understanding, adapting as they build a new, interwoven community.
