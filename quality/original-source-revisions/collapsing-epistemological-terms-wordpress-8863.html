  • belief
  • truth
  • fact
  • evidence
  • demonstration
  • proof
  • rationality
  • reasonable
  • credence
  • assent to
  • confidence

There are three ways we can approach the definition of terms in an attempt to arrive at a degree of denotative agreement adequate for a productive dialogue.

1: Demand that your interlocutor adopt your denotations.

This is the least likely way to accomplish your goal of a productive dialogue since it requires that your interlocutors has an accommodating personality and that you have the patience to educate them to employ terms in ways foreign to their default denotations.

2: Accommodate the denotations of your interlocutor.

This will require the following:

  • You learn the terms they assign to various epistemological concepts.
  • You assess their constellation of epistemological terms for internal logical coherence.
  • You engage in subsequent dialogues on epistemology with consistency in the way you employ terms.

Two problems with this method are 1) you must arrive at a rigorous understanding of your interlocutor’s denotation of every commonly used epistemological term that might enter the conversation, and 2) many of these terms have significant, intrinsic conceptual overlap, making equivocation and semantic creep very easy.

I propose a third method that may require a higher initial investment, but will yield far greater productivity in subsequent discussion.

Collapsing 2nd-Order Epistemological Concepts into 1st-Order Terms

To simplify and clarify epistemological discussions, several 2nd-order concepts can be redefined using more fundamental, 1st-order terms. Here are a few examples:

2nd-Order Concept: Knowledge
Collapsed Phrase: High credence in a proposition based on sufficient justification.

Rationale: Knowledge often implies a justified true belief. By redefining it as a high level of credence (belief strength) in a proposition that is well-supported by evidence and reasoning, we can make the term more precise and less ambiguous.

2. Belief

2nd-Order Concept: Belief
Collapsed Phrase: Credence assigned to a proposition.

Rationale: Belief can vary in strength and certainty. By expressing it in terms of credence, we explicitly denote the degree of confidence in the proposition, making the concept more quantifiable and clear.

3. Truth

2nd-Order Concept: Truth
Collapsed Phrase: A proposition with maximum credence based on coherence and correspondence with reality.

Rationale: Truth is often debated in terms of its nature and criteria. By defining it as a proposition that holds the highest possible credence due to its coherence with other beliefs and correspondence with observable reality, we provide a more operational and practical understanding of truth.

4. Fact

2nd-Order Concept: Fact
Collapsed Phrase: A proposition with high credence assigned by the majority of relevant experts.

Rationale: Facts are often accepted based on the consensus of experts in a field. By anchoring the concept of fact in expert credence, we emphasize the role of expert evaluation and agreement in establishing facts.

5. Evidence

2nd-Order Concept: Evidence
Collapsed Phrase: Information that increases the credence of a proposition.

Rationale: Evidence is used to support beliefs or propositions. Defining it as information that boosts the credence of a proposition highlights its function in the epistemic process and makes the term more functional.

6. Demonstration

2nd-Order Concept: Demonstration
Collapsed Phrase: A logical process that significantly increases credence in a proposition.

Rationale: Demonstration involves showing something to be true through logical reasoning or empirical evidence. By focusing on the process and its impact on credence, the term becomes clearer and more precise.

7. Proof

2nd-Order Concept: Proof
Collapsed Phrase: An argument or set of arguments that maximizes credence in a proposition within a given system.

Rationale: Proofs are definitive within specific logical or mathematical systems. By relating proof to the maximization of credence, we ground the concept in epistemic terms that reflect certainty within a defined context.

8. Rationality

2nd-Order Concept: Rationality
Collapsed Phrase: Consistency in assigning and updating credences based on evidence and logical principles.

Rationale: Rationality involves making decisions and forming beliefs based on logic and evidence. By framing it in terms of credence assignment and updating, we highlight the dynamic and evidence-responsive nature of rational thought.

9. Reasonable

2nd-Order Concept: Reasonable
Collapsed Phrase: Holding credences that are proportionate to the available evidence.

Rationale: Being reasonable means having beliefs that align with the evidence. By defining it in terms of proportional credence, we emphasize the balance and justification involved in reasonable thinking.

10. Assent to

2nd-Order Concept: Assent to
Collapsed Phrase: Assigning a significant credence to a proposition.

Rationale: Assenting to a proposition means accepting it as true. By relating it to the assignment of significant credence, we clarify the degree of belief involved in assent.

11. Confidence

2nd-Order Concept: Confidence
Collapsed Phrase: High credence in the accuracy or reliability of a proposition or source.

Rationale: Confidence often refers to trust in a proposition or its source. By defining it in terms of high credence, we provide a more measurable and precise understanding of confidence.

  9. Understanding: Understanding goes beyond simply knowing something is true. The depth and nature of comprehension can vary considerably.
  10. Fact: Similar to truth, the distinction between fact and opinion can be blurred. What constitutes a “fact” can depend on the context and available evidence.
