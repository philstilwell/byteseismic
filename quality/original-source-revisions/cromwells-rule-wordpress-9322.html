Cromwell’s Rule: An Elaboration

Cromwell’s Rule, named after the English statesman Oliver Cromwell and introduced by statistician Dennis Lindley, is a principle in Bayesian statistics. This rule advises against assigning a prior probability of exactly 0 or 1 to any event unless it is logically certain or impossible. Below is a detailed explanation of Cromwell’s Rule, its implications, and applications.

Understanding Cromwell’s Rule

In Bayesian statistics, prior probabilities represent our initial beliefs about the likelihood of events before considering new evidence. Cromwell’s Rule specifically addresses the dangers of assigning absolute certainty (1) or impossibility (0) to these priors.

  1. Prior Probabilities: These are the probabilities assigned to events before observing any new data. They reflect our initial state of belief or knowledge.
  2. Bayesian Updating: This is the process of adjusting our prior probabilities based on new evidence to obtain posterior probabilities.
Why Avoid 0 and 1?

Assigning a prior probability of 0 or 1 implies absolute certainty about an event. This can be problematic for several reasons:

  1. Inflexibility to New Evidence: If a prior probability is set to 0 or 1, no amount of new evidence can change this belief. This goes against the fundamental Bayesian principle of updating beliefs with evidence.
  2. Practical Considerations: In the real world, few events are absolutely certain or impossible. Allowing for some degree of uncertainty (i.e., probabilities between 0 and 1) is more practical and realistic.
  3. Logical Certainty: The only exceptions to this rule are statements that are logically true or false (e.g., mathematical truths like 2 + 2 = 4).
Implications of Cromwell’s Rule
  1. Flexibility in Belief Revision: By avoiding 0 or 1, we remain open to updating our beliefs in light of new evidence, fostering a more flexible and adaptive mindset.
  2. Avoiding Dogmatism: This rule helps prevent dogmatic thinking, where beliefs are held rigidly regardless of contrary evidence.
  3. Encouraging Rationality: It promotes rational decision-making by ensuring that our beliefs can be modified based on evidence, which is central to Bayesian reasoning.
Applications of Cromwell’s Rule
  1. Scientific Inquiry: Scientists often adopt Cromwell’s Rule to maintain an open-minded approach to hypotheses, allowing them to be updated or discarded based on experimental data.
  2. Risk Assessment: In fields like finance and engineering, where decisions are based on probabilistic models, avoiding absolute priors helps in making more robust and adaptable predictions.
  3. Decision Theory: In decision-making processes, especially under uncertainty, this rule ensures that decisions remain flexible and responsive to new information.
Examples
  1. Scientific Hypotheses: Suppose a scientist is testing a new hypothesis. Assigning a prior probability of 0 to the hypothesis would mean they are certain it is false, regardless of any evidence that may arise. By assigning a prior probability slightly above 0, the scientist remains open to considering evidence that might support the hypothesis.
  2. Medical Diagnosis: In a medical context, if a doctor assigned a prior probability of 0 to a rare disease for a patient, no amount of symptoms or test results suggesting the disease could change the diagnosis. By assigning a low but non-zero prior probability, the doctor stays open to adjusting the diagnosis based on new information.

Conclusion

Cromwell’s Rule is a critical principle in Bayesian statistics, emphasizing the importance of keeping prior probabilities between 0 and 1 unless dealing with logically certain statements. This rule encourages flexibility, rationality, and openness to new evidence, which are essential for effective decision-making and scientific inquiry.

Cromwell’s Rule in Statistics

Cromwell’s rule, named after statistician Dennis Lindley, is a principle in probability theory and decision analysis. It discourages assigning extreme probabilities of 1 (certain event) or 0 (impossible event) to outcomes, except in specific situations.

The Core Idea: Avoiding Overconfidence

The core idea behind Cromwell’s rule is to avoid overconfidence in our initial beliefs, also known as prior probabilities. Assigning a probability of 1 to an event suggests absolute certainty, while 0 implies impossibility. In most real-world scenarios, such certainties are rare.

Why Not Absolute Values?

There are two main reasons to avoid absolute probabilities:

  1. Limited Knowledge: Our knowledge about the world is often incomplete. New evidence or data can emerge, changing the likelihood of an event. Assigning a 0 probability excludes the possibility of encountering such evidence entirely.
  2. More Nuanced Inferences: By using probabilities between 0 and 1, we allow the data we collect to have a greater influence on our final conclusions (posterior probabilities). This leads to more flexible and adaptable inferences.

Exceptions: Logically True or False Statements

There are exceptions to Cromwell’s rule. As the quote mentions, assigning extreme probabilities is acceptable for logically true or false statements. For example, the probability that 2 + 2 equals 4 is undeniably 1, and the probability that it equals 5 is undeniably 0.

Cromwell’s Rule in Action

Imagine flipping a coin. You might initially believe it’s a fair coin (prior probability of heads = 0.5). But after three consecutive heads, you might start to suspect it’s biased. By avoiding extreme probabilities (0 or 1), Cromwell’s rule allows you to update your beliefs as you gather more information (the coin flips).

In conclusion, Cromwell’s rule encourages a more nuanced approach to probability judgements. By acknowledging the limitations of our knowledge and allowing for new information, we can make more reliable and robust inferences from data.

Cromwell’s Rule is a principle in Bayesian statistics that advises against assigning prior probabilities of exactly 0 or 1 to events or hypotheses, except in cases where the statement is logically true or false.

This is problematic because in most real-world situations, there is always some degree of uncertainty, and it is important to remain open to revising beliefs in light of new evidence.

Exceptions:
The rule makes an exception for statements that are logically true or false, such as mathematical identities (e.g., 2 + 2 = 4) or logical tautologies (e.g., “If it is raining, then it is raining”). In these cases, it is appropriate to assign a prior probability of 1 or 0, respectively, because the truth or falsity of the statement is known with absolute certainty.

Importance of the Rule:
Cromwell’s Rule is particularly relevant in the context of Bayesian inference, where prior probabilities are combined with likelihood functions to calculate posterior probabilities. If a prior probability of 0 or 1 is used, it can lead to inappropriate or misleading conclusions, as the posterior probability will always be 0 or 1, regardless of the evidence.

By avoiding the use of extreme prior probabilities (0 or 1) in most situations, Cromwell’s Rule helps maintain a degree of flexibility and openness to updating beliefs based on new data. This is essential for making rational and evidence-based decisions in situations where there is inherent uncertainty or incomplete information.

How Absolute Credence of 0 or 1 Entails Epistemic Blindness

Epistemic blindness refers to the inability or unwillingness to revise one’s beliefs in the face of new evidence. When we assign an absolute credence of 0 or 1 to an event or proposition, we essentially close ourselves off from any possibility of learning or updating our beliefs. Here, we explore how this practice leads to epistemic blindness and its implications.

Understanding Epistemic Blindness
  1. Definition: Epistemic blindness is a state where one is unable to see or consider alternative possibilities due to rigid belief structures.
  2. Consequences: This rigidity can lead to a failure to recognize new information, resulting in a static and potentially flawed understanding of the world.
The Role of Credence in Epistemic Processes

Credence refers to the degree of belief or probability that we assign to a proposition. In Bayesian terms, this is represented as a value between 0 and 1.

  1. Non-absolute Credence: A credence between 0 and 1 indicates a belief that is open to revision based on new evidence.
  2. Absolute Credence: A credence of exactly 0 or 1 signifies absolute certainty or impossibility, leaving no room for change.
Why Absolute Credence Leads to Epistemic Blindness
  1. Inflexibility to New Evidence:
    • Bayesian Updating: Bayesian reasoning relies on updating our beliefs in light of new evidence. If we start with a credence of 0 or 1, our belief system becomes impervious to change, as no amount of evidence can alter these extreme probabilities.
    • Learning from Evidence: When we assign a non-absolute credence, we acknowledge that our current understanding may be incomplete or flawed, and we remain open to learning from new evidence.
  2. Dogmatic Belief Systems:
    • Resisting Change: Absolute credence fosters dogmatism, where beliefs are held rigidly despite contradictory evidence. This resistance to change can lead to outdated or incorrect views.
    • Closed-mindedness: By refusing to consider alternative possibilities, individuals with absolute credence can become closed-minded, dismissing new information that could refine or correct their beliefs.
  3. Undermining Rational Decision-Making:
    • Rationality and Evidence: Rational decision-making involves weighing evidence and updating beliefs accordingly. Absolute credence undermines this process by precluding any consideration of new or conflicting evidence.
    • Error in Judgment: Decisions based on absolute credence can be flawed, as they ignore the probabilistic nature of most real-world events and the potential for error in our initial assessments.
Examples Illustrating Epistemic Blindness
  1. Scientific Rigor:
    • Hypothesis Testing: Scientists often begin with hypotheses that have non-absolute credence. This openness allows them to update their hypotheses based on experimental results. An absolute credence of 0 in a hypothesis would mean no evidence, however compelling, could ever support it.
    • Scientific Progress: Scientific advancements depend on the ability to revise theories. Epistemic blindness resulting from absolute credence stifles this progress.
  2. Medical Diagnosis:
    • Diagnostic Flexibility: Doctors must remain open to revising diagnoses based on patient symptoms and test results. Assigning a prior probability of 0 to a rare disease can lead to misdiagnosis, as no amount of evidence will prompt the doctor to consider that disease.
    • Patient Outcomes: Flexible credence ensures better patient outcomes by allowing doctors to consider and incorporate new evidence into their diagnoses.
  3. Everyday Decision-Making:
    • Risk Assessment: When assessing risks, individuals benefit from maintaining flexible beliefs. An absolute credence of 1 in the safety of a particular activity, despite new evidence suggesting otherwise, can lead to dangerous outcomes.
    • Financial Decisions: Investors who assign absolute credence to market trends without considering new data risk significant financial loss. Keeping a non-absolute credence allows for adaptive strategies based on emerging market conditions.

Assigning an absolute credence of 0 or 1 to any proposition leads to epistemic blindness by rendering our belief systems inflexible and closed to new evidence. This rigidity undermines rational decision-making, fosters dogmatic thinking, and hampers our ability to learn and adapt. By maintaining non-absolute credences, we preserve the capacity to update our beliefs and make more informed, rational decisions.
