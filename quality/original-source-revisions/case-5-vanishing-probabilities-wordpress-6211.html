A grade school teacher with 33 students claims a ghost is the best explanation for the disappearance of an apple from the teacher’s desk. Assess the teacher’s rationale below.

The probability of any one of the 33 students taking the apple is an insignificant 3%. Therefore none of the students took the apple. Therefore we should default to a ghost being the most likely explanation for the disappearance of the apple from the teacher’s desk until someone proves otherwise.

The teacher’s rationale for concluding that a ghost took the apple is problematic for several reasons. Here is an assessment of the argument provided:

  1. Misuse of Probability: The teacher states that the probability of any one student taking the apple is 3%, which is calculated as ( \frac{1}{33} \approx 0.03 ) or 3%. However, this calculation is misinterpreted:
  • The 3% figure reflects the probability of a specific student taking the apple, assuming there is no reason to suspect any one student over another.
  • The proper question to ask is: “What is the probability that at least one of the 33 students took the apple?” This is calculated as the complement of the probability that none of the students took the apple, which is ( 1 – (0.97)^{33} \approx 0.64 ) or 64%. This indicates a much higher likelihood that at least one student took the apple.
  1. Logical Fallacy: The teacher’s conclusion that because individual probabilities are low, “none of the students took the apple” is a logical fallacy known as the fallacy of division. This fallacy involves assuming that something true of a thing must also be true of all or some of its parts.
  2. Assumption of Ghost Existence: The teacher assumes the existence of ghosts as a more likely explanation without sufficient basis:
  • The premise “It is improbable that all of the sightings of ghosts are made up” is not substantiated. The existence of multiple reports of ghosts does not necessarily validate the existence of ghosts; multiple factors including cultural influences, optical illusions, and other psychological factors could explain sightings.
  • The argument that ghosts are the most likely explanation until proven otherwise is an example of shifting the burden of proof, a logical fallacy where one claims something is true because it has not yet been proven false.
  1. Default Position: The default position in rational inquiry should be skepticism and reliance on evidence. The absence of evidence for the involvement of the students (or lack of evidence pointing specifically to any one student) does not automatically validate an alternative supernatural explanation.
  2. More Likely Explanations: There are more plausible explanations that should be considered before supernatural ones:
  • Misplacement or misremembering by the teacher or another staff member.
  • A student, or group of students, taking the apple without the teacher’s notice.
  • Even if the probability of each student individually taking the apple is low, the combined probability is much higher.

In conclusion, the teacher’s rationale for attributing the disappearance of the apple to a ghost is not based on a sound understanding of probability or logical reasoning. Alternative explanations grounded in common everyday occurrences are much more likely and should be considered first.

  • A student took the apple: 60%

Step 3: Update Credences Based on Further Evidence

As new information becomes available, the teacher should update these credences. For instance:

  • If a student is seen eating an apple shortly after the disappearance, increase the credence for “A student took the apple”.
  • If the teacher recalls placing the apple in a different spot or finds the apple elsewhere, increase the credence for “The teacher misplaced the apple”.
  • If cleaning schedules or other staff activities around the desk are confirmed, adjust the credences for those scenarios.

Step 4: Consider the Rational Basis for Each Explanation

  1. Rational basis for human actions: Theft, misplacement, and accidents are common and mundane explanations that require no additional assumptions beyond known human behaviors and mistakes.
  2. Rational basis for supernatural claims: Requires significant assumption that goes beyond established scientific understanding. It should not be favored without compelling, specific evidence.

Conclusion

A rational approach involves:

  • Using a probabilistic framework to weigh the likelihood of various scenarios.
  • Being ready to update beliefs as new evidence comes in, using principles of Bayesian reasoning.

Pedagogical Explanation of the Flawed Rationale

Understanding Probability

The major flaw in the asserted explanation for the disappearance of the vehicle is a fundamental misunderstanding of probability and statistical reasoning. The probability of each conventional scenario is low individually but when considered collectively, they substantially increase the likelihood that the disappearance is due to a mundane reason.

Law of Total Probability

The law of total probability states that the total probability of an event occurring can be found by considering all mutually exclusive ways in which it can occur. In this scenario, the ways the car could have vanished (theft, borrowing, towing, repossession) are not overlapping and together form a more comprehensive explanation. The mathematical formulation would be:

[ P(\text{Car Missing}) = P(\text{Theft}) + P(\text{Borrowed}) + P(\text{Towed}) + P(\text{Repossessed}) ]

Thus:
[ P(\text{Car Missing}) = 10\% + 5\% + 5\% + 4\% = 24\% ]

Dismissing High Probability Events

By focusing on a dimensional shift—an event with a 1% probability based on speculative fiction rather than empirical evidence—the owner irrationally dismisses more probable and mundane explanations. This is an example of the psychological bias known as the availability heuristic, where individuals overestimate the importance of information that is more readily available to them (in this case, influenced by recent science fiction media).

Bayes’ Theorem and Updating Beliefs

Rational thinking would require updating beliefs based on evidence using Bayes’ Theorem:

[ P(H|E) = \frac{P(E|H) \times P(H)}{P(E)} ]

Where:

  • ( H ) is the hypothesis (e.g., the car was stolen).
  • ( E ) is new evidence (e.g., the car is missing).
  • ( P(H|E) ) is the probability of the hypothesis given the evidence.

Without specific evidence supporting the dimensional shift, its probability remains low compared to other hypotheses.

Practical Implications

Believing in the low-probability explanation without empirical evidence can lead to inappropriate actions or lack of action:

  • Failure to Investigate: Dismissing high-probability scenarios might prevent the owner from taking practical steps like checking with relatives, contacting the police, or verifying with the bank or city towing records.
  • Resource Misallocation: Resources might be wasted on seeking unlikely explanations (e.g., consulting fiction-based theories) rather than addressing plausible causes.

Conclusion

The pedagogical takeaway is the importance of applying rational, probabilistic reasoning to everyday problems. Understanding how to aggregate probabilities of independent, non-mutually exclusive events and using appropriate statistical reasoning (like Bayes’ Theorem for updating beliefs) is crucial in making informed decisions. This scenario underscores the need for skepticism and empirical validation when confronting unusual or unexpected situations. This approach prevents the fallacy of favoring sensational but implausible explanations over more probable mundane ones.

The Flawed Allure of the Improbable: Analyzing Scenario 1 – The Vanishing Vehicle

Let’s dissect the flawed reasoning in Scenario 1: The Vanishing Vehicle. Here’s why the owner’s fixation on the “dimensional shift” theory is illogical:

  1. Probability vs. Possibility: While the sci-fi explanation (1%) is technically possible, it’s incredibly improbable. There’s no scientific evidence to support spontaneous dimensional shifts in everyday life. The combined probability of the listed conventional scenarios (24%) is significantly higher, making them far more likely explanations.
  2. Occam’s Razor: This principle of parsimony suggests that in the absence of compelling evidence, the simplest explanation is usually the most likely. Stealing, borrowing, mistaken towing, or repossession are all well-understood phenomena with established patterns, making them far simpler explanations than a dimensional shift requiring exotic physics.
  3. Confirmation Bias: The owner might be experiencing confirmation bias. Their recent exposure to the sci-fi movie might make them subconsciously seek out evidence to fit that narrative. They might misinterpret details (e.g., no signs of forced entry) to support the dimensional shift theory, overlooking simpler explanations.
  4. Lack of Evidence: The sci-fi explanation lacks any supporting evidence. There’s no indication of futuristic technology or displaced objects, unlike the potential evidence associated with the conventional explanations (e.g., broken window for theft, witness for borrowing).

Here’s a more logical approach:

  • Gather Information: The owner should first investigate the more likely scenarios. This might involve checking with relatives, contacting the police or towing company, and verifying details with the bank.
  • Evaluate Evidence: Any evidence found (e.g., security camera footage, witness accounts) should be used to refine the possibilities.
  • Seek Expert Opinion: If no clear explanation emerges, consulting a mechanic or law enforcement might be necessary.

By focusing on evidence-based explanations and employing a systematic approach, the owner can arrive at a more rational conclusion than resorting to a highly improbable sci-fi scenario.
