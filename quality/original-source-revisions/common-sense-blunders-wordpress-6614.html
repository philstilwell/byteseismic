Provide 5 cases in different domains of knowledge in which “common sense” ran counter to truth.

Certainly! Here are five examples from different domains where common sense beliefs have been contradicted by scientific or empirical evidence:

  1. Astronomy:
    Common Sense: Before the scientific era, common sense dictated that the Earth was the center of the universe because it appears that the sun, stars, and planets revolve around the Earth.
    Truth: The heliocentric model proposed by Copernicus and later refined by Galileo and Kepler, established that the Earth and other planets orbit the sun, contradicting the geocentric view that was intuitive to most observers.
  2. Health and Medicine:
    Common Sense: Common sense might suggest that exposing yourself to cold weather can cause a cold.
    Truth: Colds are caused by viruses, not cold weather. While cold weather might correlate with an increase in colds, it’s primarily because people spend more time indoors in close contact with others who can transmit viruses.
  3. Economics:
    Common Sense: A common sense approach might lead one to think that during economic downturns, austerity measures (cutting public spending) are necessary to save money and recover the economy.
    Truth: Economic studies and historical data suggest that austerity can actually exacerbate economic problems by reducing demand and slowing down recovery. Economists argue for increased spending (stimulus) during downturns to boost economic activities.
  4. Physics:
    Common Sense: Common sense may tell us that heavier objects fall faster than lighter ones because they appear more “forceful” when dropped.
    Truth: Galileo’s experiments demonstrated that in the absence of air resistance, all objects fall at the same rate regardless of their mass. This was a revolutionary idea contrary to the then-common Aristotelian belief.
  5. Environmental Science:
    Common Sense: It might seem logical to assume that paper bags are more environmentally friendly than plastic bags because they are made from natural materials and are biodegradable.
    Truth: Life cycle assessments show that paper bags require more energy and water to produce, contribute significantly to air and water pollution, and have a larger carbon footprint than plastic bags when considering the entire lifecycle of the product.

These examples illustrate how intuitive or “common sense” views can often be misleading and how empirical evidence and scientific inquiry are essential for uncovering the truth.

List the common sources of common sense errors.

Common sense errors often arise from cognitive biases, flawed reasoning processes, or limited perception. Here are some of the typical sources that contribute to these errors, including legitimate inductive inference, errors in probability assessments, and emotional salience:

  1. Heuristic Overreliance:
    • Definition: Heuristics are mental shortcuts that help people make quick decisions. While often useful, they can lead to systematic errors.
    • Impact: People might overly rely on simple rules of thumb such as “like goes with like” or “if it happened before, it will happen again,” which can oversimplify complex decisions and lead to erroneous conclusions.
  2. Legitimate Inductive Inference:
    • Definition: Inductive reasoning involves making generalizations based on specific observations. While a legitimate method of reasoning, it can lead to errors when the observed cases are not representative of the general case.
    • Impact: Common sense might dictate that what is true for several observed instances will be true for all similar instances, which can be misleading if the sample is biased or too small.
  3. Errors in Probability Assessment:
    • Definition: Humans often struggle with assessing probabilities accurately, especially in complex situations.
    • Impact: Common errors include the gambler’s fallacy (believing past events affect the probability of something happening in the future), misunderstanding the law of large numbers, and ignoring base rate information (failing to consider prior probability of an event).
  4. Confirmation Bias:
    • Definition: The tendency to search for, interpret, favor, and recall information in a way that confirms one’s preexisting beliefs or hypotheses.
    • Impact: This can lead individuals to give more weight to evidence that confirms their existing beliefs and disregard information that contradicts them, perpetuating common sense errors.
  5. Emotional Salience:
    • Definition: Emotionally significant events or information are more likely to be remembered and considered than those that are neutral.
    • Impact: This can skew reasoning processes, making people overestimate the likelihood of dramatic or emotionally charged outcomes (e.g., plane crashes) while underestimating more mundane risks (e.g., car accidents).
  6. Anchoring Effect:
    • Definition: People tend to rely too heavily on the first piece of information they hear (the “anchor”) when making decisions.
    • Impact: Once an anchor is set, subsequent judgments are made by adjusting away from that anchor, and there is a bias toward interpreting other information around the anchor.
  7. Availability Heuristic:
    • Definition: People judge the probability of events by the ease with which examples come to mind.
    • Impact: If a particular type of event has been widely reported in the media, people may believe it is more common than it actually is.
  8. Status Quo Bias:
    • Definition: The preference to keep things the same or maintaining a previous decision.
    • Impact: This can lead to common sense errors where individuals assume that things should stay the same because they have always been that way, often ignoring new evidence or changing conditions that warrant a different approach.

These sources highlight the variety of ways in which common sense can lead to incorrect judgments or decisions, emphasizing the importance of critical thinking and empirical validation in various domains.

However, it can lead to errors if the observations are not representative of the whole population or if there are other factors that are not being considered. For example, a child might observe that every time it rains, the puddles get bigger. They might then infer that the rain makes the puddles bigger. However, this is not always the case. Rain can also evaporate or soak into the ground.
  • Errors in probability assessments: Overestimating or underestimating the likelihood of events. This can be caused by a number of factors, including our own personal experiences, the media we consume, and the way that information is presented to us. For example, people are more likely to be afraid of flying than they are of being struck by lightning, even though lightning is actually more likely. This is because plane crashes are more widely reported in the media than lightning strikes.
  • Emotional salience: Placing greater weight on information that evokes strong emotions. This can lead us to believe things that are not true, or to make decisions that are not in our best interests. For example, people are more likely to believe information that is presented in a fear-inducing way. This is because emotions can hijack our rational thinking.
  • Confirmation bias: We tend to seek out information that confirms our existing beliefs and ignore information that contradicts them. This can lead us to cling to false ideas even when presented with evidence to the contrary.
  • Availability bias: We judge the likelihood of an event based on how easily we can recall examples of it. This can be misleading, as some events are simply more memorable than others, regardless of their actual frequency.
  • Cultural bias: Our cultural background shapes our understanding of the world and can lead to misunderstandings. What seems like common sense in one culture might not be true in another.
  • Language limitations: Language can sometimes restrict how we think about the world. For example, the phrase “common sense” itself implies that there’s a universally agreed-upon set of truths, which isn’t always the case.

Provide a cognitive process we can use to ensure what we perceive as commonsensical is actually true.

To ensure that what we perceive as commonsensical is actually true, it’s important to implement a rigorous cognitive process that can critically assess and validate our assumptions. Here’s a structured approach you can use:

1. Question Initial Assumptions

  • Reflect: Begin by questioning the assumptions underlying your commonsense belief. Ask yourself what assumptions are being made and why do you believe them to be true?
  • Challenge: Consider alternative perspectives or scenarios. What evidence supports these assumptions? Could the opposite be true?

2. Seek Diverse Perspectives

  • Consult: Talk to people with different backgrounds, educations, and life experiences to gain alternative viewpoints.
  • Read: Look for credible sources that challenge or support your belief. Scholarly articles, reputable news sources, and expert opinions can provide insights that differ from initial assumptions.

3. Use Critical Thinking Techniques

  • Apply Logical Reasoning: Use formal logic to test whether the belief logically follows from the initial assumptions. Identify any logical fallacies or biases that might affect reasoning.
  • Test Hypotheses: Formulate hypotheses based on your commonsense belief and think of ways you could empirically test them.

4. Employ Scientific Methods

  • Experimentation: Where possible, design and conduct experiments to test the validity of your commonsense belief. Controlled experiments can provide concrete evidence about the truth of a belief.
  • Observation and Data Analysis: Collect data through observation or existing sources. Use statistical methods to analyze the data to avoid biases in how the data is interpreted.

5. Consider Probabilities and Alternatives

  • Assess Probabilities: Use probability assessments to evaluate the likelihood of different outcomes or the truth of various statements. This helps in understanding the risks associated with certain beliefs.
  • Think in Terms of Risk and Reward: Evaluate the potential risks and rewards of a belief being true or false. This can provide practical reasons for whether or not to hold onto a belief.

6. Integrate Feedback Loops

  • Iterative Feedback: Regularly update your beliefs based on new evidence or feedback from the environment. This iterative process is crucial in adapting to new information or contexts.
  • Error Correction: Be open to recognizing and correcting errors in your thinking process. Acknowledge when a belief is disproven and adjust your views accordingly.

7. Use Metacognitive Strategies

  • Reflect on Thinking Process: Regularly reflect on your own thinking process. Consider whether you are being unduly influenced by emotions or biases.
  • Engage in Mindfulness and Awareness Practices: Mindfulness can increase your awareness of your thought processes and help mitigate the impact of biases on your beliefs.

Conclusion

By applying these cognitive and methodological tools, you can more reliably verify whether commonsensical beliefs are actually true. This approach not only helps in challenging potentially flawed intuitions but also promotes a more thoughtful, informed, and rational understanding of the world.

Here’s a cognitive process you can use to challenge common sense assumptions and get closer to the truth:

  1. Question your initial assumptions: When you encounter something that seems like common sense, take a moment to acknowledge it as an assumption, not a proven fact.
  2. Seek evidence: Look for data, research, and expert opinions on the topic. Reliable sources like scientific studies, peer-reviewed journals, and reputable news outlets can offer evidence to support or refute your initial belief.
  3. Consider alternative explanations: Don’t stop at the first piece of evidence that confirms your initial thought. Actively seek out explanations that contradict your common sense idea. This helps avoid confirmation bias.
  4. Evaluate the source: Critically analyze the information you find. Is the source credible? Is there a potential bias present?
  5. Context matters: Consider the specific context of the situation. What might be true in one scenario might not hold up in another.
  6. Be open to updating your beliefs: New information and evidence can change what we know about the world. Be prepared to adjust your beliefs based on what you learn.

Remember: This process takes time and effort. But by actively questioning common sense and seeking evidence, you can develop a more nuanced and accurate understanding of the world.
