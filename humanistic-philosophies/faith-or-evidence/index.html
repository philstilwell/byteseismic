Composite Response

Prompt 1: Is there not an intrinsic logical tension or incoherency between 1) citing miracles as reasons to believe in a God and, at the same time, 2) pointing to faith as reason to believe in that God without such evidence?

Is there a tension between miracles and faith as reasons to believe?

Empirical Dependency By appealing to miracles as evidence, one acknowledges a dependency on empirical validation for belief in God. This approach suggests that the truth of religious claims can be tested and confirmed through observable phenomena.

Faith Dependency By emphasizing faith, one endorses a non-empirical basis for belief, implying that religious truths are beyond the purview of empirical scrutiny and should be accepted without observable evidence.

Complementarity Some argue that faith and reason (including empirical evidence from miracles) are complementary rather than contradictory. Miracles might strengthen faith or serve as a sign for those who are already believers, rather than as foundational proof of God’s existence.

Different Contexts for Different Audiences Another perspective is that miracles serve to confirm faith for those who are already believers and to challenge the skepticism of non-believers, thereby functioning differently depending on one’s prior stance towards belief.

Developmental View of Faith Some theologians suggest that faith may initially require empirical evidence (like miracles) but should mature into a trust that transcends the need for such evidence.

Faith vs. Evidence Faith is often described as belief without concrete evidence. Miracles, on the other hand, are presented as exceptions to natural laws, offering evidence for God’s existence.

Verifying Miracles A key challenge is verifying miracles. Events can be misperceived, have natural explanations, or be based on unreliable sources.

Miracles Bolster Faith Some believers see miracles as confirmations of their existing faith, not the sole reason for it. They strengthen a belief already held on other grounds.

Faith as Foundation Another approach is to see faith as the foundation for even considering miracles. If you don’t believe a powerful being exists, then why would you even look for miracles?

Different Types of Faith Some distinguish between believing in God based on personal experiences or a sense of the divine and believing based on miracles reported by others.

  1. Intrinsic Tension in Religious Belief: There is indeed a noted tension in religious epistemology concerning the use of miracles as evidence for the existence of a God while simultaneously advocating for faith as a means to believe absent empirical evidence.
  2. Miracles as Evidence: Miracles are often cited as extraordinary events that defy natural explanations, suggesting divine intervention.
  3. Faith as Intrinsic Belief: On the other hand, faith is typically understood as belief that does not rely on empirical evidence.
  4. The Tension Explained: The tension arises when these two approaches are used simultaneously or interchangeably in arguing for the existence of God.
  5. Philosophical and Theological Responses: Theologians and philosophers have proposed various solutions to reconcile these aspects.

A syllogism exposing the tension between miracles and faith

Premise 1 (P1) If faith alone is sufficient for belief in God, then no additional evidence is necessary to sustain this belief.

Premise 2 (P2) Miracles are cited as necessary evidence for belief in God.

Conclusion (C) Therefore, if faith alone is sufficient, citing miracles as necessary contradicts the sufficiency of faith (making the necessity of miracles redundant).

Premise 1 (P1) If miracles are necessary to substantiate belief in God, then belief in God depends on empirical evidence.

Premise 2 (P2) Faith is defined as belief in God without dependence on empirical evidence.

Conclusion (C) Therefore, if miracles are necessary, it undermines the concept that faith alone is a valid or necessary path to religious truth (contradicting the non-empirical nature of faith).

Premise 1 If faith is sufficient for belief in God, then evidence (miracles) is not necessary for such belief.

Premise 2 Miracles are presented as evidence for the existence of God.

Major Premise If faith is sufficient for belief in God, then miracles as evidence are unnecessary. Minor Premise: Miracles are presented as necessary evidence for belief in God. Conclusion: Therefore, faith cannot be sufficient for belief in God.

Major Premise If miracles are necessary evidence for belief in God, then faith alone is not sufficient. Minor Premise: Faith alone is claimed to be sufficient for belief in God. Conclusion: Therefore, miracles cannot be necessary evidence for belief in God.

The syllogisms highlight the contradiction between the two claims 1) Faith alone is sufficient for belief in God 2) Miracles are necessary evidence required for belief in God

Prompt 3: If it is more virtuous in the mind of God for humans to strongly believe in his existence based on weak evidence, we can logically conclude that God would not want to provide humans with indisputable miracles as evidence of his existence, right?

If faith without strong evidence is more virtuous, should miracles disappear?

Premise 1 (P1) If believing in God based on weak evidence is more virtuous, then God values faith that does not rely on strong evidence.

Premise 2 (P2) Indisputable miracles constitute strong evidence of God’s existence.

Conclusion (C) Therefore, if it is more virtuous to believe based on weak evidence, God would not provide indisputable miracles as evidence of his existence.

Value of Different Kinds of Faith Maybe God values different kinds of faith. Perhaps unwavering faith despite weak evidence is valued, but so is a willingness to seek truth through reason and evidence.

Nature of Miracles Maybe God doesn’t see miracles as removing free will or genuine belief. Perhaps they nudge someone towards considering the possibility of God.

Mystery and Revelation Some believe God intentionally leaves things mysterious, allowing for faith and personal exploration.

Premise 1 If strong belief based on weak evidence is more virtuous in God’s eyes (This is your assumption),

Premise 2 Providing indisputable miracles would remove the need for strong belief based on weak evidence,

Note This syllogism is logically valid, but its conclusion relies on the debatable premise that God prioritizes a specific type of faith.

Major Premise If it is more virtuous to strongly believe in God’s existence based on weak evidence, then God would not want to provide indisputable evidence of His existence.

Minor Premise It is more virtuous to strongly believe in God’s existence based on weak evidence.

  1. Syllogism on the Virtue of Belief Based on Weak Evidence: If it’s considered more virtuous to believe in God based on weak evidence, then it follows that providing strong or indisputable evidence could undermine this virtue.
  2. Syllogism: The Virtue of Belief with Weak Evidence: This syllogism aligns with the theological perspective that the virtue of faith is heightened by the absence of clear, empirical proof, encouraging a deeper, more personal commitment to belief that transcends visible or tangible confirmation.
