Commentary on the Coherency of Bertrand Russell’s Quote

Bertrand Russell’s Quote: “Where there is evidence, no one speaks of ‘faith.’ We do not speak of faith that two and two are four or that the earth is round. We only speak of faith when we wish to substitute emotion for evidence.”

Coherency Analysis:

  1. Distinction Between Evidence and Faith:
    • Russell draws a clear distinction between evidence and faith. He suggests that evidence is used to justify beliefs about well-established facts, such as mathematical truths (“two and two are four”) or empirical observations (“the earth is round”).
    • Russell uses faith here for conviction unsupported by evidence. Other uses denote trust or commitment, which may have evidential support; the definition should not silently shift between these senses.
  2. Logical Structure:
    • The passage proposes a contrast and a generalization about usage. It does not establish a cause-and-effect relationship or prove that every use of “faith” replaces evidence with emotion.
  3. Use of Examples:
    • The examples distinguish elementary deduction from empirical knowledge. Two clear examples cannot establish a universal claim about how people use a word.
  4. Philosophical Implication:
    • The quote implies a critique of faith as a basis for belief, suggesting that it is an emotional response rather than a rational one. This reflects Russell’s broader philosophy that values reason and empirical evidence over emotional or irrational beliefs.
  5. Rhetorical Effectiveness:
    • The quote is rhetorically effective because it is concise and memorable. The juxtaposition of evidence and faith in such stark terms underscores the importance Russell places on rationality and critical thinking.

The passage coherently expresses a criticism of conviction insulated from evidence. Its universal wording is too broad as a description of ordinary language, where “faith in a friend” can reflect a record of reliability.

The passage is rhetorically coherent, but evidence does not eliminate belief: a person can believe something because the evidence supports it. Whether “faith” is appropriate then depends on the intended sense.

In other words, faith becomes a factor when objective proof is lacking, and one relies more on subjective feelings or desired conclusions instead of verifiable reality.

A useful critique targets substituting conviction for adequate reasons. It should not conflate all trust, hope or religious commitment with one epistemic practice.

Suggested Terms for Epistemic Contexts

To avoid semantic confusion in epistemic contexts, it is important to use terms that clearly distinguish between beliefs based on evidence and those not supported by evidence. Here are some rigorously denoted terms to use in different contexts:

1. Contexts with Accompanying Evidence

Evidence-Based Belief:

  • Definition: A belief that is supported by empirical data or logical reasoning.
  • Examples: Scientific theories, historical facts, and medical diagnoses.
  • Usage: “The theory of evolution is an evidence-based belief supported by extensive fossil records and genetic research.”

Justified Belief:

  • Definition: A belief that is logically or empirically supported and has been subjected to critical scrutiny.
  • Examples: Legal conclusions drawn from forensic evidence, economic predictions based on data analysis.
  • Usage: “The evidence supports this conclusion, subject to its reliability and possible alternatives.” The mere existence of forensic evidence does not establish that a particular verdict is justified.

Rational Belief:

  • Definition: A belief that is consistent with reason and logic.
  • Examples: Mathematical proofs, well-supported scientific hypotheses.
  • Usage: “It is rational to believe in the laws of physics as they have consistently been validated through experiments.”

Knowledge:

  • Knowledge: ordinarily entails truth, unlike merely strong conviction. Justified true belief is a traditional analysis with well-known counterexamples, not an uncontested definition.
  • Examples: Scientific knowledge, verified historical events.
  • Usage: “Knowledge of gravity allows us to predict the behavior of objects under free fall.”
2. Contexts Without Accompanying Evidence

Faith:

  • Faith: specify the sense—trust, commitment or conviction beyond adequate evidence—rather than assigning one definition to every use.
  • Examples: Religious beliefs, personal convictions without scientific backing.
  • Usage: “Faith in a higher power often provides comfort to individuals despite the lack of empirical evidence.”

Speculation:

  • Speculation: a conjecture whose support is limited or uncertain; it need not have literally no evidence.
  • Examples: Theories about the existence of extraterrestrial life without direct evidence.
  • Usage: “The idea that there are intelligent civilizations in distant galaxies remains speculation at this point.”

Assumption:

  • Assumption: a proposition taken as a starting point for reasoning. It may be supported, idealized or adopted only conditionally.
  • Examples: Assumptions in mathematical models, initial hypotheses in scientific research.
  • Usage: “The model operates under the assumption that the market will continue to grow at a steady rate.”

Supposition:

  • Definition: An uncertain belief or hypothesis that is proposed without solid evidence.
  • Examples: Early theories in a scientific investigation, untested ideas.
  • Usage: “The initial supposition was that the disease was caused by a virus, which was later confirmed through research.”

Credence:

  • Credence: degree of belief, often represented probabilistically. It can be rational or poorly calibrated and can concern sparse as well as abundant evidence.
  • Example: assigning 0.6 credence to a hypothesis, then explaining the evidence, assumptions and uncertainty supporting that assignment.
  • Usage: “While the witness’s account was given credence, further evidence was needed for a conclusive judgment.”
Conclusion

These terms mark different dimensions: strength of belief, evidential support, truth and role in reasoning. They should not be treated as mutually exclusive bins.

  • Oxford English Dictionary: Definitions of faith, speculation, assumption, supposition, credence

Claude response · editorial edition

To avoid semantic confusion when discussing epistemic contexts involving evidence or lack thereof, I would suggest using more rigorously denoted terms as follows:

When Evidence is Present:

Instead of “faith”, use:

  • Evidential belief – A belief substantiated by available empirical evidence, though the evidence may be incomplete or open to interpretation.
  • Reasoned confidence – Confidence derived from logical reasoning applied to the existing evidence.
  • Theoretical acceptance – Accepting a theory or explanation as the currently best-supported based on the cumulative evidence.

When Little or No Evidence is Present:

Instead of “faith”, use:

  • Assumption: a starting point adopted for an argument or model, with or without independent support.
  • Conjecture – An opinion or conclusion formed on the basis of incomplete information.
  • Hypothesis – A tentative theoretical principle posited for further investigation.
  • Speculation: exploratory conjecture with limited support, not necessarily a total absence of evidence.
  • Creed – A set of fundamental beliefs or guiding principles adopted firmly (usually in a religious context).

By using these more precisely defined terms, we can distinguish between beliefs grounded in available evidence versus those formed in the absence of evidence.

Additionally, the terms “trust” or “credence” could apply when relying on testimony, authority, or established systems of knowledge that one has evaluated as trustworthy, even if one cannot directly verify all underlying evidence.

This terminology allows for nuanced discussions acknowledging both the roles of evidence and reason, as well as philosophical/existential beliefs that transcend what can be empirically proven. It avoids conflating disparate epistemic contexts under the ambiguous umbrella of “faith.”
