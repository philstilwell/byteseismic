Steel-Man Argument

The strongest version is a comparative evidential argument: these teachings would be surprising under a narrowly self-serving fabrication hypothesis. To favor divine origin, the leader must also explain why they are more expected under that hypothesis and consider other human-origin accounts.

  1. Counterintuitive core: distinguish difficulty understanding a doctrine from an actual contradiction. A counterintuitive belief might be unlikely under one simple wish-fulfillment account, but humans can invent counterintuitive or inconsistent ideas.
  2. Demanding ethics: costly obligations may count against the hypothesis that a founder sought only immediate comfort for every adherent. They do not exclude ideals, discipline, group loyalty or rewards deferred to an afterlife.
  3. Humility and cleansing: these challenge pride but can satisfy desires for renewal, acceptance or moral improvement. A stronger argument must assess the whole motivational package.
  4. Servant leadership: this can challenge domination while appealing to cooperation and the wish for trustworthy leaders. Human authorship is not ruled out by a demanding ideal.
  5. Counterintuitive teachings: their evidential force depends on an explicit model of human motives. Comfort is one motive among others, not a complete theory of invention.
  6. Comparison with rivals: the excerpt’s caricature of other religions is not established evidence. Apply the same criteria to demanding and permissive features in each tradition.
Appeal of “Unpalatable” Religion

Despite its challenging features, humans find such religions appealing for several reasons:

  1. Sense of Higher Purpose: The difficulty and counterintuitive nature of the religion suggest a higher purpose beyond human comprehension, offering a sense of connection to the divine and the transcendent.
  2. Moral and Ethical Guidance: Austere ethical systems provide clear guidelines for behavior, helping adherents lead disciplined and purposeful lives. This structure can be appealing to those seeking direction and meaning.
  3. Community and Belonging: Religions often foster strong community bonds among followers who share and support each other through the challenges of adhering to demanding teachings.
  4. Psychological Comfort: The humility and submission required by such religions can offer psychological comfort, as they encourage followers to let go of their ego and trust in a higher power, reducing anxiety and stress.
  5. Transformative Potential: The rigorous practices and high standards can lead to personal transformation, fostering growth, resilience, and a deeper sense of fulfillment.
  6. Perceived credibility: costly commitment can make sincerity seem more credible, but sincerity and supernatural truth are different. A sincere believer can be mistaken.

Conclusion

Demanding teachings can challenge a narrow account of opportunistic invention while remaining understandable through ordinary human motives. Their appeal does not authenticate divine origin, and explaining their appeal does not by itself disprove that origin.

  • Historical Context: Many religions emerged during times of hardship or societal upheaval. Strict codes and rituals might have been seen as crucial for survival or social cohesion, leading to religions that appear more austere.

Problems with the “Austerity = Divinity” Argument:

  • Appeal of Asceticism: There’s a long history of asceticism in various religions. People willingly embrace hardship and self-denial as a path to spiritual growth or divine connection. A religion could be deliberately created around such practices.
  • Control and Power: Religion can be used for control and power. A leader might create a deliberately restrictive religion to maintain control over followers or solidify their own authority. Difficulty could be seen as a test of loyalty or a way to limit dissent.
  • Cultural Bias: The leader’s view might be culturally specific. Many cultures value duty, sacrifice, and adherence to tradition, which could readily lead to a religion with stricter moral codes.

Alternative Explanations for Austerity:

  • Evolutionary Process: Many religions evolve over time. Beliefs and practices can become more or less strict through interpretation, reform, and adaptation to changing circumstances.
  • Focus on Different Aspects: Religions might emphasize different aspects of the human experience. A religion that appears more restrictive might simply prioritize different values compared to the leader’s religion.

Conclusion:

The leader’s argument that austerity points to divine origin is an oversimplification. Human motivations for creating and following religion are complex. A religion’s perceived strictness can be influenced by historical context, cultural values, and individual needs. While the leader raises interesting points, it’s important to consider the broader range of factors that shape religious beliefs and practices.

Here is my comprehensive critique of the underlying logic in the religious leader’s statements:

Unsupported generalization: humans can formulate demanding ideals in secular and religious settings. This challenges the premise that a human-origin belief system must minimize constraint without assuming in advance that every religion is fabricated.

Selection effects: an inference from surviving religions would need a comparison with failed or forgotten movements. The excerpt alone does not establish survivorship bias, and the traits of unobserved movements should not be invented.

Possible circularity: if the account of human nature is defended solely through the religion whose origin is at issue, support could be circular. That dependency is not established by the excerpt. Its evident weakness is the unsupported account of motivation.

Subjective Interpretation
What qualifies as “austere,” “counterintuitive” or “unpalatable” is highly subjective and culturally relative. Practices considered normal or even liberating in one context could be viewed as extremely harsh by an outsider’s perspective. The leader frames these qualities through their own parochial lens.

Possible incentives: authority or group identity could favor this rhetoric, but that does not establish the speaker’s private motive or determine the argument’s truth.

Comparative inference: this is better analyzed as an argument from alleged improbability under human invention than simply called teleological. Posterior odds depend on prior odds multiplied by P(features|divine origin)/P(features|human origin). A low denominator alone does not establish a high posterior, and human development is not limited to deliberate fraud.

The argument needs defensible comparisons between origin hypotheses and a less narrow account of motives. Demanding teachings do not by themselves authenticate revelation; neither does identifying ordinary motives alone settle every historical origin claim.
