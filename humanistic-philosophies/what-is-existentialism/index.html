Prompt 1: What is Existentialism?

Existence, choice, and different approaches to meaning

ChatGPT response · editorial edition

Existentialism is a family of approaches to lived existence, freedom, choice, and responsibility. Its nineteenth-century precursors include Kierkegaard and Nietzsche; it became a prominent twentieth-century movement through thinkers such as Sartre and Beauvoir. Atheistic versions question a divinely assigned human purpose, while Christian versions understand personal commitment in relation to God. The tradition therefore cannot be defined simply as the belief that life has no meaning.

Recurring themes include anxiety about possibilities and commitments; the absurd, especially in Camus’s account of the conflict between our demand for intelligibility and the world’s silence; and authenticity, or taking responsibility for how one lives. These themes overlap without forming a doctrine accepted identically by every thinker.

Kierkegaard and Nietzsche are precursors rather than members of an organized existentialist movement. Sartre and Beauvoir explicitly developed existentialist philosophies. Camus is often taught alongside them, although he rejected the label. Their disagreements about faith, ethics, and meaning are part of the subject, not exceptions to be removed from it.

Existentialism has influenced various fields outside philosophy, including literature, theology, psychology, and the arts, underscoring its impact on 20th-century thought and beyond.

Gemini response · editorial edition

Existentialism is a philosophical school of thought that emphasizes the individual’s experience of existence. Here are some central ideas of existentialism:

  • Individual existence: General descriptions of human beings do not settle how this particular person should live. For Sartre, a person’s identity develops through projects and actions rather than a predetermined human purpose; Kierkegaard instead explores becoming a self before God.
  • Freedom and responsibility: We must respond to possibilities within circumstances we did not choose. Freedom here is not unlimited power: poverty, coercion, history, and the actions of others can severely restrict what is possible. Responsibility requires attending to those circumstances as well as to choices.
  • Authenticity: Living authentically involves acknowledging one’s situation and commitments instead of hiding behind a role or pretending that every decision has already been made. Unconventional behavior alone is no guarantee of authenticity.
  • Absurdity: Camus explores the unresolved encounter between our desire for ultimate explanation and a world that does not supply it. He asks how to live lucidly with that tension; ordinary relationships and projects need not become worthless.

Important figures include Kierkegaard, Nietzsche, Heidegger, Sartre, Beauvoir, and Camus, with differences in both their positions and their willingness to accept the existentialist label.

Existentialism can be a complex philosophy, but it can also be a helpful way to think about the challenges and opportunities of human existence.

Prompt 2: Expound on a few of the key notions within Existentialism.

Anxiety, absurdity, authenticity, and situated freedom

ChatGPT response · editorial edition

Five connected notions help explain why existentialist writing moves between abstract argument and descriptions of ordinary life:

  1. Existential anxiety: Anxiety can arise when familiar routines no longer settle what to do and one confronts the possibility of choosing differently. Kierkegaard connects it with freedom, possibility, and sin within a Christian framework. Heidegger distinguishes anxiety, which unsettles our ordinary engagement with the world, from fear of a particular threat. Neither account amounts simply to discovering that morality is unreal. Leaving a secure career can illustrate anxiety about possibilities, without exhausting either philosopher’s analysis.
  2. The absurd: For Camus, absurdity arises in the confrontation between a human demand for meaning or intelligibility and the world’s failure to answer it on our terms. It is not just a property of the universe considered alone. His response is to sustain lucid engagement without pretending the tension has disappeared through a final explanation. Creating projects can make life worth living without supplying the cosmic guarantee one originally sought.
  3. Authenticity: Authenticity involves owning one’s possibilities and commitments rather than treating social expectations as an automatic excuse. In Sartre’s account, it is not the discovery of a finished inner essence: actions help constitute the person one becomes. In Heidegger, it concerns taking over one’s finite possibilities within an already shared world. A conventional occupation can be thoughtfully chosen, while a rebellious pose can be another way of conforming. Authenticity alone also does not settle whether an action is just.
  4. Freedom and responsibility: Sartre treats human beings as responsible for the projects through which they give their lives direction, without a divine blueprint settling those projects in advance. This is not a claim that we choose our birthplace, can do anything we wish, or deserve every misfortune. Beauvoir makes the ethical importance of others’ freedom and material conditions especially clear: a genuine opportunity to act differs from freedom asserted only in words. Religious existentialists give responsibility a different grounding, so rejection of universal moral truth is not a shared premise.
  5. Nothingness and mortality: Sartre’s nothingness concerns the ways consciousness can negate, question, and take distance from what is given. Heidegger’s being-toward-death concerns owning the finitude of one’s possibilities rather than treating death solely as something that happens to other people. These are distinct analyses, neither reducible to the slogan that life is ultimately meaningless. Awareness that time is limited can change which commitments deserve attention now.

These notions connect because choices take place in a world already underway, with other people and a limited future. Their philosophical value lies in examining those connections while keeping the thinkers’ disagreements visible.

Gemini response · editorial edition

Here’s a deeper dive into two core existentialist notions:

  1. Anxiety and Freedom: Imagine yourself standing on a precipice. The wind whips around you, and the ground far below seems to disappear. This feeling of vertigo is a metaphor for existential anxiety in some existentialist thought, particularly that of Søren Kierkegaard.

Kierkegaard connects this dizziness with awareness of possibility: one can act, and the very openness of action can unsettle one. His account concerns sin and the formation of a self before God, not simply inventing personal values in a godless universe. The precipice image is useful because the disturbing possibility belongs partly to the person looking, not only to the external danger.

This philosophical account does not make distress automatically beneficial. It suggests that confronting possibilities can change one’s understanding of responsibility, rather than merely removing an unpleasant feeling.

  1. Absurdity and meaning: Imagine looking for a final instruction manual that would justify every important decision, then finding that the world supplies no such answer. The analogy captures part of Camus’s concern: the absurd belongs to the collision between our demand and the world’s silence. It does not prove that every kind of meaning is absent.

Camus’s proposed revolt keeps this tension in view instead of resolving it by wishful certainty. Work, friendship, art, and resistance to injustice can matter without becoming a complete cosmic explanation. His later account of rebellion also turns toward shared human limits and solidarity, which complicates the picture of an isolated individual inventing values at will.

The two notions therefore pose different challenges: Kierkegaard asks how possibility, anxiety, and faith enter the task of becoming a self; Camus asks how to live without escaping the absurd through an unjustified final answer. Their contrast is more illuminating than treating authenticity as a single formula that both accept.

Prompt 3: Give a brief introduction to the most influential Existentialist philosophers.

Thinkers and their disagreements

ChatGPT response · editorial edition

Certainly, here’s a brief introduction to some of the most influential existentialist philosophers:

  1. Søren Kierkegaard (1813-1855):
  • A major precursor to existentialism, Kierkegaard examined subjectivity, anxiety, despair, and becoming a self within an explicitly Christian project. His pseudonymous works present perspectives that should not all be identified directly with his own voice. The familiar phrase leap of faith summarizes themes of commitment beyond objective guarantees, but it can obscure the specifically religious difficulties his works explore.
  1. Friedrich Nietzsche (1844-1900):
  • Nietzsche’s critiques of inherited morality, religion, and assumptions about truth helped shape later existentialist debates. His death-of-God diagnosis concerns the erosion of a framework that had organized European values; it is not a report of a literal divine death. His accounts of life affirmation, self-overcoming, and the Übermensch challenge readers to reconsider values rather than treating every private preference as equally justified.
  1. Jean-Paul Sartre (1905-1980):
  • Sartre is perhaps the most prominent figure associated with existentialism in the 20th century. In his major work “Being and Nothingness,” he explores themes such as radical freedom, responsibility, and the nature of consciousness. Sartre famously proclaimed that “existence precedes essence,” emphasizing the idea that individuals create their own essence through their actions and choices. His concept of “bad faith” describes the self-deceptive ways individuals avoid acknowledging their freedom and responsibility.
  1. Simone de Beauvoir (1908-1986):
  • Beauvoir developed an existentialist ethics of situated freedom and a major analysis of women’s oppression in The Second Sex. Her account of woman as Other examines how social institutions and everyday relationships make the male standpoint appear universal. Her philosophical contribution extends beyond applying Sartre: freedom must be considered with embodiment, dependence, and the concrete possibilities available to others.
  1. Albert Camus (1913-1960):
  • Camus explored the absurd in The Myth of Sisyphus and fiction such as The Stranger, while The Plague and The Rebel develop problems of solidarity and resistance. He rejected the existentialist label but remains central to discussions of related themes. His question is how to live lucidly without a final guarantee of meaning, not how to prove that nothing in life can matter.

These philosophers, among others, have shaped existentialist thought and continue to influence diverse fields, including philosophy, literature, psychology, and social theory.

Gemini response · editorial edition

Here’s a quick introduction to some of the most influential existentialist philosophers:

  • Søren Kierkegaard (1813–1855): A Christian precursor who investigates anxiety, despair, commitment, and becoming a self before God. His treatments of faith cannot be reduced to choosing whichever personal meaning feels attractive.
  • Friedrich Nietzsche (1844–1900): A precursor whose criticism of inherited values and accounts of self-overcoming influenced later existentialists. The Übermensch, often translated as overman, is an ideal associated with overcoming existing forms of life and valuation, not simply any person who follows personal desires.
  • Martin Heidegger (1889-1976): Heidegger focused on human existence (Dasein) and our experience of being-in-the-world. He explored themes of anxiety, authenticity, and our relationship with Being itself.
  • Jean-Paul Sartre (1905-1980): A prominent 20th-century figure, Sartre argued that “existence precedes essence,” meaning we exist first and define ourselves through our choices. He explored freedom and responsibility in his famous work “Being and Nothingness.”
  • Albert Camus (1913–1960): Often discussed with existentialists despite rejecting the label, Camus examines the absurd as a relation between our demands and the world. He argues for lucid living and revolt, later emphasizing solidarity and limits on rebellion rather than arbitrary meaning creation.
  • Simone de Beauvoir (1908–1986): An existentialist philosopher whose work on ethics and The Second Sex analyzes situated freedom and oppression. Her account of woman as Other concerns social organization, embodiment, and recognition, not merely how women are visually represented.

This is just a brief overview, and there are many other important existentialist thinkers. Each philosopher has their own unique take on core existential themes, making it a rich and diverse school of thought.

Prompt 4: Provide 12 notable quotes from Existentialist philosophers.

Twelve sourced quotations in each response

ChatGPT response · editorial edition

Twelve short quotations follow in the original eight-author order. Wording follows the linked translation unless marked otherwise; two additional source entries now serve as context notes. These writers do not all accept the existentialist label, and the excerpts express different positions.

  1. Søren Kierkegaard:
  • life must be understood backwards — Kierkegaard, journal JJ:167 (1843), excerpt. The same entry insists on living forward; the familiar one-line maxim compresses a longer reflection.
  1. Friedrich Nietzsche:
  • the manner of it can take care of itself — Nietzsche, Twilight of the Idols, Maxims and Missiles §12, excerpt. The preceding condition concerns knowing the reason for one’s existence; this is an aphorism about purpose, not a guarantee that suffering becomes harmless.
  • God is dead! God remains dead! And we have killed him! — Nietzsche, The Gay Science (The Joyful Wisdom), §125.
  1. Jean-Paul Sartre:
  • man is condemned to be free — Sartre, Existentialism Is a Humanism, excerpt. He means that refusing to choose does not remove responsibility, not that circumstances place no limits on action.
  • existence precedes essence — Sartre, Existentialism Is a Humanism, excerpt.
  1. Simone de Beauvoir:
  • One is not born, but rather becomes, woman — Beauvoir, The Second Sex, opening of volume II. This is the wording reproduced by the linked publisher; English translations differ over the article before woman.
  • To will oneself free is also to will others free. — Beauvoir, The Ethics of Ambiguity, part II. Her account connects personal freedom with the concrete freedom of others.
  1. Albert Camus:
  • One must imagine Sisyphus happy. — Camus, The Myth of Sisyphus, closing sentence. This replaces an unverified attribution about becoming absolutely free in an unfree world.
  • In the midst of winter, I finally learned that there was within me an invincible summer. — Camus, Return to Tipasa, translation here from the linked French. The image concerns recovered joy alongside a continuing commitment to justice.
  1. Martin Heidegger:
  • Context note: Heidegger’s analysis of being-in-the-world is not a claim that an isolated mind first exists and later encounters external objects. The source’s unsourced sentence about openness is treated here as a topic for explanation, not as an authenticated quotation.
  • reason, glorified for centuries, is the most stiffnecked adversary of thought — Heidegger, The Word of Nietzsche: God Is Dead, closing sentence, excerpt. The provocation challenges a particular understanding of thought; it is not a general argument for believing contradictions.
  1. Franz Kafka (often associated with existentialist themes):
  • In the struggle between yourself and the world, side with the world. — Kafka, Zürau aphorism 52, wording reproduced in Jonathan Gibbs’s translation comparison. Kafka is a literary writer associated with existential themes; the instruction is deliberately puzzling rather than a summary of the movement.
  • Context note: Kafka’s aphorisms resist conversion into a consistent set of life instructions. The room-and-waiting passage occupied a second quotation slot in the source; this note keeps its place while bringing the selection to the requested twelve quotations.
  1. Gabriel Marcel:
  • I am my body — Marcel, The Mystery of Being, volume I, lecture V, excerpt. He is describing lived embodiment, while resisting both the body-as-tool picture and a simple reduction of the person to an external object.

Read each excerpt with its source. Anxiety, self-overcoming, embodied life, and solidarity are connected concerns, but these quotations are starting points for comparison rather than evidence of a single shared doctrine.

Existentialism Quiz

Test your knowledge of existentialist thought!

  1. In Sartre’s atheistic existentialism, who must take responsibility for the projects through which a person gives life direction?
  2. Which writer associated with existentialist debates is especially known for his account of the absurd?
  3. The idea that “existence precedes essence” means:
    a) We are born with a predetermined purpose.
    b) We define ourselves through our choices and actions.
    c) There is no inherent meaning in the universe.
  4. Existentialism is characterized by a focus on:
    a) Finding comfort in tradition.
    b) Individual freedom and responsibility.
    c) Obeying a set of universal moral codes.
  5. What is a common theme in existentialist writings?
    a) Living a carefree and impulsive life.
    b) Anxiety arising from freedom and meaning-making.
    c) Accepting fate and predetermined destiny.

Answers:

  1. The individual, acting within a situation that also involves other people and constraints.
  2. Albert Camus.
  3. (b) We define ourselves through our choices and actions.
  4. (b) Individual freedom and responsibility.
  5. (b) Anxiety arising from freedom and meaning-making.

Prompt 6: Provide 12 discussion questions relevant to the content above.

Two sets of twelve discussion questions

ChatGPT response · editorial edition

Certainly, here are 12 discussion questions relevant to the content on existentialism:

  1. How do existentialist philosophers define the concept of freedom, and what role does it play in shaping human existence?
  2. Discuss the significance of the idea that “existence precedes essence” in Jean-Paul Sartre’s philosophy. How does this concept relate to individual freedom and responsibility?
  3. In what ways does existential angst or dread manifest in everyday life, and how do existentialist thinkers propose to confront or navigate this feeling?
  4. Explore the concept of authenticity in existentialism. What does it mean to live authentically, and why is it considered crucial in existentialist thought?
  5. How do Kierkegaard, Sartre, and Camus differ on meaning in life? Distinguish religious commitment, chosen projects, and living with the absurd.
  6. How do existentialist accounts connect choice with ethical responsibility? Compare Beauvoir’s concern for others’ freedom with the idea that any personally chosen value is acceptable.
  7. Discuss the influence of existentialist ideas on fields outside philosophy, such as literature, psychology, and theology. Provide examples of how existentialist themes are expressed in these disciplines.
  8. Compare and contrast the existentialist views of Søren Kierkegaard and Friedrich Nietzsche on the nature of existence, freedom, and the search for meaning.
  9. How do existentialist philosophers address the tension between individual freedom and social conformity? Can one be authentically oneself while still adhering to societal norms?
  10. Explore the existentialist perspective on the nature of human relationships and interpersonal connections. How do existentialist thinkers view the role of others in shaping individual identity and existence?
  11. Discuss the feminist existentialism of Simone de Beauvoir. How does she critique traditional gender roles and advocate for women’s freedom and autonomy?
  12. Reflect on the relevance of existentialist ideas in contemporary society. Do you think existentialism still offers valuable insights into the human condition in the 21st century? Why or why not?

Gemini response · editorial edition

  1. Do you believe humans have inherent meaning in life, or do we create our own? Why?
  2. Imagine yourself completely free of societal expectations and pressures. What choices would you make to define your life? Would this freedom be exhilarating or terrifying?
  3. Existentialists grapple with anxiety as a response to freedom. Have you ever experienced this kind of anxiety? If so, how did you deal with it?
  4. Camus locates the absurd in the clash between our demand for meaning and the world’s silence. How does that differ from saying that every human activity is pointless?
  5. How can we create a meaningful life in a world that may not have predetermined meaning?
  6. Existentialism emphasizes individual responsibility. Is this an important concept in today’s society? Why or why not?
  7. How does Kierkegaard’s account of religious commitment differ from an ordinary decision under uncertainty? Where might the familiar leap-of-faith analogy illuminate or obscure that distinction?
  8. Jean-Paul Sartre famously stated “existence precedes essence.” How does this concept influence how we view ourselves and others?
  9. Camus links rebellion with a shared human claim. How might solidarity and limits on harming others complicate the idea that revolt means doing whatever one chooses?
  10. Simone de Beauvoir explored existentialist ideas through a feminist lens. How can existentialism be applied to social justice issues?
  11. Some people find comfort in tradition and established belief systems. How does existentialism compare to these perspectives?
  12. Existentialist ideas can be seen in literature and film. Can you think of any examples where characters grapple with themes of freedom, responsibility, or the search for meaning?
